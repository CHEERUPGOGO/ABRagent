Although the FEC additive shows a negligible impact on initial lithium deposition, it effectively mitigates dead lithium accumulation by altering its growth kinetics from exponential to linear with the cycle repeated. Comparative studies in ether-based electrolytes reveal that $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ additive significantly affects deposition morphology by promoting dense lithium deposition. However, it fails to suppress dead lithium accumulation as the growth kinetics still follow an exponential trend with the cycle repeated. Lithium metal battery is regarded as a competitive selection for next-generation high-energy-density battery due to its low reduction potential $(-3.04\mathrm{V}$ vs standard hydrogen electrode) and exceptionally high theoretical specific capacity $(3860\mathrm{mAh}$ $\mathbf{g}^{-1})$ . However, capacity decay and safety risks caused by lithium dendrite growth and dead Li accumulation persistently hinder their practical applications. Electrolytes are pivotal components of anode-free lithium metal batteries (AFLMBs), as they determine lithium deposition morphology and the composition of the solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) layer formed on the lithium surface. By precisely tailoring electrolytes to induce uniform and smooth lithium deposition, the growth of high-surface-area "mossy" lithium and undesirable side reactions between the electrolyte and metallic lithium could be suppressed.

Ester- and ether-based electrolytes are the two dominant organic electrolyte categories for AFLMBs.[11,12] Traditional carbonate ester-based electrolytes exhibit high conductivity, strong wettability, and robust oxidation stability ( $>4.2\mathrm{V}$ vs $\mathrm{Li^{+} / Li}$ ), making them ideal candidates for high-voltage AFLMBs.[13,14] However, challenges persist in carbonate ester-based electrolytes, including their high reactivity with lithium metal, chemical instability, and transition metal dissolution at high voltages.[15-19] Compared to carbonate-based electrolytes, ether-based electrolytes demonstrate better compatibility with

lithium metal but face the problem of oxidative decomposition above $4.0\mathrm{V}$ , rendering them incompatible with high-voltage intercalation-type cathodes.[20-22] On the other side, uncontrolled dendritic or mossy lithium deposition on copper foils and continuous active lithium consumption during repeated stripping/deposition cycles severely hinder the practical application of AFLMBs in both ester-based and ether-based electrolytes.[23,24] The consumption of lithium primarily stems from two sources: chemical instability between lithium metal and the electrolyte, leading to lithium incorporation as lithium ions $(\mathrm{Li}^{+})$ in the SEI; physical/mechanical instability during lithium stripping, which detaches dendritic lithium from the bulk metal or current collector, forming electrochemically inactive lithium $(\mathrm{Li}_{0})$ .[25]

In AFLMBs, reducing the active lithium metal consumption is crucial for enhancing battery performance. Introducing functional electrolyte additives which could significantly

improve SEI composition and lithium deposition morphology even in small quantities, is the most economical and effective way to mitigate active lithium loss.[26,27] Electrolyte additives are broadly categorized into inorganic (e.g., $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ ) and organic (e.g., fluoroethylene carbonate (FEC)) types, widely applied in ether- and ester-based electrolytes, respectively.[28,29] These electrolyte additives could regulate the chemical composition of SEI, promote lithium-ion migration, and control lithium plating/stripping behavior.[30] However, due to the lack of real-time detection methods, the precise impact of these additives on lithium deposition/stripping processes and dead lithium evolution still remains unclear, as the relative proportion of SEI to dead Li dynamically evolves during cycling and is heavily

influenced by operating conditions. Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) technology, as a nondestructive and quantitative detection method, is highly effective for in situ monitoring of the lithium deposition process, including the study of its reaction kinetics and the formation of microstructural lithium. $^{31-33}$ Due to the characteristic Knight shift of lithium NMR, it is easy to distinguish between "mossy" and "dendritic" lithium metals; their Knight shifts are located at 260 and $270~\mathrm{ppm}$ or even higher, while the bulk lithium metal is situated between 240 and $250~\mathrm{ppm}$ .

In this work, the effects of FEC additive in ester-based electrolytes along with $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ additive in ether-based electrolytes on lithium deposition and dead Li formation in Cull

$\mathrm{LiFePO_4}$ batteries are investigated by a self-developed in situ NMR in combinations with post-mortem scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Results show that while FEC has no significant effect on initial lithium deposition, the LiF-rich SEI effectively slows the growth of dead Li by transforming its growth mode from exponential to linear. In contrast, $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ additive notably improves lithium deposition morphology but fails to suppress dead Li formation during early cycles, where growth of dead Li still follows exponential trend. However, dead Li saturation is observed with the cycle repeated. These findings provide new insights into the mechanisms of different additives on lithium deposition and dead Li evolution in AFLMBs.

The basic ester electrolyte (EC/DEC) consisted of $1\mathrm{M}$ $\mathrm{LiPF_6}$ dissolved in ethylene carbonate (EC) and diethyl carbonate (DEC) $(1:1\mathrm{v / v})$ . Advanced ester electrolyte (EC/DEC+FEC) incorporated 5 wt % FEC additive. For ether-based systems, the basic electrolyte (DOL/DME) contained 1 M lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) in 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) and 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) $(1:1\mathrm{v / v})$ while the modified version $(\mathrm{DOL} / \mathrm{DME} + \mathrm{LiNO}_3)$ added 2 wt $\%$ $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ . All electrolytes were purchased from Duoduo Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. The lithium iron phosphate $(\mathrm{LiFePO_4})$ cathode (areal capacity: $8.67~\mathrm{mg~cm^{-2}}$ ) was sourced from Guangdong Kelude New Energy Technology Co., Ltd. Cathodes, copper foils $(25\mu \mathrm{m}$ thickness), were cut into specified dimensions of $5\mathrm{mm}\times 10\mathrm{mm}$ , while PP separators (Cegard 3500, $25\mu \mathrm{m}$ ) and glass fiber separators (Whatman GF/D, $6.75~\mathrm{mm}$ ) were cut into specified dimensions of $6\mathrm{mm}\times 12\mathrm{mm}$ . Then all the materials were dried under vacuum at $100^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ for $12\mathrm{h}$ and transferred to an argon-filled glovebox for storage.

The in situ NMR test cells were assembled in a cylindrical polyetheretherketone (PEEK) mold as shown in Figure S1. Each cell was filled with $50\mu \mathrm{L}$ of electrolyte. To ensure airtight sealing and prevent air exposure during testing, ultraviolet (UV)-curable adhesive was applied to the mold seams within an argon-filled glovebox. All electrochemical tests were conducted at $25^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ under a voltage window of $2.8 - 3.8\mathrm{V}$ , using a constant current density of $1\mathrm{mAcm}^{-2}$ as detected by LAND CT-20001A tester.

In situ $^{7}\mathrm{Li}$ NMR NMR spectra were acquired on a Bruker Ascend 400WB spectrometer (9.4 T) equipped with a custom-built probe containing a $12\mathrm{mm}$ solenoid coil as shown in Figure S2. The sample chamber of probe incorporated a $12\mathrm{mm}$ solenoid coil, and all experiments were performed with the NMR circuit tuned to $155.6\mathrm{MHz}$ . $^{7}\mathrm{Li}$ NMR spectra were acquired via a single-pulse excitation of $5.0~\mu \mathrm{s}$ , corresponding to a flip angle of $30^{\circ}$ and a relaxation delay of $0.25\mathrm{s}$ . Each spectrum requires 512 scans to achieve sufficient signal-to-noise ratio, resulting in a total acquisition time of $152\mathrm{s}$ . The calibration of Knight shift of $^{7}\mathrm{Li}$ was referenced to a $1\mathrm{M}$ LiCl aqueous solution $(0\mathrm{ppm})$ . Probe retuning was performed during $5\mathrm{min}$ equilibrium intervals after each charge/discharge step to compensate for circuit drift.

The ratio of dead Li and SEI is calculated from the data acquired by NMR and electrochemistry measurements. According to previous study,[37] the detailed calculation method is shown below. First, the total irreversible capacity $C_{\text{irreversible}}$ is obtained from the subtraction between charging capacity $C_{\text{charge}}$ and discharging capacity $C_{\text{discharge}}$ which is

$$

C _ {\text {i e}} = C _ {\text {c e}} - C _ {\text {d e}} \tag {1}

$$

Here, $C_{\text{irreversible}}$ is assumed to reflect the total amount of dead Li and SEI. The capacity loss due to the formation of SEI $C_{\text{SEI}}$ and the capacity related to the formation of dead Li $C_{\text{dead Li}}$ can then be calculated as below:

$$

C _ {\mathrm {S E I}} = C _ {\text {i e}} - C _ {\text {d i}} \tag {2}

$$

The $C_{\mathrm{irreversible}}$ , $C_{\mathrm{charge}}$ and $C_{\mathrm{discharge}}$ can be obtained according to the calculation method reported before.37 Thus, the capacity of SEI and dead Li can be calculated through the above equations. The proportion of SEI $\phi_{\mathrm{SEI}}$ and dead Li $\phi_{\mathrm{dead~Li}}$ can be calculated as below:

$$

\phi_ {\mathrm {S E I}} = \frac {C _ {\mathrm {S E I}}}{C _ {\text {c e}}} \tag {3}

$$

$$

\phi_ {\text {d i}} = \frac {C _ {\text {d i}}}{C _ {\text {c e}}} \tag {4}

$$

The relative proportion of dead Li over SEI can be acquired from the following equation:

$$

\frac {\phi_ {\text {d i}}}{\phi_ {\text {S E I}}} = \frac {C _ {\text {d i}}}{C _ {\text {S E I}}} \tag {5}

$$

The above analysis indicates that both FEC and $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ additives could effectively reduce the dead Li content, but their mechanisms are quite different. The initial nucleation behavior of lithium acts as a "template" for subsequent deposition and also governs the chemical composition and spatial distribution of the nascent solid electrolyte interphase. Studies demonstrate that advanced electrolyte formulations facilitate the formation of an SEI with highly consistent chemistry on both lithium metal and copper current collector surfaces.[53] Such compositional homogeneity ensures uniform interfacial lithium-ion transport during plating and stripping, which promotes spatially homogeneous lithium dissolution and helps avoid localized isolation, often referred to as "pinch-off" that leads to dead lithium formation.[54] Conversely, nonuniform SEI formation and heterogeneous nucleation result in loose, porous, or dendritic lithium morphologies from the initial cycle. This inhomogeneity is further amplified in subsequent cycling: dendritic tips are prone to fracture, generating dead lithium, while the high-surface-area porous structures continuously consume electrolyte and foster a thicker, less stable SEI, collectively accelerating active lithium loss. As shown in Figure 1a,b, FEC additives show minor influences on lithium metal deposition/stripping behavior in the first cycle, different from prior studies which suggests that FEC promote denser lithium deposition.[44] In addition, the formation of SEI consumes $16.1\%$ of capacity in the first cycle with FEC compared with that consumes $4.4\%$ of capacity without FEC as shown in Figure 4b, which suggests the introduction of FEC additive promotes the growth of SEI during the first cycle along with modifying SEI composition. With the cycle repeated to the third cycle, the deposition lithium peak shifts rightward to $255.86~\mathrm{ppm}$ , approaching bulk lithium NMR signals, suggesting denser deposition as shown in Figure 2b. Despite the minor influence on the deposition and stripping during first cycle, FEC additive promotes a denser lithium deposition during the second and third cycle by inducing the formation of LiF-rich SEI,[45,46] since the LiF-rich SEI has a higher mechanical strength which could mitigate volume fluctuations and suppress dendritic growth.[47,48] In contrast, the SEI formed without FEC additive weakens rapidly, leading to leftward peak shifts as shown in Figure 2a and dendritic lithium proliferation as shown in Figure 3b,c. After the fourth cycle, the growth modes of SEI aligns regardless of the usage of FEC additive as shown in Figure 4b, reflecting FEC depletion after the first five cycles. The variations in lithium metal deposition behavior also lead to different formation of dead Li during cycling. While initial deposition/stripping remains unaffected, the dead Li peak becomes smaller in the first cycle with FEC as shown in Figure 2j, which suggests FEC reduces dead lithium accumulation. Previous study has shown the effect of FEC additive on suppressing dead Li growth in the first five cycle.[41] In this study, the knowledge of FEC additive is renewed as the investigated cycle number is extended to 25. With the cycle

repeated, the LiF-rich SEI continues suppressing dead Li growth in the first three cycles but fails to prevent gradual accumulation of dead Li in later cycles due to FEC depletion and SEI cracking as shown in Figure 4a. $^{49,50}$ Nonetheless, the growth mode of dead Li transforms from exponential to linear with the formation of LiF-rich SEI, which suggests the enhanced stability of LiF-rich SEI. Unlike FEC, the $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ additive alters lithium metal deposition/stripping behavior of $\mathrm{CuLiFePO_4}$ cells employing an ether-based electrolyte during the first cycle as shown in Figure 1c,d, where the deposition lithium peak closely resembles that of bulk lithium, indicating highly compact deposition. The $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ additive induces spherical lithium deposition, enabling faster coverage of the copper foil surface and a pronounced rightward Knight shift. $^{51-53}$ Thus, the $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ additive significantly promotes dense lithium deposition with the b-Li content increased to $63\%$ , while the b-Li content is $32\%$ and m-Li dominates without $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ in the first cycle as shown in Figure 1h-j. However, the $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ additive does not reduce the dead Li content in the first cycle, as reflected by Figure 2k,l. In subsequent five cycles, the $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ additive maintains reversible lithium deposition but triggers localized microlithium growth which is evidenced by the emergence of left-shoulder peaks shown in Figure 2d. Moreover, the growth modes of dead Li show no significant difference regardless of the usage of $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ additive in the first five cycles, but the growth mode with $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ additive tends to stabilize afterward as shown in Figure 4d,e. This suggests that the compact regions in $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ -modified systems enable reversible cycling while noncompact regions will drive the growth of LMs and consequently lead to dead Li formation and SEI thickening. These distinct mechanisms of FEC and $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ additives are schematically shown in Figure 5.

In summary, the effects of FEC and $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ additives on lithium metal deposition and inactive lithium evolution of Cull $\mathrm{LiFePO_4}$ cells employing ether-based and ester-based electro

lyte are studied through in situ NMR. The FEC additive shows no impact on the first-cycle lithium deposition/stripping behavior. However, in the second and third cycles, the LiF-rich SEI layer effectively induces dense lithium deposition. After the third cycle, FEC fails to suppress dendrite growth. The $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ additive significantly improves lithium deposition morphology during the first charging cycle and partially enables reversible lithium deposition in subsequent cycles, though localized dendrite growth persists. Both FEC and $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ reduce the dead Li content throughout the lifespan of $\mathrm{CuIIFePO_4}$ batteries. FEC shifts dead Li growth from exponential to linear, achieving a final dead lithium content of $29.5\%$ after 25 cycles. $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ delays dead lithium mitigation until midcycle, stabilizing at $31.3\%$ after 35 cycles. With FEC, the failure mode of batteries shifts from dead Li-dominated to SEI-dominated. The failure mode of $\mathrm{CuIIFePO_4}$ cells employing ester-based electrolyte consistently maintains SEI-dominated regardless of usage of the $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ additive. Our findings provide new insights into the role of FEC and $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ additives on lithium metal deposition and inactive lithium formation, which assists to improve the cycling performance of AFLMBs.
