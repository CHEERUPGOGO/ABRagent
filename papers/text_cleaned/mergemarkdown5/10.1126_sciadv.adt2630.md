# RESULTS To fulfill the requirement for highly reversible Li storage, the interlayer ought to combine the mechanical stability, mixed ion-electron conductivity, and sufficient closed volume. As an anode material, LTO has the zero-strain nature during Li-ion intercalation (36), inherent lithiophilicity, and high ionic conductivity, which is promising to serve as a fundamental material to eliminate the problems of interfacial ion diffusion and volume variation during Li deposition. Considering its limitation of intrinsically slow electron transport, the subsequent spatial design urgently needs to introduce electronic conductive component. Therefore, we engineered a hollow hybrid architecture incorporating conductive carbon through a polystyrene (PS) template-mediated synthesis method, aiming to integrate zero-strain characteristic with balanced mixed conductivity and nanocavity confinement (Fig. 2A and figs. S1 and S2). In detail, controlled hydrolysis of tetrabutyl orthotitanate (TBOT) and lithium hydroxide (LiOH) adsorption in aqueous ethanol (EtOH) media first yielded a core-shell LiOH-TBOT@PS precursor, as validated by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and energy-dispersive x-ray spectroscopy (EDX) analyses (fig. S3). The subsequent low-temperature calcination induced dual transformations, including in situ solid-phase reactions between TBOT derivatives and LiOH, coupled with PS template decomposition, ultimately creating a hollow architecture composed of carbon and LTO (named HCLTO). Structural characterization revealed that the HCLTO nanocavity exhibited a diameter of $\sim495\mathrm{nm}$ [scanning electron microscopy (SEM); Fig. 2, B and C] with smooth and dense surfaces ( $\sim20\mathrm{nm}$ in shell thickness; TEM; Fig. 2D and fig. S4), which are essential for effectively preventing electrolyte penetration while maintaining a large internal void space for Li storage. In addition, the mild and argon-protected thermal treatment was expected to achieve two synergistic objectives of gradient conductive structure, including a carbon-rich inner layer caused by PS carbonization to modulate electron transport and a low-crystalline LTO to heighten Li-ion mobility.

We then investigated the crystallographic and chemical structures of the HCLTO. High-resolution TEM imaging denoted the coexisting crystalline and amorphous domains, with the distinct lattice fringes of 2.90 and $2.15\AA$ intersecting at $45^{\circ}$ , coinciding well with the (220) and (400) planes of the face-centered cubic $\mathrm{Li_4Ti_5O_{12}}$ structure, respectively (Fig. 2E). Electron energy-loss spectroscopy (EELS) and the corresponding mapping revealed the detailed orbital information associated with Li, C, Ti, and O elements, in which the homogeneous distribution of all elements confirmed the mixed ionic-electronic conductivity and good connection of the HIEC architecture (Fig. 2, F and G). In detail, the coexistence of $\pi^{*}$ and $\sigma^{*}$ orbits in the K-edge of C EELS (37), as well as the D and G bands in the Raman spectra (Fig. 2I), synergistically proved the coexistence of $\mathfrak{sp}^2$ and $\mathfrak{sp}^3$ carbon domains. The broadened D peak suggested that a quantity of amorphous carbon could be present in the hybrid domains ( $\sim 7.5\%$ by mass as shown in fig. S5). On the basis of previous studies, we consider that the structural defects and vacant bonds in the amorphous carbon can enhance Li atom adsorption, thereby lowering the nucleation barrier (38, 39). Moreover, the splitting of $\mathrm{L}_{2,3}$ from the L-edge of Ti demonstrated that a crystal field lifts the simplicity of the Ti 3d orbit (40). Notably, the elevated energy levels in the K-edge of Li and the lower energy gap $(2.10\mathrm{eV})$ between $\mathbf{e_g}$ and $\mathrm{t}_{2\mathrm{g}}$ in the L-edge of Ti compared to the ideal structure indicated the oxygen vacancy formation (table S1) (41). This finding aligned with the spectral analysis including a $g$ factor of 2.002 in electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR; Fig. 2J and fig. S6) and O 1s deconvolution data in x-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS; fig. S7 and table S3). These oxygen vacancies can regulate local electronic environments of the HIEC architecture thereby creating electron-deficient regions, which enhance Li-salt anion adsorption and improve the interfacial charge transfer kinetics (42, 43). Moreover, on the basis of specific surface area and pore analysis (fig. S8), HCLTO potentially confines the electrolyte to diffuse into internal cavities, thereby limiting electrolyte permeation to a low level. From the analysis above, HCLTO nanocavity is adequate as an HIEC to construct stable interlayers. To apply the HIEC and validate the scalability in AFLMBs, we fabricated a large-area uniform HCLTO interlayer (4 cm by 8 cm) on copper (Cu) foil via standard slurry processing (Fig. 2K). Focused ion beam (FIB) tomography imaging as a nondestructive cross-sectional analysis verified the densely packed nanocavities with intact hollow architecture, which is critical for Li accommodation (fig. S9). The subsequent mechanical characterization (fig. S10) revealed that the Derjaguin-Müller-Toporov (DMT) modulus (5.31 GPa) surpassed the shear modulus of metallic Li (2 to 2.5 GPa) (10, 44). According to the Monroe and Newman's theory, nonuniformity of Li deposition and the development of dendrites can be effectively suppressed when the shear modulus of the material in contact with the Li plating is about twice higher than metallic Li (45). Therefore, the above findings collectively indicate that the HCLTO interlayer preliminarily aligns with the design of an ideal architecture, thereby demonstrating promising potential to stabilize Li deposition.

As an anode material, $\mathrm{Li_4Ti_5O_{12}}$ undergoes the sequential phase transitions to $\mathrm{Li_7Ti_5O_{12}}$ even $\mathrm{Li_8Ti_5O_{12}}$ through Li-ion intercalation before Li plating (46). Hence, the initial-cycle CE (ICE) was not satisfactory when directly using the HCLTO interlayer to store Li (fig. S11). To offset the inherent Li loss, we developed a mild and scalable CPL protocol involving biphenyl (BP)-mediated Li reduction in the 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) solvent (fig. S12) (47). This CPL approach has also become a common method used to supplement Li in some anodes (47-49). In the process, the HCLTO interlayer can achieve a nondestructive Li supplement by being immersed in the prelithiation solution (named P-HCLTO), as validated by a negligible variation in thickness and the color change from light yellow to black (Fig. 3A). The negligible discharge specific capacity before Li plating confirmed the success of prelithiation (Fig. 3B). Further optimization via open-circuit potential (OCP) monitoring and electrochemical testing (CE and cycling life) identified 3-hour immersion as the ideal duration, which balanced Li supplementation without the risk of structural degradation caused by excessive SEI formation or material delamination (fig. S13 and table S2). Following the acquisition of optimized conditions, we applied the in situ electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) coupled with distribution of relaxation time (DRT) analysis to study the evolution of CPL/electrochemical prelithiation of the HCLTO interlayer in Li|Cu electrochemical cells. Typical EIS data of HCLTO or P-HCLTO interlayers both included interfacial resistance $(R_{\mathrm{SEI}})$ in the high-frequency region, charge transfer resistance $(R_{\mathrm{ct}})$ in the mid-frequency region, and Warburg-type diffusion resistance in the low-frequency region (fig. S14). DRT spectra decoupled the electrochemical phase transitions of the first two dominant processes, where the peaks located at $10^{-4}$ to $10^{-3.5}\mathrm{s}(\tau_{1})$ and $10^{-2}$ to $10^{-1.5}\mathrm{s}(\tau_{2})$ represent the Li-ion diffusion through the SEI and charge transfer processes at the electrode/electrolyte interface (50), respectively (Fig. 3C). In the electrochemical lithiation process, the impedance evolution can be divided into three stages, where the $\gamma (\tau_{2})$ decreased rapidly at the first stage (OCP to $1.5\mathrm{V}$ ) and stabilized at the second stage (1.25 to $0.5\mathrm{V}$ ) and the third stage (0.5 to $0\mathrm{V}$ ). In contrast, $\gamma (\tau_{1})$ decreased slightly through the whole period and replaced the $\gamma (\tau_{2})$ as the dominant impedance in the last two stages. The results demonstrated that the $R_{\mathrm{ct}}$ dominated the early lithiation process (OCP to $1.5\mathrm{V}$ ), and the reaction kinetics of deep lithiation process (1.5 to $0\mathrm{V}$ ) was dominated by the $R_{\mathrm{SEI}}$ . In addition, the total impedance reached the minimum value after full lithiation (at $0\mathrm{V}$ ). Notably, CPL replicated the impedance reduction process and the impedance was even lower than that after the electrochemical lithiation, suggesting the better lithiation homogeneity enabled by the chemical approach (red line in Fig. 3C). The above EIS and DRT analyses indicated that the HCLTO interlayer after full lithiation was endowed with the fastest kinetics, no matter whether electrochemical or chemical approaches were used. We then further quantified Li diffusivity by the galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT) profiles, which revealed that the Li diffusion coefficient $(D_{\mathrm{Li^{+}}})$ of P-HCLTO quickly increased to the level of $10^{-6}\mathrm{cm}^2\mathrm{s}^{-1}$ and reached $4.46\times 10^{-6}\mathrm{cm}^2\mathrm{s}^{-1}$ at $0.01\mathrm{V}$ , exceeding the $D_{\mathrm{Li^{+}}}(1.37\times 10^{-6}\mathrm{cm}^2\mathrm{s}^{-1})$ of fully electrochemically lithiated HCLTO (Fig. 3D). Considering the conventional Li self-diffusion coefficient of $10^{-11}\mathrm{cm}^2\mathrm{s}^{-1}$ , the faster diffusion provides the basic characteristic of P-HCLTO as the SEI (51). Furthermore, the four-probe measurements determined moderate electronic conductivities $(1.5\times 10^{-3}\mathrm{Scm}^{-1}$ for HCLTO versus $1.4\times 10^{-2}\mathrm{Scm}^{-1}$ for P-HCLTO), attributed to the hybrid $\mathfrak{sp}^2 /\mathfrak{sp}^3$ carbon structure, which are not as high as traditional metals and carbon derivatives, alleviating the concerns of direct Li deposition outside the HIEC (Fig. 3E). The enhanced ionic conductivity and decent electronic conductivity synergistically improve the mixed ionic-electronic conducting characteristics of the P-HCLTO interlayer, facilitating the equilibrium in charge distribution. Moreover, TEM mapping of the HIEC suggested that the fast ionic transport through outer shell and higher electron conductivity located at inner shell (fig. S15), indicating the formation of structure with gradient mixed conductivity. In addition, to investigate how CPL affects the surface energetics thus analyzing the potential interfacial ion transport behavior, we applied the atomic force microscopy (AFM) and Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM) to directly image the surface potential distribution of the HCLTO and P-HCLTO interlayers. The results revealed a substantial reduction in average surface potential from $\sim 810.9$ (HCLTO) to $\sim 416.9\mathrm{mV}$ (P-HCLTO), indicative of the restructured space-charge layer enriched with ordered Li species that lowered the activation barriers for Li-ion migration (Fig. 3, F and G) (52, 53). 2D/3D AFM topographic analysis (fig. S16) coupled with KPFM potential mapping further demonstrated uniform Li distribution across the prelithiated interlayer, which confirmed that the spatially homogeneous insertion reaction was critical for establishing stable and electrochemically compatible interfaces. To conclude, the P-HCLTO interlayer obtained by the CPL protocol delivers encouraging physicochemical characteristics as an HIEC. The compensated interfacial Li-ion depletion without weakening the preexisting hybrid conductivity and Li accommodation features demonstrates promising potential in reducing nucleation overpotential and improving electrochemical kinetics/ reversibility during Li plating/stripping processes.

To verify the role of the HIEC in alleviating the volume expansion of thick Li plating, we first adopted the gap between the doctor blade and Cu CC during the coating process to fabricate the P-HCLTO interlayer with a thickness of 25 to $30\mu \mathrm{m}$ . The thickness can be further adjusted by controlling the gap of doctor blade (fig. S17). Theoretically, metallic Li $(1\mathrm{mAhcm}^{-2})$ can give rise to a deposited thickness of $\sim 5\mu \mathrm{m}$ ; thus, this interlayer is promising for conformal deposition of $\sim 5\mathrm{mAhcm}^{-2}$ . Here, we used a typical ether-based electrolyte [1 M lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) 1,3-dioxolane (DOL)/DME (1:1 by volume) with a $2\mathrm{wt}\%$ lithium nitrate $(\mathrm{LiNO}_3)$ additive] to study the Li deposition behavior in $\mathrm{Li||Cu}$ electrochemical cells (fig. S18). Li deposition on the bare Cu substrate revealed a stress-mediated morphological transition. The initial spheroidal Li clusters, a typical morphology in the presence of the $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ additive, progressively evolved into lamellar aggregates with irregular protrusions. Further cross-sectional SEM analysis demonstrated the thickness of Li plating reaches 11, 22, and $37~{\mu\mathrm{m}}$ at 1, 2.5, and $5\mathrm{mAhcm}^{-2}$ , respectively (Fig. 4A). The reduced growth rate of Li deposition was attributed to the internal stress accumulation caused by high-areal capacity, which, in turn, suppressed the porosity evolution (54). However, the voids adjacent to the substrate remained especially at the stage of Li nucleation and early growth. Moreover, a thick or broken SEI layer and "dead Li" (Li electrically isolated from the substrate) were observed on the surface due to the nonuniform stripping resulting from the variation of internal stress (Fig. 4C). In contrast, under the same current density, there was no clearly detectable Li deposits on the top surface of the P-HCLTO interlayer even if the deposition capacity reached $2.5\mathrm{mAh}$ $\mathrm{cm}^{-2}$ (Fig. 4B). The thickness remained constant, and Li deposits were predominantly observed near the bottom of the Cu CC, accompanied by smooth Li plating on the surface. When the deposition capacity reached $5\mathrm{mAh}\mathrm{cm}^{-2}$ , the entire interlayer was conformally filled with dendrite-free metallic Li, whose thickness only increased to $34~{\mu\mathrm{m}}$ . This increase might arise from the slight exceeding of the Li accommodation threshold as nanocavity architectures themselves also occupied a certain volume. Thus, this transition is triggered by internal space depletion. At this point, the external surface offers shorter transport paths and lower resistance. Li ion preferentially undergoes direct overall external reduction on the HIEC interlayer. After Li stripping, the interlayer returned to the original thickness, with no notable "dead Li" remaining on the surfaces (Fig. 4D). We then conducted in situ optical microscopy (OM) imaging to investigate Li deposition under the stress-free condition (Fig. 4, E and F, and fig. S19). Different Li growth patterns can be observed from the cross-sectional OM images after Li plating for only $10\mathrm{min}$ . The bare Cu substrate exhibited loosely distributed Li deposits with visible voids, attaining an average thickness of $17\mu \mathrm{m}$ . Prolonged deposition resulted in the formation of the mossy structure with high specific surface area, accompanied by many filamentous Li structures. Upon extending the deposition to $60\mathrm{min}$ , severe dendrites and isolated Li clusters emerged, with the Li plating thickness expanding markedly to $642\mu \mathrm{m}$ . In contrast, the use of the P-HCLTO interlayer improved Li deposition behavior, exhibiting nanocavity-confined Li plating and suppressed dendrite growth. Thus, the interlayer maintained the morphology during the initial growth stage, demonstrating that Li can nucleate inside the HIEC without external stress. The subsequent deposition was also dominated by the planar deposition pattern with a slight increase in thickness, which is attributed to the function of the HIEC in homogenizing the Li-ion flow under the electric field. This merit was also supported by the excellent interfacial wettability of the P-HCLTO interlayer (fig. S20), which can optimize the ionic conductive pathway and reduce the interfacial resistance. To further unveil the mechanism of Li deposition within the HIEC, we applied FIB-SEM tomography imaging as a nondestructive approach to characterize the Li plating structures with various deposition capacities. The initial Li deposition $(1\mathrm{mAhcm}^{-2})$ revealed that Li mainly nucleated within the HIEC (Fig. 5, A and B). The pores between nanocavities in the HIEC interlayer are considered to have similar Li-ion concentrations due to electrolyte wetting. Thus, we believed that this phenomenon resulted from the combined effects of electron transfer and lithophilicity. Hollow nanocavities near the Cu CC had absolute locational advantages in both aspects, leading to the lowest effective nucleation energy barrier and the fastest reaction kinetics within them (fig. S21). At $2.5\mathrm{mAhcm}^{-2}$ , the HIEC close to the Cu CC was preferentially full-filled, and Li plating was observed in the gaps of the interconnected HIEC at the bottom (Fig. 5, C and D). Upon exceeding the capacity threshold of the interlayer $(5\mathrm{mAhcm}^{-2})$ , Li conformally filled all residual voids and gaps, achieving near-complete volumetric utilization (Fig. 5, E and F). After 10 cycles, the preferred internal deposition was maintained, further confirming that the inward-increasing electronic conductivity and lithophilic amorphous carbon inner layer continued to guide the deposition site (fig. S22, A to C). Poststripping analysis demonstrated structural integrity of the HIEC across all regions with the absence of "dead Li" in the interlayer, confirming that the encapsulated Li had a higher electrochemical reversibility (Fig. 5, G and H). In addition, the HIEC interlayer retained the structural integrity after repeated cycling (10 and 20 cycles) without notable accumulation of "dead Li" inside the nanocavities, indicating that the Li stripping process was also highly reversible (fig. S22, D to I). Therefore, we propose the mechanism for stabilizing Li deposition through the HIEC interlayer as follows: Li nucleation takes place at the HIEC interior surface driven by a mismatched gradient-conducting behavior and lithophilicity (the HIEC with a fast-ion-transport outer layer and a carbon-enriched lithophilic conductive inner layer; fig. S27); (ii) Li growth is governed by synergistic electron transport kinetics and stress distribution, preferentially filling the HIEC and gaps close to the Cu CC with a shorter intersection zone between the "Li-ion transport path" and "electron transport path" (fig. S21); (iii) uniform external filling is controlled by Li-ion transport because of internal 1 Li C2HO- LiF TiO2- Max Min space limitations. The efficient Li stripping in HIEC nanocavities can be explained by Coble creep (fig. S23) (29). During the stripping process, the inner shell of the HIEC maintains close contact with the nanoconfined electrodeposited Li, preventing random fractures and detachment. In addition to provide sufficient space to accommodate deposited Li, the low electrochemical potential of P-HCLTO as the HIEC also inevitably results in the formation of a new SEI, whose structural stability also critically governs the Li encapsulation effect and thus affects the CE and long-term cycling life (8, 10). To chemically decouple the interfacial architecture, we used the time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (TOF-SIMS) depth profiling to spatially resolve Li distribution and interphase composition. The 3D reconstruction of secondary ion distributions and intensity profiles with sputtering time identified the characteristic SEI fragments, including $\mathrm{C_2HO^-}$ , $\mathrm{LiF_2^-}$ , and $\mathrm{LiO^{-}}$ , mainly from the decomposition of Li-salt and solvents (Fig. 5, I and J). Characteristic $\mathrm{Li}^-$ fragment belonging to multiple components and $\mathrm{TiO_2^-}$ fragment corresponding to LTO exhibited distinct spatial-temporal evolution patterns, whereas the distributions of $\mathrm{Li}^-$ and $\mathrm{TiO_2^-}$ fragments in 3D space were almost complementary (Fig. 5I). Therefore, the interfacial architecture was decoupled into three electrochemically distinct domains (Fig. 5K): SEI and Li outside the HIEC (Area I); (ii) HIEC matrix (Area II); (iii) protected Li encapsulated within the HIEC (Area III). The Area I illustrated a hierarchical SEI feature on the HIEC surface, comprising an outer organic-dominated layer (RCOOLi and ROLi) and an inner inorganic-rich phase ( $\mathrm{LiF / Li_2O}$ ). XPS depth profiling data (fig. S25) also revealed that the proportion of inorganic species originating from anionic decomposition gradually increased with sputtering time. Meanwhile, signals from oxygen-containing functional groups (characteristic signal of ROLi species produced by solvent decomposition) exhibited a continuous decline. The formation of the inner inorganic-rich SEI is driven by the abundant oxygen vacancies in the LTO matrix, where the electron-deficient regions preferentially adsorb and catalytically decompose Li-salt anions (42, 43), as evidenced by the enhanced anion decomposition characteristics of the SEI formed on the HIEC interlayer compared to that on bare Cu (XPS; figs. S24 to S26 and table S4). Meanwhile, the low-content organic fraction in the outer layer is mainly derived from the decomposition of DME and DOL. This hierarchical SEI feature structure was also shown in the TEM images and corresponding EDX mapping results (fig. S27). In addition, the Area II exhibited characteristic $\mathrm{TiO_2^-}$ intensity maxima in the presence of the HIEC, whereas the Area III displayed the decay signal of $\mathrm{TiO_2^-}$ . We thus speculated that metallic Li has been deposited into the HIEC. To validate this hypothesis, we defined a parameter ( $\lambda = \text{Intensity}_{\text{Li}^{-}}/\text{Intensity}_{\text{LiF}_{2}^{-}}$ ) to filter the interference signals of SEI fragments. The enhancement of $\lambda$ in the Area III and the corresponding TEM images (fig. S28, A to D) confirmed that the additional Li source deposited within the nanocavities, which was consistent with the FIB-SEM observations. Notably, the absence of detectable SEI components and negligible EDX signals (fig. S28E) from electrolyte decomposition in the Area III suggested that this closed structure effectively limited electrolyte permeation to minimal levels. Briefly, the HIEC architecture endows the P-HCLTO interlayer with a stable surface/interface to achieve nearly unexpanded encapsulation and protection for active metallic Li.

The above findings indicate the potential merit of P-HCLTO as HIEC interlayers in regulating interfacial kinetics and stabilizing long-term cycling of Li plating/stripping. As expected, the lithiophilicity of the HIEC reduced the nucleation energy barrier of Li deposition, yielding a substantially lower overpotential $(\sim 22.8\mathrm{mV})$ than that of bare Cu $(\sim 55.9\mathrm{mV})$ (Fig. 6A). In addition, the Li||Cu halfcells with the P-HCLTO interlayer exhibited enhanced CE and cycling life at various deposition capacities (Fig. 6B, figs. S29 and S30, and table S5). Especially during the stable cycling period (CE threshold $\geq 97\%$ ), quantitative correlation analysis between CE fluctuation and cycling life further confirmed this trend (Fig. 6C). Among these, the P-HCLTO system achieved an average CE of at least $98.5\%$ under all cycling conditions and demonstrated a longer cycle life, in contrast to the rapid degradation below $90\%$ for the bare Cu counterpart. Notably, the bare Cu system displayed an increase in CE at elevated capacities, which was probably attributed to denser Li deposits resulting from internal stress accumulation, consistent with the SEM analysis (Fig. 4A). However, accelerated battery failure illustrated that mechanical pulverization of metallic Li caused by high volume expansion was detrimental to the cycling life. Conversely, the P-HCLTO interlayer can serve as a bifunctional architecture that integrates the Li host with SEI functions, effectively confined Li deposition within the HIEC architecture while protecting metallic Li from electrolyte corrosion. In particular, the latter capability was critical for improving carrier transport dynamics and interfacial stability, as evidenced by reduced and stable $R_{\mathrm{SEI}}$ in EIS analyses (fig. S31). This protective mechanism became increasingly pronounced under stringent operational scenarios, including the high current density (Fig. 6D and fig. S32) or limited Li configuration $(20 - \mu \mathrm{m}$ Li foil as the counter electrode; fig. S33), demonstrating the role of the HIEC in mitigating parasitic reactions and accelerating the interfacial Li-ion diffusion. We then applied in situ EIS analysis as a nondestructive technique to trace the dynamic evolution of the anode/electrolyte interface at temporal resolution. As the acquired Nyquist plots revealed that the impedances of multiple electrochemical processes strongly coupled together (fig. S34), DRT analysis was applied to decouple the individual contribution of distinct interfacial phenomena (Fig. 6, E to H). The DRT spectra resolved three characteristic relaxation time constants, including $\tau_{1}$ ( $10^{-5}\mathrm{s}$ ), $\tau_{2}$ ( $10^{-4}\mathrm{s}$ ), and $\tau_{3}$ ( $10^{-2}\mathrm{s}$ ), which are assigned to be bulk Li-ion diffusion in the electrolyte, interfacial Li-ion diffusion, and charge transfer, respectively (50, 55). The contour maps of DRT profiles demonstrated SEI-dominated Li plating/stripping dynamics on both bare Cu and P-HCLTO systems. Although a similar evolutionary trend was observed during the plating process (0 to $5\mathrm{mAhcm}^{-2}$ ) as evidenced by stable peak positions and intensities in DRT profiles (Fig. 6, E and G), the lower $\tau_{2}$ and $\gamma (\tau_{2})$ for P-HCLTO implied faster Li deposition kinetics. When charging to $1\mathrm{V}$ (Fig. 6F), the bare Cu system exhibited increased $\gamma (\tau_{2})$ and $\gamma (\tau_{3})$ , which were associated with SEI structural degradation and irreversible metallic Li accumulation, as previously illustrated in the SEM analysis (Fig. 4C). In contrast, these relaxation constants demonstrated attenuated variation when using the P-HCLTO interlayer (Fig. 6H). This enhancement indicates that the HIEC and the derived hierarchical SEI architecture have the good mechanical strength and Li-ion enrichment capacity, thus maintaining the structural integrity and promoting the high-concentration percolation channels formation that enable fast Li-ion transport and reduce concentration gradient during the deep Li stripping. To further elucidate the kinetics and stability of encapsulated Li in the P-HCLTO interlayer, we assembled Li||Li symmetric cells using the bare Cu or P-HCLTO substrates with the equivalent amount of predeposited metallic Li. The exchange current density $(j_0)$ , a critical parameter governing the charge transfer kinetics at the electrode-electrolyte interface, was determined through Tafel plot analysis (Fig. 6I) (56). The P-HCLTO system exhibited a substantially enhanced $j_0$ value of $0.562\mathrm{mAcm}^{-2}$ compared to $0.295\mathrm{mAcm}^{-2}$ for the bare Cu counterpart, demonstrating accelerated interfacial equilibrium establishment in the electrochemical reaction. In addition, $j_0$ directly correlated with the activation energy of the electrochemical processes, which can be quantified through Arrhenius analysis of temperature-dependent EIS data (Fig. 6, J to L, and table S6). The results showed that the P-HCLTO system had lower $R_{\mathrm{SEI}}$ and $R_{\mathrm{ct}}$ than bare Cu, and the activation energies of Li-ion diffusion $(E_{\mathrm{a,SEI}},37.67\mathrm{kJ mol}^{-1})$ and charge transfer $(E_{\mathrm{a,ct}}, 31.23 \mathrm{kJ} \mathrm{mol}^{-1})$ of the former were also lower than those of the latter (57.98 and $37.46 \mathrm{kJ} \mathrm{mol}^{-1}$ ). Apart from the accelerated interfacial kinetics, we noticed that the P-HCLTO system delivered superior chemical stability, which was evidenced by time-dependent interfacial resistance evolution modeled through the empirical relationship $y = a * t^{b}$ (where $y$ represents $R_{\mathrm{SEI}}$ or $R_{\mathrm{ct}}, t$ denotes resting time, and $b$ quantifies resistance growth rate). Both $R_{\mathrm{SEI}}$ and $R_{\mathrm{ct}}$ in the P-HCLTO system underwent a slower increase (lower $b$ values) during storage (Fig. 6, M and N, figs. S35 and S36, and table S7), which confirmed that the HIEC architecture and its derived SEI were effective in inhibiting parasitic reactions. Long-term cycling performance further revealed electrochemical stability of Li encapsulation effect through the P-HCLTO interlayer, demonstrating a reduced polarization voltage ( $\sim 16.4\mathrm{mV}$ ), extended cycling life (2000 hours) and enhanced high-current/high-capacity tolerance compared to the bare Cu counterpart (Fig. 6O and figs. S37 and S38). These performance enhancements stem from synergistic effects brought about by the HIEC architecture, including lithophilicity-mediated homogeneous nucleation, Li encapsulation, and corrosion-resistant interface facilitated by robust hollow nanocavities, as well as optimized Li-ion flux and kinetics that effectively mitigate voltage polarization.

# Electrochemical performance of anode-less LMBs and AFLMBs  The intriguingly high reversibility and rapid kinetics demonstrated by P-HCLTO as an HIEC interlayer reveal the emergence of a promising Li reservoir concept, prompting systematic evaluation of LMBs under anode-less or even anode-free configurations. Hence, we assembled all of batteries under the industrially relevant conditions, i.e., high-mass-loading cathode, constrained negative electrode/positive electrode capacity (N/P) ratio, and limited electrolyte/cathode capacity (E/C) ratio. The commercial-level $\mathrm{LiFePO_4}$ (LFP) cathode with the high mass loading ( $\sim 12.8\mathrm{mgcm}^{-2}$ ) was first used in anode-less LMBs with the E/C ratio of $10\mu \mathrm{l}\mathrm{mAh}^{-1}$ and the N/P ratio of 0.5 (Fig. 7, A and B, and fig. S39). The battery with P-HCLTO consistently outperformed the bare Cu counterpart across all current densities, delivering higher specific capacities and reduced voltage polarization, attributed to faster Li-ion diffusion and interfacial charge transfer. In addition, extended cycling at $0.5\mathrm{C}$ further revealed the superior stability in the battery with Li@P-HCLTO, achieving $\sim 93\%$ capacity retention and $\sim 99.7\%$ average CE over 200 cycles (Fig. 7C). In stark contrast, the Li@bare Cu counterpart exhibited the continuously elevated polarization profile with premature battery failure, likely arising from "dead Li" accumulation and electrolyte depletion (fig. S40). Notably, the cycling life of the battery with Li@P-HCLTO can be further prolonged at an increased N/P ratio of 1.5 (fig. S41). The electrochemical reversibility of Li plating/stripping was further investigated through the AFLMB configuration. The battery with P-HCLTO retained $\sim 66\%$ capacity with $\sim 99.2\%$ average CE over 100 cycles, whereas the bare Cu counterpart underwent the rapid degradation pattern, which was consistent with the previous failure mechanism (Fig. 7D). This result arises from HIEC-mediated Li accommodation, which enables dendrite-free morphological feature and suppresses the mechanical pulverization caused by parasitic reactions and volume expansion, as evidenced by comparative SEM imaging analysis (Fig. 7, E and F). Here, we also evaluated the impact of the CPL protocol on the AFLMBs (fig. S42). The result showed that the battery with P-HCLTO could deliver a higher discharge capacity compared to that with HCLTO, further demonstrating its efficacy in addressing irreversible Li loss at initial cycle. In addition, the P-HCLTO architecture maintained practical viability even pairing with the ultrahigh-mass-loading LFP cathode ( $\sim 29.2\mathrm{mgcm}^{-2}$ ). These batteries achieved $\sim 88\%$ capacity retention over 220 cycles at the N/P ratio of 1.5 and $\sim 50\%$ capacity retention over 140 cycles in the anode-free configuration, respectively (figs. S43 and S44). To further validate the applicability of our strategy in the high-energy-density LMBs, we evaluated the electrochemical performance using a high-mass-loading NCM811 cathode $(\sim 18.5\mathrm{mgcm}^{-2})$ , in which a localized high-concentration electrolyte [ $1.4\mathrm{M}$ lithium bisfluorosulfonimide (LiFSI) DME/2H,3H-decafluoropentane (HFC) (1:3 by mol)] was used as the abovementioned electrolyte (DOL/ DME-based) is unable to afford long-term high voltage stability (57). Under stringent operational parameters (N/P ratio of 1.5, cutoff voltage of $4.4\mathrm{V}$ , and $\mathrm{E / C}$ ratio of $10\mu \mathrm{l}\mathrm{mAh}^{-1}$ ), the Li@bare Cu configuration exhibited rapid capacity fade after 70 cycles with complete failure at 130 cycles (Fig. 7G and fig. S45). In contrast, the battery with Li@P-HCLTO demonstrated exceptional cyclability, achieving $\sim 80\%$ capacity retention and $\sim 99.7\%$ average CE over 160 cycles. Extended AFLMB testing further revealed the battery using P-HCLTO that exhibited slower capacity decay and stable voltage polarization across various operational conditions (figs. S46 to S48). Notably, under extremely lean electrolyte (E/C ratio of $3\mu \mathrm{l}\mathrm{mAh}^{-1}$ ), the battery with P-HCLTO still delivered $\sim 83\%$ capacity retention after 50 cycles with $\sim 99.4\%$ average CE and stable polarization voltage (Fig. 7, H and I, and fig. S49). Last, as a proof of concept, we fabricated a single-layer stacking pouch battery with a higher-mass-loading NCM811 cathode $(\sim 21.0\mathrm{mgcm}^{-2}$ $1.5~\mathrm{cm}$ by $1.5\mathrm{cm}$ in area) to validate the scaling potential of our designed AFLMB (Fig. 7J and fig. S50). Even at a low stacking pressure $(\sim 240\mathrm{kPa})$ , the pouch battery still demonstrated good performances, achieving $\sim 68\%$ capacity retention after 100 cycles and smooth voltage profiles (Fig. 7K and fig. S50E). Through comparing the recent progress on AFLMBs, our results stand out as one of the best performances without capacity diving phenomenon in particular considering the stringent testing conditions (Fig. 7L and table S8). It should be noted that, although the AFLMBs with bare Cu are endowed with the highest volumetric energy density, this calculation is usually based on the initial state of batteries. When the batteries are fully charged, a substantial amount of lithium will be deposited on Cu CCs with sharply increased volume, leading to discount in energy density. In comparison, the preliminary evaluation indicates that the HIEC interlayer exhibits an even higher volume energy density when considering the volume expansion at fully charged state (fig. S51 and tables S9 and S10). Although traditional 3D porous CCs can also achieve similar effects, their internal pores have an open structure, requiring a large amount of electrolyte wetting during operation and largely reducing the energy density (fig. S52). On the basis of the above analysis, we confirmed that the interlayer with the integrated HIEC architecture can promote accelerated interfacial dynamics, robust Li protection, and suppressed Li expansion, thus demonstrating the practical benefits of the strategy. Stable internal stress in the HIEC interlayer modified AFLMBs Unlike the intercalation chemistry of Li ion in graphite anodes (volume expansion $< 15\%$ ), LMB operation exhibits asymmetric dimensional dynamics, where cathode delithiation induces minor structural contraction during charge whereas simultaneous Li plating at the anode triggers substantial thickness variation. This expansion-contraction duality reverses during discharge, generating notable cyclic internal stress fluctuations in the battery (fig. S53). In principle, the host-free Li plating exhibits the infinite volume expansion and consequent stress accumulation. These inherent limitations are extremely detrimental to electrochemical reversibility and mechanical stability of LMBs. In comparison, the P-HCLTO interlayer is capable of accommodating substantial Li to enable the stress modulation. To monitor the stress evolution in coin-type LMB configuration, we developed an operando system (Fig. 8A and fig. S54). The core thought of this method is the implantation of thin-film force-sensitive resistor (FSR), using its piezoresistive response to dynamically track internal mechanical forces 1 generated in Li plating/stripping cycles. Specifically, the real-time resistance variations of FSR induced by interfacial stress fluctuations are captured through a Wheatstone bridge configuration, digitized by the computer, and computationally converted to internal stress $(P)$ -time profiles using the predefined function. This approach enables continuous mapping of $P$ value evolution synchronized with the galvanostatic cycling time. As a proof of concept, we chose the AFLMB with the LFP cathode as the two-phase transformation from $\mathrm{LiFePO_4}$ to $\mathrm{FePO_4}$ during the delithiation/lithiation process was endowed with a negligible volumetric strain ( $\sim 7\%$ ). The initial $P$ values exhibited similar baseline values for both bare Cu (0.47 MPa) and P-HCLTO (0.46 MPa) architectures (Fig. 8, B and C). However, distinct internal stress evolution patterns emerged during cycling. The bare Cu system demonstrated progressive mechanical stress accumulation, with the end-of-plating $P$ value increasing from 0.51 MPa (cycle 1) to 0.53 MPa (cycle 5), reflecting Li dendrite growth and "dead Li" formation. In contrast, the P-HCLTO system maintained near-constant stress profiles throughout cycling, which directly confirmed the capability of the HIEC interlayer to suppress the volume expansion of Li plating. Through analysis of the anode morphology of batteries in different charge and discharge states (fig. S55), we found that an additional thick lithium coating ( $\sim 18~\mu \mathrm{m}$ ) formed on the bare Cu surface, and after stripping, there was a large amount of SEIs and "dead Li." In contrast, P-HCLTO, as a Li storage interlayer, exhibited an unchanged thickness, thereby maintaining more stable interface stress within the battery. In addition, the AFM analysis (fig. S56) showed a higher DMT modulus for the P-HCLTO interlayer than the native SEI derived from the bare Cu substrate, which provided additional evidence for stabilizing internal stress.

# DISCUSSION  Uncontrolled Li deposition behavior and Li-electrolyte parasitic reactions in AFLMBs represent outstanding roadblocks to their long-term operation. We have fabricated a Li-rich nanocavity-confined interlayer through gradient hybrid integration of ion-conductive LTO and electron-conductive carbon. This HIEC architecture synergistically combines the following merits: intrinsic lithophilicity for nucleation guidance, (ii) mixed ion-electron transport pathways, (iii) autonomous metallic Li accommodation through nanocavity encapsulation, and (iv) electrolyte penetration blocking via dense interface. The CPL further offsets the irreversible Li-ion loss of the native HIEC structure and enables the formation of the hierarchical SEI dominated by an outer organic adsorption layer and an inner inorganic-rich matrix, which effectively passivates the interface and enhances the interfacial carrier diffusion kinetics. As a result, the nanocavity-confined mechanism of the HIEC is capable to accommodate Li up to $\sim 5\mathrm{mAhcm}^{-2}$ with nearly stress-free condition. Therefore, the CE and cycling life of anode-less LMBs or even AFLMBs under the lean electrolyte condition are synergistically extended. Overall, this study presents a nanoarchitecture-tailored interlayer that fundamentally resolves the structural incompatibility limitations inherent to the conventional artificial SEI or open-cell host architectures, confirming that the stabilizing Li anode requires an integrative strategy to simultaneously suppress parasitic reactions and buffer volume variations. In comparison with the negligible volume change of Li-ion batteries, the high volume change of next-generation energy-dense batteries during the charge/discharge process has become the bottleneck of applications. For example, the huge internal stress also leads to the inferior contact and subsequent fast failure of solid-state batteries. This HIEC can suppress volume deformation and interfacial detachment of Li metal during cycling, thereby enhancing the interfacial compatibility and cycling durability. The fundamental insights not only establish a paradigm for the design of AFLMBs but also provide inspirations for high-activity metal (Na, K, or Ca) battery systems facing similar challenges.

# MATERIALS AND METHODS

# Materials  Anhydrous EtOH was purchased from Energy Chemical. TBOT $(99\%)$ was purchased from Aladdin. Anhydrous LiOH $(99\%)$ was purchased from J&K Scientific. BP $(99.5\%)$ , LiTFSI $(99\%)$ and HFC $(98\%)$ were purchased from Tokyo Chemical Industry. $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ $(99.99\%)$ and DOL $(99.8\%)$ were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. DME $(99.9\%)$ and LiFSI $(99.9\%)$ were purchased from Dodo Chem. Poly(lithium acrylate) [PAALi; 3.89 wt % in water $(\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O})]$ was purchased from Guangdong Canrd New Energy Technology Co. Ltd. PS nanospheres were purchased from Haian Zhichuan Battery Material Technology Co. Ltd. All solvents were used without further purification.

# Methods In a typical process, $0.2\mathrm{g}$ of PS nanospheres were dispersed in a mixed solvent consisting of anhydrous EtOH $(40\mathrm{ml})$ and $\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}$ $(0.4\mathrm{ml})$ under ultrasonication for 1 hour. Subsequently, $0.3\mathrm{ml}$ of TBOT was introduced into the resulting suspension, and the mixture was stirred vigorously for $30\mathrm{min}$ . Thereafter, $15\mathrm{ml}$ of LiOH/EtOH solution $(2\mathrm{mg}\mathrm{ml}^{-1})$ was slowly added, followed by gentle stirring for $30\mathrm{min}$ to facilitate the adsorption of LiOH onto the surface of the nanospheres. Upon reaction completion, the initial reaction mixture was transferred into a Teflon-lined autoclave and aged for 3 hours at $120^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ . Last, after washing three times with anhydrous EtOH and centrifugation, the resulting composite nanospheres were collected and dried for 8 hours at $60^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ for calcination. The calcination procedure typically involved heating to $400^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ in an argon atmosphere at a rate of $3^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\mathrm{min}^{-1}$ and then keeping at $400^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ for 2 hours, followed by natural cooling to room temperature.

The HCLTO nanocavities were dispersed into a PAALi/ $\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}$ solution under continuous magnetic stirring for 6 hours to form a homogeneous slurry with a mass concentration of $0.03\mathrm{gml}^{-1}$ (the concentration of HCLTO in $\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}$ ). The solid-phase mass ratio between HCLTO nanocavities and the PAALi binder was optimized to 7:3. The slurry was subsequently coated onto the Cu foil using a precision doctor blade with a designated wet film thickness of $400\mu \mathrm{m}$ . After coating, the resulting interlayer was subjected to vacuum drying at $60^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ overnight, achieving a final dry thickness of 25 to $30\mu \mathrm{m}$ .

The preparation of the CPL solution and CPL treatment of interlayers were performed in an argon-filled glovebox $\left[\mathrm{O}_{2}\right.$ and $\left.\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}\right. < 0.01$ parts per million (ppm)]. The CPL solution was prepared by incorporating Li metal into a $0.5 \mathrm{M}$ BP/DME solution, followed by vigorous shaking for 1 hour and subsequent aging for 5 hours to ensure complete reaction. The molar ratio of Li metal to BP was maintained at 4 to facilitate sufficient reaction kinetics. Before use, residual Li metal in the CPL solution was removed by filtration. For the CPL treatment, the as-prepared HCLTO interlayer was immersed in the CPL solution, with the degree of lithiation precisely controlled by varying the immersion time. The lithiation process was terminated by rinsing the interlayer with electrolyte. The resulting prelithiated HCLTO was denoted as P-HCLTO.

# Cell assembly and electrochemical measurements  The 2032-type coin cells assembled in an argon-filled glovebox $(\mathrm{O}_2$ and $\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O} < 0.01~\mathrm{ppm})$ were used for all electrochemical measurements. The electrochemical performance under ambient conditions was evaluated on a battery test system (LAND CT2001A). The polarization voltage for all types of cells was calculated as the value of the charging medium voltage minus the discharging medium voltage.

A Li foil (10 mm in diameter) with a thickness of either 20 or $750\mu \mathrm{m}$ was used as the counter electrode. The CC (12 mm in diameter) consisted of either bare Cu or P-HCLTO. A single layer of PP/PE/PP separator (Celgard 2325, 19 mm in diameter) was used. The used electrolyte consisted of 1 M LiTFSI in a 1:1 volume ratio of DOL and DME with 2 wt $\%$ $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ as an additive [1 M LiTFSI DOL/DME (1:1 by volume) + 2 wt $\%$ $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ , 40 $\mu \mathrm{l}$ ]. Galvanostatic cycling was conducted to evaluate long-term CE, defined as the ratio of the charging (stripping) capacity to the discharging (plating) capacity in each cycle. During the cycling process, a controlled amount of Li was deposited onto the Cu CC and then stripped to a cutoff potential of 1 V. For half-cells using a $750 - \mu \mathrm{m}$ Li foil as the counter electrode and bare Cu as the CC, 10 precycles were performed between 0 and 1 V (versus $\mathrm{Li} / \mathrm{Li}^{+}$ ) under $0.063\mathrm{mAcm}^{-2}$ before galvanostatic cycling. The half-cells with a $20 - \mu \mathrm{m}$ Li foil as the counter electrode were tested without precycling.

A Li-metal layer of $5\mathrm{mAhcm}^{-2}$ was initially preelectrodeposited on the CC (bare Cu or P-HCLTO, $12\mathrm{mm}$ in diameter) at $0.5\mathrm{mA cm}^{-2}$ within the aforementioned half-cells. Subsequently, the cells were disassembled in the glovebox to obtain the Li-plated electrodes, denoted as $\mathrm{Li@}$ bare Cu or $\mathrm{Li@P}$ -HCLTO. The Li-plated electrode was carefully rinsed with DME and dried. The symmetric cell was assembled using the above two identical Li-plated electrodes, a single layer of Celgard 2325 separator ( $19\mathrm{mm}$ in diameter), and 1 M LiTFSI DOL/DME (1:1 by volume) + 2 wt $\%$ $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ electrolyte $(100\mu l)$ for the plating/stripping measurement.

A controlled amount of Li, corresponding to the N/P ratio specified in the main text, was initially preelectrodeposited on the CC (bare Cu or P-HCLTO, $12\mathrm{mm}$ in diameter) at $0.5\mathrm{mAcm}^{-2}$ within the aforementioned half-cells. Subsequently, the cells were disassembled in the glovebox to obtain the Li-plated electrodes, denoted as $\mathrm{Li@}$ bare Cu or $\mathrm{Li@P - HCLTO}$ . The Li-plated electrode was carefully rinsed with DME and dried. The coin-type battery was assembled using a Li-plated electrode, a single layer of Celgard 2325 separator $(19\mathrm{mm}$ in diameter), and a cathode $(10\mathrm{mm}$ in diameter), in which the volume of electrolyte was adjusted according to the E/C ratio. For the Li||LFP battery evaluation, a commercial LFP cathode ( $\sim 2.2$ or $\sim 5.0\mathrm{mAhcm}^{-2}$ in areal capacity) was used in conjunction with a 1 M LiTFSI DOL/DME (1:1 by volume) + 2 wt $\%$ $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ electrolyte, and the test was conducted within a voltage window of 2.0 to $3.8\mathrm{V}$ . The Li||NCM811 battery evaluation used a commercial NCM811 cathode ( $\sim 3.9\mathrm{mAhcm}^{-2}$ in areal capacity), coupled with an electrolyte comprising $1.4\mathrm{M}$ LiFSI in a 1:3 (mol/mol) mixture of DME and HFC [1.4 M LiFSI DME/HFC (1:3 by mol)], and was tested within a voltage window of 2.8 to $4.4\mathrm{V}$ . For the AFLMB experiment, the batteries were assembled with a CC (bare Cu, HCLTO, or P-HCLTO, $12\mathrm{mm}$ in diameter), a single layer of Celgard 2325 separator $(19\mathrm{mm}$ in diameter), a cathode (LFP or NCM811, $10\mathrm{mm}$ in diameter), and the corresponding electrolyte. The anode-free LFP battery was cycled within a voltage window of 2.0 to $3.8\mathrm{V}$ , whereas the anode-free NCM811 battery was tested within a voltage window of 3.6 to 4.4 or 3.0 to $4.4\mathrm{V}$ . Notably, the coin-type battery incorporating the NCM811 cathode was designed with aluminum (Al)-clad cathode cases, wherein an Al foil $(19\mathrm{mm}$ in diameter) was placed on the inner surface to mitigate corrosion of the stainless steel components. For the assembly of an anode-free single-layer pouch battery, a CC (P-HCLTO, $1.8\mathrm{cm}$ by $1.8\mathrm{cm}$ in area), a single layer of Celgard 2325 separator $(2.0\mathrm{cm}$ by $2.0\mathrm{cm}$ in area), an NCM811 cathode $\sim 4.4\mathrm{mAhcm}^{-2}$ in areal capacity, $1.5\mathrm{cm}$ by $1.5\mathrm{cm}$ in area), and a $1.4\mathrm{M}$ LiFSI DME/HFC (1:3 by mol) electrolyte were used. The battery components were encapsulated within an Al-plastic film packaging. Subsequent to assembly, the pouch battery was subjected to electrochemical testing within a voltage window of 3.6 to $4.4\mathrm{V}$ .

The diffusion coefficient based on Fick's law is evaluated by following the simplified equation (58, 59) $$ D = \frac {4}{\pi \tau} \left(\frac {n _ {m} V _ {m}}{S}\right) ^ {2} \left(\frac {\Delta E _ {s}}{\Delta E _ {t}}\right) ^ {2} \tag {1} $$ $$ \tau \ll \frac {L ^ {2}}{D} \tag {2} $$ where $\tau$ is the relaxation time, $n_m$ is the mole value, $V_{m}$ is the mole volume, $S$ is the contact area of the electrode/electrolyte, $\Delta E_{s}$ is the voltage response stimulated by the pulse current, and $\Delta E_{t}$ is the voltage change by the galvanostatic discharge.

EIS and $j_0$ measurements were performed using an electrochemical workstation (CHI 760E). EIS was conducted over a frequency range of $10^{6}$ to $10^{-1}\mathrm{Hz}$ with an applied ac amplitude of $5\mathrm{mV}$ . For the determination of $j_0$ , a symmetric Li||Li cell was assembled using the Li-plated electrode (Li@bare Cu or Li@P-HCLTO), a glass fiber separator (GF/D, Whatman), and 1 M LiTFSI DOL/DME (1:1 by volume) + 2 wt % $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ electrolyte $(100\mu l)$ . Subsequently, an $I - V$ curve was recorded within a voltage range of $-0.05$ to $0.05\mathrm{V}$ at a scan rate of $10^{-4}\mathrm{Vs}^{-1}$ .

# Physicochemical characterizations Microscopic morphology and structural analysis  The morphology and elemental distribution of the samples were characterized through field-emission SEM, (JSM-7900F, JEOL) equipped with EDX (Nordlys Max3, Oxford). Microstructural characterizations, including high-resolution transmission electron microscope (HRTEM) images, high-angle annular dark field (HAADF) images, and EELS data, were performed by scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM; Talos F200X, FEI, 200 KV) and TEM (JEM-ARM200F, JEOL, 200 KV) with an spherical-aberration corrector and EELS spectrometer. Nondestructive cross-sectional morphology of the Li-plated electrode was observed using dual-beam SEM (Crossbeam 340SEM, ZEISS) with FIB. The in situ Li deposition process was visually documented through OM (BX43, Olympus). The surface roughness and DMT modulus were measured by AFM (Dimension Icon, Bruker). The surface potential mapping was acquired via KPFM (Dimension Icon, Bruker). The crystallographic structure was analyzed with an x-ray diffractometer (SmartLab, Rigaku) with Cu Kα radiation ( $\lambda = 1.5406\AA$ ) as the excitation source. The wettability and surface tension were evaluated using an optical contact angle system (OCA25, DataPhysics).

A Raman spectrometer (inVia Qontor, RENISHAW, excitation wavelength of $532\mathrm{nm}$ ) was used to obtain molecular structural information. XPS (Escalab 250Xi, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to analyze the composition of functional groups, where a monochromatic Al Kα x-ray acted as the excitation source (1486.6 eV) and all peaks would be calibrated for adventitious carbon with the binding energy of the C 1s peak at 284.80 eV. An EPR spectrometer (ELEXSYS E580, Bruker) was used to characterize oxygen vacancies. A time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometer (TOF-SIMS5, IONTOF) was used to study the chemical composition with sputtering times. For SEM, XPS, and TOF-SIMS characterizations, the lithiated and Li metal-containing samples were meticulously rinsed with DME to remove residual electrolyte and subsequently dried in the glovebox before testing. To prevent oxidation or potential contamination from ambient air, the entire handling and transfer procedure of the samples, from the glovebox to the respective vacuum chambers, was conducted under a protective atmosphere using a vacuum transfer device or an argon environment.
