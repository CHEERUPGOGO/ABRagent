# 2. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION  2.1. Steric Effect on $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ Solvation. In previous works, PFPN was generally served as a flame-retardant additive (less than 5 vol %) in lithium battery electrolyte, and it was seldom employed as a cosolvent in electrolyte.[36-38] Here, we surprisingly found that PFPN could be used as a special $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ coordinating solvent (as high as 40 vol %) which exhibits a large steric hindrance effect. As shown in Figure 1a, the fluorinated cyclotriphosphazene ring in PFPN is responsible for flame-retardant and film-forming functions, while it is almost noncoordinating with $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ due to its low polarity.[39] The ethoxy group in PFPN provides the coordinating site and the synergistic effect of oxygen and nitrogen atoms realizing its stable coordination with $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ . Therefore, PFPN can enter the solvation shell, and the steric hindrance from the bulky cyclotriphosphazene group will potentially reconstruct the $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ solvation. Thus, our designed electrolyte was denoted as E-PFPN, i.e., $1.5\mathrm{M}$ lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) and $0.5\mathrm{M}$ lithium difluoro(oxalato)borate (LiDFOB) in 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME)/fluoroethylene carbonate (FEC)/PFPN, 3:3:4 by volume. To find out the role of PFPN in the electrolyte, for comparison, E-DFOB/FEC (1.5 M LiTFSI and 0.5 M LiDFOB in DME/FEC, 7:3 by volume) was used as the control electrolyte. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were first conducted to illustrate the $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ solvation structure. $^{12,40}$ The snapshot, radical distribution function (RDF) and coordination number resulting from MD were shown in Figure1d,e and Figures S1 and S2. Although the coordination number of PFPN was less than that of DME or FEC, the bulky steric hindrance from PFPN reduces the coordination number of other solvents (Figures 1d and S2a). In addition, the proportion of anions is increased in E-PFPN due to the local increased concentration caused by steric effect (Figures 1e and S2b). The average coordination numbers in the inner solvation shell for each electrolyte were collected in Figure 2h. For E-DFOB/FEC, the coordination ratio of DME and FEC is higher than that of the anions, which causes a large preference for SSIPs abounding in solvation. For E-PFPN, stronger tendency for anions to coordinate with $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ in the solvation, which finally boosting the overall proportion of CIPs and AGGs from $62\%$ to $85\%$ (Figure 1i). Density-functional theory (DFT) calculations were carried out to quantify the influence of the steric effect from PFPN (Figure 1f,g). $^{31}$ The electrostatic potential mapping expresses all the solvents have coordinating sites with $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ (Figure S3). Calculating the binding energy between solvent and $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ , when PFPN participates in solvation, the binding energy between $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ and DME or FEC increases by about 20 $\mathrm{kJ / mol}$ , indicating the $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ -solvent coordination are weakened by the steric hindrance of PFPN. The spatial distance can also concretize the influence of steric effect on solvation,[31] taking the Li-DME system as example (Figure 1f), the Li-O (on DME) distance was $1.858\AA$ versus $1.918\AA$ for Li-O (on DME with PFPN). Such an increased distance contributed to a looser $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ solvation and impaired the solvent coordination. According to the theoretical calculation, we proposed the following hypothetical model of $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ solvation. The strong $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ -solvent coordination contributes to a tight $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ solvation dominated by solvent molecules in E-DFOB/FEC (Figure 1b); however, E-PFPN exhibits a looser solvation with weakened solvating power of solvents and broadened range of solvation (Figure 1c). The bulky PFPN will compress the effective space within the solvation and thereby increase the local anions concentration, which makes the solvents no longer dominate the solvation and the levels of CIPs and AGGs in solvation will increase. Districting from conventional solvation tuning strategies, this strategy relies on molecular steric effect dispense with extreme solute/solvent ratios or completely dominant weakly solvents, therefore, it not only provides sufficient solubility, but also reconciles the ionic conductivity and electrochemical stability of electrolyte.[11] The ionic conductivity of relevant electrolytes was provided in Table S1. If we just replace the PFPN in E-PFPN with the conventional diluent 1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl-2,2,2-trifluoroethyl ether (HFE) to create a LHCE (E-HFE) similar to E-PFPN, its conductivity is significantly less than E-PFPN, which should be attributable to the interaction between PFPN and $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ provides additional solubility. The ionic conductivity of E-PFPN is also higher than that of HCE, WSE, and FWSE provided from other typical references. $^{12,22,29,31}$ To obtain a deeper understanding of $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ solvation, nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) measurements were implemented. $^{32}\mathrm{H}$ NMR spectra were recorded to evaluate the coordination environment of solvents. Compared with that in E-DFOB/FEC, an upfield shift in E-PFPN indicates increased electron density around solvents due to the weakened electron delocalization effect from $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ and intensified shielding effect from increased anions in solvation (Figure 2a). The $^{19}\mathrm{F}$ NMR spectra were shown in Figure 2b and Figure S4 to explore the environment of anions in solvation. The signals of both $\mathrm{TFSI^{-}}$ and $\mathrm{DFOB^{-}}$ in E-PFPN are upshifted compared with that in E-DFOB/FEC, demonstrating the increased $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ -anion coordination. $^{12}$ An apparent downfield shift of $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ nuclei in E-PFPN is observed in $^{7}\mathrm{Li}$ NMR spectra, indicating the loose solvation E-PFPN brings a stronger deshielding effect to $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ (Figure 2c). $^{32}$ In order to further identify the $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ -PFPN coordination, we compared two electrolytes containing LiTFSI or sodium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (NaTFSI). ${}^{1}\mathrm{H}$ NMR spectra of the ethoxy oxygen on PFPN upfield shifted when the cation goes from $\mathrm{Na^{+}}$ to $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ , which was ascribed to the $\mathrm{Li^{+}-}$ PFPN coordination. This enhances the shielding effect on the protons in ethoxy, and the larger ionic radius of $\mathrm{Na^{+}}$ relatively reduces this shielding effect compared with that in $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ solvation (Figure 2d). Compared with pure PFPN, ${}^{19}\mathrm{F}$ NMR spectra of the electrolytes contain LiTFSI or NaTFSI show peaks at high field. However, there is negligible shift between the electrolytes with different cations (Figure 2e). These data support that the ethoxy group provides the coordinating site with cations and the cyclotriphosphazene group is relatively far away from the cations. Once the PFPN participate in the solvation, there is slight effect for distinct cations in the surrounding environment of cyclotriphosphazene groups. This model is exactly consistent with the hypothetical model in theoretical analysis. Next, the solvation was further elucidated by Raman spectroscopy.[29] The S–N–S peak of $\mathrm{TFSI}^{-}$ at $741.9~\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ in 1 M LiTFSI–DME indicates most $\mathrm{TFSI}^{-}$ are present as a SSIP structure (Figure 2f). By comparison, the peaks in E-DFOB/ FEC and E-PFPN show a blue shift from 741.9 to 746.6 ( $\mathrm{TFSI}^{-}$ in CIP) or $751.3~\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ ( $\mathrm{TFSI}^{-}$ in AGG).[41] Higher intensity peaks for $\mathrm{TFSI}^{-}$ in CIP and AGG in E-PFPN indicate more anions coordinate with $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ and larger proportion of CIPs and AGGs structures in solvation distribution (Table S2).42 Those data agree well with the theoretical calculation results in Figure 1i. It is noteworthy that PFPN is helpful to improve the safety of the LMBs especially in the ether-based electrolyte. The electrolyte ignition tests given the self-extinguishing time (SET) was $0\mathrm{~s~g}^{-1}$ and $144\mathrm{~s~g}^{-1}$ (Figure 2g, h) for E-PFPN and E-DFOB/FEC, respectively, which prove the effectiveness of the PFPN in fire resistance. From the theoretical and experimental evidence above, we could assert that the steric effect of PFPN in $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ solvation can weaken the solvation ability of other solvents and increase the ratio of anions in $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ solvation. 2.2. Li Metal Reversibility, Morphology, and SEI Structure. We first assembled $\mathrm{Li / Cu}$ and $\mathrm{Li / Li}$ cells to explore the compatibility of E-PFPN with the Li anode. As shown in Figure 3a, the Coulombic efficiency (CE) of $\mathrm{Li / Cu}$ cells in commercial carbonates electrolyte (1 M lithium hexafluorophosphate $(\mathrm{LiPF}_6)$ in ethylene carbonate (EC):diethyl carbonate (DEC) 1:1 by volume) quickly reduced from 85% to less than 40% within 100 cycles (1.0 mA cm $^{-2}$ , 1.0 mAh cm $^{-2}$ ). For E-DFOB/FEC electrolyte, the CE of E-DFOB/FEC rose to 93–96% within 150 cycles due to the promotion from additives; however, it showed rapid decline after 200 cycles probably due to the damage of unstable SEI after the exhaustion of additives. Impressively, E-PFPN enabled an average CE up to 97.7% (inset in Figure 3a) and maintained over 600 cycles. The corresponding voltage profiles also show better stability (Figure S5). The tests with higher deposition capacity and Aurbach CE tests were further applied (Figures 3b,c and S6). $^{43}$ When increasing the deposition capacity to 5 mAh cm $^{-2}$ , the Li/Cu cells with E-PFPN also show excellent cycling stable performance (100 cycles, CE 97.7%), while cells with LiPF $_6$ /EC/DEC or E-DFOB/FEC fall rapidly after 50 cycles. Aurbach CE test results displayed in Figure 3c demonstrate the superiority of E-PFPN by showing an obviously improved CE (98.7%). Furthermore, as shown in Figure 3d and Figure S7, Li/Li symmetric cells with E-PFPN show lower overpotential and extraordinary cyclic stability (2800 h, 0.5 mA cm $^{-2}$ , and 1 mAh cm $^{-2}$ ), compared with E-DFOB/FEC (1800 h). The excellent performance in Li/Cu and Li/Li cells using E-PFPN demonstrates the outstanding Li metal stability and reversibility, which may result from the improvement in interface characteristics and $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ transfer kinetics. The anion-dominated solvation in E-PFPN enables an anion-derived SEI with favorable Li metal compatibility and this loose solvation can decrease the migration barrier of $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ to improve $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ transfer kinetics. This view can be proved by the significantly higher $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ transference number of E-PFPN (0.72) than E-DFOB/FEC (0.33) (Figure S8). This much higher transference number leads to a higher $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ conductivity, which could not only accelerate $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ transfer kinetics but also inhibit lithium dendrites growth (Table S1).41 The deposition morphology of Li metal was examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The surface and cross-section morphologies of Li deposited on Cu substrates with different electrolytes were compared in Figure 4a-d and Figure S9. In $\mathrm{LiPF_6 / EC / DEC}$ , a porous Li deposition with large amounts of Li whiskers was observed, and the thickness of corroded Li is $19.3\mu \mathrm{m}$ in cross-section image (Figure S9). With the additive optimization in E-DFOB/FEC, it still presented as nonuniform Li deposition (Figure 4a), and the thickness of Li corrosion is slightly reduced to $11.9\mu \mathrm{m}$ with a Li deposition layer of $6.4\mu \mathrm{m}$ underneath (Figure 4b).[44-47] In sharp contrast, the deposited Li in E-PFPN shows a flat, smooth and densely packed Li morphology, and a dense deposited Li metal layer with a thickness of $11.1\mu \mathrm{m}$ was found in the cross-section image (Figure 4c,d). Even under a higher deposition capacity at $2.0\mathrm{mAhcm}^{-2}$ or a higher current density at $2.0\mathrm{mA cm}^{-2}$ , E-PFPN can still enable dense and flat Li depositions, whereas E-DFOB/FEC appears serious Li corrosion and dendrite growth (Figures S10 and S11). Those advantages of E-PFPN in Li morphology guarantee the stable Li plating/stripping, which should be attributed to the high-quality SEI and the accelerated $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ transfer kinetics.[40,48] To identify the relationship between the "bulky coordinating" solvation and the high-quality SEI, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was used to analyze the compositions of the SEI layer. Parts e and f of Figure 4 display the results for Li anode retrieved after 50 cycles in different electrolytes. The C 1s spectrum can be used as a representative of organic species, which usually derived from solvents decomposition.[47] Apparent signals of $\mathrm{C - C / C - H}$ (284.8 eV) and $\mathrm{C - O}$ (285.8 eV) species can be observed from the SEI in E-DFOB/FEC, while that in E-PFPN shows significantly reduced organic species, indicating less solvent reduction on the anode surface in E-PFPN. In the O 1s and F 1s spectrum, the SEI in E-PFPN contains much more LiF (684.8 eV) and $\mathrm{Li}_2\mathrm{O}$ (528.5 eV) compared with that in E-DFOB/FEC.[24] Even after 100 s of sputtering, these inorganic species are still abundant in the SEI derived from E-PFPN. This inorganic-rich SEI should be attributed to the anion-dominated solvation in E-PFPN induces anions participated in SEI formation. In addition, more decomposition products of $\mathrm{TFSI}^{-}$ such as $\mathrm{N - SO}_{x}, \mathrm{Li}_{3}\mathrm{N},$ and lower-valence sulfur $(S_{n}^{2-}$ and $S^{2-})$ were observed in the SEI from E-PFPN in the N 1s spectra and the S 2p spectra, testifying that $\mathrm{TFSI}^{-}$ is more completely reduced in the "bulky coordinating" solvation (Figure S12).[24,40] Moreover, the atomic ratio of C can serve as an indicator to represent the contributions from organic species in the SEI and the ratio of Li to represent the inorganic species.[24,25] As shown in Figure 4g,h, there are apparently higher Li atomic ratios and lower C atomic ratios in the SEI from E-PFPN than those in E-DFOB/ FEC, which further testify that the anion-dominated solvation in E-PFPN accelerates the reduction of anions and creates an anion-derived SEI rich in inorganic species like LiF and $\mathrm{Li}_{2}\mathrm{O}$ . Such an inorganic-rich SEI has a superior ability to isolate electrons and lower resistance to $\mathrm{Li}^{+}$ diffusion, resulting in faster $\mathrm{Li}^{+}$ transfer kinetics, suppressed corrosion reactions, inhibited lithium dendrites growth, and improved Li metal reversibility.

2.3. Oxidation Stability and Full-Cell Performance. After investigating the Li metal compatibility, we further explored the stability on the high-voltage cathode. Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) was conducted on Li/Al cells to unveil the oxidation stability of investigated electrolytes (Figure 5a). The poor oxidation stability of ether solvents leads to sudden anodic current increase below $4.0\mathrm{~V}$ in $1\mathrm{M}$ LiTFSI in DME. Forming a protective CEi can effectively isolate electrons and reduce the oxidation of solvents.[49] Thereby, relying on the CEi-forming ability of additives, E-DFOB/FEC broadens its electrochemical stability window to $4.5\mathrm{~V}$ , but it is still insufficient to enable the ultrahigh-voltage LMBs $(>4.5\mathrm{~V}$ vs. $\mathrm{Li}^{+}/\mathrm{Li})$ . Surprisingly, with E-PFPN, there are no obvious oxidation current increases until $5.1\mathrm{~V}$ . Floating tests were employed by continuously exposing the cathode at $4.5\mathrm{~V}$ and recording steady leakage current to quantify the rate of the parasitic reaction. The cell using E-PFPN reaches a minimum current value of $2.0\mu\mathrm{A}$ , which is significantly lower than that of $\mathrm{LiPF_6/EC/DEC}$ and E-DFOB/FEC (Figure 5b).[50] A self-discharge test was conducted by recording the open-circuit voltage of a fully charged cells to indicate the reactivity of electrolytes with cathodes.[50] As shown in Figure S13, the cell with E-PFPN exhibits less voltage decrease than $\mathrm{LiPF_6/EC/DEC}$ and E-DFOB/FEC. These observations from floating tests and self-discharge tests indicate E-PFPN has favorable oxidation stability and can effectively suppress the interfacial parasitic reaction. Therefore, LMBs with E-PFPN is qualified for the ultrastable and ultrahigh voltage cycling (Figure S14). With a cutoff voltage of $4.5\mathrm{~V}$ , the Li/NMC811 half-cells with E-PFPN achieved a discharge capacity of 218.1 mAh g $^{-1}$ at $0.5\mathrm{~C}$ and retained $90.8\%$ capacity even after 300 cycles, while that of E-DFOB/FEC is only $72.6\%$ . Even at an ultrahigh voltage of $4.6\mathrm{~V}$ , E-PFPN still enabled stable cycling over 200 cycles with a capacity retention of $81.5\%$ .

Li metal full-cells were further assembled under aggressive cycling conditions. Li/NMC811 full-cells using E-PFPN achieved superior cycling performance at $4.5\mathrm{V}$ with a high charge and discharge currents of 0.8 and $1.2\mathrm{mA}\mathrm{cm}^{-2}$ , which delivered a capacity retention of $89.2\%$ after 180 cycles. In contrast, cells with E-DFOB/FEC could only operate stably for 60 cycles and degraded after 140 cycles (Figure 5c). At an ultrahigh voltage of $4.6\mathrm{V}$ , the full-cells with E-PFPN provide $84.1\%$ initial capacity after 150 cycles with a high average CE of $>99.56\%$ (Figure 5d), while in E-DFOB/FEC, cells show a massive capacity fading after 100 cycles and a low average CE of $97.2\%$ . Despite the harsher testing conditions, our designed electrolyte shows higher capacity retention, higher energy efficiency and more stable voltage profiles than control electrolyte (Figure S15). The ultrahigh voltage cycling performance of the Li/NMC811 full-cells using E-PFPN is observed among the top levels to the best of our knowledge.

Summarily, we concluded that E-PFPN has a better oxidation stability and can form a protective CEI with low resistance on the NMC cathode. Thus, it realizes the outstanding cycling performance of ultrahigh voltage LMBs.

2.4. Characterization of NMC811 and Interphase between Cathode and Electrolyte. Three mechanisms are well accepted for the capacity degradation of the high voltage cathode:5,56-58 (1) Cracks between connected primary particles in a secondary cathode particle. (2) The reconstruction of cathode surface at high voltages through a phase transition from the layered to rock-salt structure. (3) The dissolution of the transition metal (TM) under high voltages. Thus, a series of characterizations were carried out based on these mechanisms to explore the influence of different electrolytes on ultrahigh voltage NMC cathode.

To characterize the microstructural degradation, morphologies of cathodes particles after 100 cycles at $4.6\mathrm{V}$ were observed under SEM. NMC particles cycled in E-DFOB/FEC and $\mathrm{LiPF_6 / EC / DEC}$ presented extensive cracks (Figures 6a and S20). These cracks facilitate the penetration of the electrolyte and result in the grievous particle pulverizations. However, such particle cracks were drastically restrained in E-PFPN, revealing that the CEI derived from E-PFPN is capable of suppressing corrosive reactions and preserving the cathode integrity at high voltage (Figures 6b, S21, and S22).57 The irreversible phase transition of cathode is another cause for capacity degradation. High resolution transmission electron microscope (HRTEM) was conducted to ascertain the phase transition process. After cycled at $4.6\mathrm{V}$ for 30 cycles, a 0.241 nm layer was observed over the cathode surface in E-DFOB/ FEC, corresponding to the (111) plane of rock-salt phase (Figure 6c and Figure S23). The rock-salt phase is a poor $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ conductor, which evidently slows the ion transport and increases the cell impedance.[56] As a comparison, for the sample cycled with E-PFPN (Figure 6d and Figure S24), a $0.473\mathrm{~nm}$ layered structure was maintained without any detectable rock-salt phase on the cathode surface, indicating the phase transition is effectively suppressed in E-PFPN. In addition, the CEI generated in E-DFOB/FEC is coarse and irregular with a thickness of $10 - 30\mathrm{~nm}$ (Figure 6e), while in E-PFPN, the cathode surface presents a uniform and smooth CEI layer with a thickness of $4.1\mathrm{~nm}$ (Figure 6f). This stark difference in interface morphology indicates that the CEI from E-DFOB/FEC undergoes persistent damage due to the electrolyte decomposition, while E-PFPN successfully builds a robust CEI to inhibit the continuous degradation of electrolyte.50 Next, X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was performed to inspect the NMC811 structure after ultrahigh voltage cycling.59,60 The degree of surface reconstruction of NMC811 can be evaluated by the intensity ratio of (003) and (104) peaks. A higher $I(003) / I$ (104) ratio represents less phase transition and cation mixing in the cathode.42,51 Compared with the $I(003) / I$ (104) ratio of the pristine NMC811 (1.46), the value of the NMC811 cycled in E-PFPN was 1.27, which is much higher than that of E-DFOB/FEC (0.57). This means the cathode cycled in E-PFPN presents much enhanced structural stability (Figures 6g and S25). The significant deformation of (003) peak in the cathode cycled in E-DFOB/FEC also demonstrate its extensive surface reconstruction, which agrees well with the TEM image in Figure 6c.

The cathode degradation is generally accompanied by the dissolution of TM, which will deposit on the Li metal and cause serious damage to SEI. An inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometer (ICP-OES) was used to quantify the amount of dissolved TM (Ni, Co, Mn) in electrolyte after 50 cycles at $4.6\mathrm{V}$ (Figures 6h and S26). The amount of these TM dissolution is significantly reduced by nearly three times than that in E-DFOB/FEC, proving that the protective CEI derived from E-PFPN can effectively stabilize the cathode surface.

In the aim to make certain the influence of the "bulky coordinating" solvation in the CEI forming process, XPS analysis was applied to investigate the detailed compositions of the CEI on NMC811 after 50 cycles at $4.6\mathrm{V}$ . The spectra of NMC811 cycled in E-DFOB/FEC were shown in Figure 6i. On account of the decomposition of solvents, apparent signals of $\mathrm{C - O}$ and $\mathrm{C = O}$ (288.6 eV) species can be observed in the C 1s spectrum, which agrees well with the O 1s spectrum (C-O at 533.2 eV and $\mathrm{C = O}$ at 531.6 eV). The signal of the metal oxide bond (M-O at 529.4 eV) is not detected at the first sputtering (0 s) due to the excessively thick CEI. The feeble signals of LiF and $\mathrm{Li}_3\mathrm{N}$ are derived from the slight decomposition of anions.[23] These XPS results demonstrate that an organic-rich CEI was generated in E-DFOB/PEC, which cannot suppress aggressive attacks from solvent decomposition and become uneven and thicken during its continuously destruction. However, for the CEI formed in E-PFPN (Figure 6j), although C-O and C=O species were also found in the C 1s spectrum, their content is obviously less than that in E-DFOB/PEC, indicating the solvent decomposition is efficiently suppressed in E-PFPN. This should be attributed to the "bulky coordinating" solvation weakening the $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ -solvent coordination and decreasing the polarization within the DME and FEC.[61] In F 1s and N 1s spectra, higher LiF and $\mathrm{LiN}_x\mathrm{O}_y$ content was detected on the cathode surface in E-PFPN, demonstrating that the anion decomposition is facilitated in E-PFPN. This could be ascribed to the "bulky coordinating" solvation increasing the probability of anions appearing at the interface and promoting the anions to participate in the CEI formation. These changes have great beneficial influence on cathode stability owing to the excellent electronic insulation of LiF and advantageous ion conducting property of $\mathrm{LiN}_x\mathrm{O}_y$ .[37] In addition, PFPN itself also has a film-forming ability.[36] In the O 1s, F 1s, N 1s, and P 2p spectra, peaks corresponding to N-O/ P-O, $\mathrm{Li}_x\mathrm{PF}_y\mathrm{NO}_2^-$ , N=P-N, and $\mathrm{Li}_x\mathrm{PO}_y$ are associated with the decomposition products of PFPN. These inorganic compounds derived from PFPN are generally believed to have superior ion conductivity, leading to fast ionic transport and low interphase impedance.[37,51] Owing to the fact that the CEI generated in E-PFPN is rich in LiF and N,P-containing inorganic compounds, parasitic reactions can be effectively suppressed at ultrahigh voltage. Thus, the signal of M-O can be observed even at the first sputtering (0 s), indicating an ultrathin CEI layer is formed in E-PFPN. Energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) was also performed to show the uniform distribution of F, N, and P elements in cathode surface, which agrees with the XPS analysis results (Figure S27).[51] Overall, since the "bulky coordinating" solvation in E-PFPN presents as anion-dominated rather than solvent-dominated, the irreversible decomposition of solvents is significantly restrained and more anions participate in the CEI formation process. Combining with the film-forming effect of PFPN, abundant $\mathrm{F}-$ , $\mathrm{N}-$ , and P-rich inorganic compounds construct a thin, robust, and ionic conducting CEI layer, which efficiently preserves the integrity of NMC particles and suppresses the cathode surface reconstruction and TM dissolution.

2.5. Performance of Practical Ultrahigh Voltage Li Metal Batteries. Industrial dry Li/NMC811 pouch cells were cycled in different electrolytes (Table S3 and Figure S28). The cycling performance of Li/NMC811 pouch cells using E-PFPN are shown in Figure 7a,b. Under the voltage range of $3.0 - 4.5\mathrm{V}$ , pouch cells exhibited a capacity retention of $84.5\%$ after 100 cycles with an outstanding average CE of nearly $100\%$ and good repeatability (Figure S29). Even at $4.6\mathrm{V}$ , it still had $80.2\%$ capacity retention over 50 cycles. Furthermore, a multilayer Li/NMC811 pouch cell with a higher capacity of 1.5 Ah was assembled and tested under the same conditions, which also showed outstanding cycling performance (Figure S30). Relying on the ultrahigh voltage capacity, the Li/ NMC811 pouch cell exhibits a high specific energy of 345 Wh $\mathrm{kg}^{-1}$ determined from the total weight of the pouch cell (Figure 7c). Considering the high proportion of the packaging in the total mass of the $500\mathrm{mAh}$ pouch cell, we calculated the specific energy based on the cell weight except packaging to simulate a practical cell with higher rated capacity, which displays a spectacular specific energy up to $495\mathrm{Whkg^{-1}}$ .

To compare the solvation tuning strategy proposed in this work with the conventional $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ solvation tuning strategies (HCE, LHCE, WSE, FWSE),[12,22,23,29] we evaluated the electrolytes from six angles: energy density, cutoff voltage, ion conductivity, full-cell life, safety and rated capability. These parameters of LMBs in this work show obvious advantages over that based on other solvation tuning strategies in previous reports (Figure 7d), which correspond to the metrics listed in Table S4.

Overall, the function of the "bulky coordinating" solvation in the electrolyte of ultrahigh-voltage LMBs are illustrated schematically in Figure 7, parts e and f, and the specific advantages can be summarized as follows: (1) Outstanding compatibility with the lithium metal anode. The anion-dominated solvation in E-PFPN contribute to form anion-derived SEI rich in LiF, which effectively reduces the irreversible decomposition of electrolyte, prevents Li metal corrosion and inhibit lithium dendrites growth. (2) Increased stability of cathode at ultrahigh voltage. E-PFPN itself has improved oxidation stability and forms a robust CEI contains F-, N-, and P-rich inorganic compounds and is able to restrain persistent parasitic reactions of electrolyte, suppress the cracks and reconstruction of the cathode, and reduce the TM dissolution. (3) Favorable $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ transport and charge transfer properties. The steric effect weakens the solvents binding energies, which decrease the barrier of $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ migration. Moreover, the thin and conductive CEI generated from E-PFPN enables a fast ionic transport and low interphase impedance. (4) Nonflammability and improved battery safety. E-PFPN is completely nonflammable, which gives an improved safety in the event of accidents.

To conclude, a solvation tuning strategy based on the molecular steric effect has been developed for ultrahigh-voltage LMBs. Demonstrated by both theoretical and experimental studies, we identified that the steric effect of bulky PFPN in $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ solvation causes weaker solvation ability of solvents and a looser solvation environment, contributing to higher anion content in the $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ solvation sheath. The designed electrolyte not only exhibits advantageous $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ conductivity and improved safety, but also generates dense and untrathin SEI and CEI to promote the stability of electrodes at ultrahigh voltage. Therefore, the designed E-PFPN electrolyte promises a high Li plating/stripping CE and $>5\mathrm{V}$ oxidative stability. Furthermore, the electrolytes allowed long-term cycling of thin Li/high-loading NMC811 $(\mathrm{N} / \mathrm{P} = 2.0)$ cells at ultrahigh voltage $(4.6\mathrm{V})$ with an outstanding capacity retention of $84.1\%$ over 150 cycles and high average CE of $>99.56\%$ . Industrial Li/NMC811 pouch cells with a cutoff voltage of $4.6\mathrm{V}$ and lean electrolyte exhibited an $80\%$ capacity retention for 50 cycles, delivering a specific energy of $495\mathrm{Whkg^{-1}}$ . The "bulky coordinating" solvation tuning strategy reported in this work provides a significant direction for the electrolyte engineering of high-energy LMBs.

# 4. EXPERIMENTAL SECTION/METHODS  4.1. Materials. Lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI, $99\%$ , battery grade, DoDoChem), lithium difluoro(oxalato)-borate (LiDFOB, $99\%$ , battery grade, DoDoChem), lithium hexafluorophosphate $(\mathrm{LiPF}_6, 99\%)$ , battery grade, DoDoChem), 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME, $>99.9\%$ , DoDoChem), sodium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (NaTFSI, $99\%$ , battery grade, DoDoChem), fluoroethylene carbonate (FEC, $>99.9\%$ , DoDoChem), ethylene carbonate (EC, $>99\%$ , DoDoChem), diethyl carbonate (DEC, $>99\%$ , DoDoChem), and ethoxy(pentafluoro)-cyclotriphosphazene (PFPN, $>99\%$ , Innochem Co., Ltd., China) were all used as received without further purification. We store all our materials in Argon-filled glovebox. Metallic Li foil was purchased by China Energy Lithium Co., Ltd. Cathode NMC811 laminates were prepared by laying a mixture of 90 wt % NMC811 material, 5 wt % super P and 5 wt % PVDF (8.0 wt % NMP) on an aluminum foil current collector. Industrial dry Li/NMC811 pouch cells were purchased from Hunan Cubic New Energy Technology Co., Ltd. 4.2. Electrolyte Preparation. The commercial electrolyte was composed of $1\mathrm{M}$ $\mathrm{LiPF_6}$ dissolved in EC:DEC (1:1 by volume). The additive optimized electrolyte (E-DFOB/FEC) was used as control electrolyte, which was composed of $1.5\mathrm{M}$ LiTFSI and $0.5\mathrm{M}$ LiDFOB dissolved in DME:FEC (7:3 by volume). The electrolyte with solvent PFPN (E-PFPN) was prepared by $1.5\mathrm{M}$ LiTFSI and $0.5\mathrm{M}$ LiDFOB dissolved in DME:FEC:PFPN (3:3:4 by volume). Two simulation electrolytes used in NMR test were $1\mathrm{M}$ LiTFSI dissolved in DME:PFPN (1:1 by volume) and $1\mathrm{M}$ NaTFSI dissolved in DME:PFPN (1:1 by volume). All electrolytes were made and stored in an argon-filled glovebox. 4.3. Characterizations. The SET was tested by igniting the electrolytes for $3\mathrm{s}$ and then evacuating the fire source and recording the combustion time. ${}^{1}\mathrm{H}-,{}^{7}\mathrm{Li}-$ and ${}^{19}\mathrm{F}$ -NMR spectra were recorded on a JEOL 400 MHz NMR spectrometer at room temperature. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM, JEOL, JSM-7401F, Japan) was conducted to characterize the surface morphology of lithium deposition and cathode NMC811. The Cu foil or cathode in a half cell after the preparation step was washed with 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) to remove residual lithium salts, dried, and then sealed in the glovebox until transferred for characterization. The whole process of sample preparation is carried out in a glovebox with oxygen and water contents below $0.1~\mathrm{ppm}$ . Transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEOL, JEM-2100Plus, Japan) were used to analyze the microstructure of cathode. The chemical composition information on SEI and CEI was obtained by using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Fisher Scientific Ltd., U.S.A) sputtering at different depths. The C 1s peak at $284.8\mathrm{eV}$ was used as the reference for all binding energies. X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements were detected by Rigaku MiniFlex600-C with a scan speed of 10.0 degree/min and a scan range from 10 to 70 degree to inspect cathode structure. Inductively coupled plasma-Optical emission spectrometer (ICP-OES, Thermo Fisher, IRIS Intrepid II, U.S.A) was used to determine the transition metal concentration in electrolyte. Raman spectrum measurements (HORIBA, Japan) were conducted on a LabRAM HR spectrometer with a $633~\mathrm{nm}$ excitation laser for the liquid electrolytes presealed in capillary tubes. The $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ conductivity was measured by a conductivity cell with two stainless steel electrodes. The conductivity cell was immersed into electrolyte and the Li ion conductivity was measured by the following formula:50 $$ \sigma = \frac {L}{A \times R} \tag {1} $$ Here $R$ is the resistance, and $A$ and $L$ are the area and spacing between the two stainless steel electrodes, respectively. The transference number of $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ was evaluated by the DC polarization measurements at $10\mathrm{mV}$ using the Bruce-Vincent method.[62] 4.4. Electrochemical Measurements. The electrochemical behavior of the coin cells was tested using 2032-type cells, and Celgard 2325 was used as the separator. The same amount $(65\mu \mathrm{L})$ of electrolyte was used in each coin cell. All batteries were fabricated in an argon-filled glovebox. The charge-discharge performances of the Li/Cu, Li/Li, and Li/NMC batteries were conducted on a battery test station (LANHE CT3001A). The electrochemical stability windows of different electrolytes were tested in a half cell with aluminum foil as a working electrode, Li foil as the working electrode and reference electrode using CHI 760E electrochemical workstation. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was performed from the frequency of $0.1\mathrm{Hz}$ to $100\mathrm{kHz}$ using two-electrode coin cells with Li as both the counter electrode and reference electrode. Aurbach CE test method was applied to evaluate the Coulombic efficiency. Specifically, $5\mathrm{mAhcm}^{-2}$ of Li were deposited on Cu under a current density of $0.5\mathrm{mA cm}^{-2}$ and then charged to $1.0\mathrm{V}$ to clean the Cu surface. Then plate quantitative Li reservoir $(Q_{t} = 5\mathrm{mAhcm}^{-2})$ on Cu foil, and repeatedly strip/plate Li with a capacity of $1\mathrm{mAhcm}^{-2}$ $(Q_{c})$ for $n$ cycles $(n = 10)$ at a current density of $0.5\mathrm{mA cm}^{-2}$ , and finally, the remaining Li was stripped to $1.0\mathrm{V}$ . The average Coulombic efficiency could be calculated by eq 2 $$ \mathrm {C E} = \frac {Q _ {R} + _ {c}}{Q _ {t} + _ {c}} \tag {2} $$ Here $Q_{R}$ indicates the remaining capacity in the final stripping procedure. 4.5. Computational Methods. Quantum chemistry calculations were first performed to optimize molecular geometries of $\mathrm{TFSI}^{-}$ , $\mathrm{DFOB}^{-}$ anion, PFPN, DME, and FEC solvent molecules using the Gaussian 16 package at B3LYP/6-311+G(d) level of theory. The atomic partial charges on these solvent molecules were computed by fitting to the molecular electrostatic potential at atomic centers. The atomistic force field parameters for all ions and molecules are described by the AMBER format and are taken from previous work.[63] Two modeling systems were constructed to mimic real experiments. Atomistic simulations of this modeling system were performed using GROMACS package with cubic periodic boundary conditions. The equations for the motion of all atoms were integrated using a classic Verlet leapfrog integration algorithm with a time step of 1.0 fs. Atomistic simulations were further performed in a canonical ensemble (NVT) for 50 ns, and simulation trajectories were recorded at an interval of 100 fs for further structural and dynamical analysis. Furthermore, representative solvation structures were extracted from atomistic simulation trajectories, and these solvation structures are then used as starting configurations for subsequent density functional theory (DFT) calculations. DFT calculations were first performed using the Gaussian 16 software at the same level of theory (B3LYP/6-311+G(d)) to optimize solvation structures and, thereafter, to calculate the specific binding energies and molecular electrostatic potential contours of these representative solvation structures.
