ABSTRACT: Integrating silicon (Si) nanoparticles with carbonous materials has been regarded as an effective strategy to suppress the serious volume expansion and poor electrical conductivity of the Si anode in lithium-ion batteries (LIBs). Herein, the Si/C composite coated with a S,N-codoped graphene layer (Si@SNG) has been prepared via a facile and easily scale-up strategy that combines the vertical fluidized-bed-derived chemical vapor deposition in which the lightweight waste tire pyrolysis oil with high S content and pyridine is utilized as carbon source and dopants. The introduction of S,N-codoped graphene layer and graphite matrix can effectively increase $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ storage room and conductivity, while buffering the volume expansion of pure Si. As result, the Si@SNG anode delivers a high initial Coulombic efficiency (ICE) of $86.91\%$ , large specific capacity of 2446

$\mathrm{mAh~g^{-1}}$ at $0.3\mathrm{A g^{-1}}$ and $90.3\%$ capacity retention rate for 600 cycles within $0.01 - 1\mathrm{V}$ . In addition, the Si@SNG@G-P composite containing Si@SNG (10 wt %) and graphite matrix (90 wt %) has been prepared by ball milling and liquid-phase coating. As the anode, Si@SNG@G-P exhibits a high specific capacity of $695\mathrm{mAh~g^{-1}}$ at $0.05\mathrm{A g^{-1}}$ coupled with $94.6\%$ capacity retention rate for 500 cycles within $0.01 - 2\mathrm{V}$ . Moreover, the full cell assembled by Si@SNG@G-P anode and $\mathrm{LiFePO_4}$ cathode shows a high specific capacity of 157.7 and $128\mathrm{mAh~g^{-1}}$ at 0.2 and 4 C, $93.1\%$ capacity retention for 200 cycles along with a high energy density of 257.3 Wh $\mathrm{kg}^{-1}$ . Therefore, this work offers a low-cost and high-efficiency preparation strategy for obtaining Si-based materials with outstanding electrochemical performance in LIBs.

KEYWORDS: Lithium-ion battery, Si anode, Waste tire pyrolysis oil, S,N codoped graphene layer, Graphite matrix

# 1. INTRODUCTION

With the increasing demand for high energy density energy storage devices, the current practical application of lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) is lagging far behind the actual demand. For example, the current low energy density of LIBs is insufficient to support the development of automotive power batteries. Currently, silicon (Si) has attracted much attention and is considered to be one of the most likely candidates for high-energy-density LIBs due to its excellent theoretical specific capacity $(4200\mathrm{mAhg}^{-1})$ , which is 11 times higher than that of commercial graphite anode $(372\mathrm{mAhg}^{-1})$ . In addition, Si owns abundant resources on the Earth and a suitable operating potential $(\sim 0.4\mathrm{V})$ , which ensures a high energy output in a full cell system and is convenient for industrial production. However, the intrinsic poor electrical conductivity and huge volume expansion of Si anode will seriously slow Li-ion transfer rate and damage the electrode structure, thereby resulting in capacity degradation rapidly during a continuous lithiation and delithiation process in LIBs. To well overcome the problem, the integration of Si and various carbonous materials such as amorphous carbon, graphene, and graphite

has been recognized as the most effective strategy for improving the overall electrical conductivity, alleviating the huge volume expansion and preventing the recurrent formation of the solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) film, thereby greatly enhancing the electrochemical performance in LIBs.6 Additionally, the physicochemical properties of the carbon coating layer on Si nanoparticles can be further functionally regulated by heteroatom doping strategy including N, S, and O dopings.7-9 This can be ascribed to the fact that the introduction of these heteroatoms will bring more active sites and enhance electrical conductivity, which are favorable for ionic/electronic transfer rates.10,11 At present, the presence of multitudinous automobiles has led to the generation of numerous waste tires, commonly referred to as "black

Thus, the ecological recycling of waste tires has become an important issue in today's society. $^{14}$ To deal with the waste tires, the main treatment methods have been employed including tire retreading, rubber powder making, thermal energy use, and pyrolysis. $^{15-17}$ Among them, pyrolysis has been employed as the mainstream method that can effectively decompose all the waste tires, and the cracking products of waste tire pyrolysis oil (WTPO) can be further used and achieve the regeneration of resources. $^{18}$ In addition, WTPO owning abundant aromatic compounds, high S content, and low thermal stability cannot be burned directly or used as a fuel. Therefore, WTPO must be further processed and purified before it can be fully used in high-quality chemicals. $^{19,20}$ However, the high cost and low yield of processing WTPO make it difficult to further develop the application in the new storage energy field. It has been recognized that compounds with benzene ring structures can be converted into graphene through dehydrogenation and rearrangement processes in a chemical vapor deposition (CVD) reactor. $^{21}$ In view of WTPO light fraction mainly contains abundant low cyclic aromatic hydrocarbons such as styrene and naphthalene, it is suitable for preparing graphene as a carbon precursor. $^{22}$ Therefore, S,N-codoped graphene-coated Si/C composite (Si@SNG) was prepared in a fluidized-bed reactor by using WTPO and pyridine as carbon and dopants. In the fluidized-bed reactor ensuring efficient mass and heat transfer, the graphene coating layer can be homogeneously formed and deposited on the Si surface via the cracking reaction of WTPO and pyridine at high temperatures, while S and N atoms are uniformly doped into the graphene frameworks. As an anode, Si@SNG exhibits an ICE value of $86.91\%$ , coupled with a $90.3\%$ capacity retention rate for 600 cycles. To better meet the commercial application requirements of the Si anode as far as possible, the Si@SNG@G-P composite containing Si@SNG (10 wt %) and graphite (90 wt %) matrix has been prepared by ball milling and a liquid-phase coating method. As the anode, Si@SNG@G-P displays an ICE value of $82.3\%$ and a large specific capacity of $695\mathrm{mAhg^{-1}}$ at $0.05\mathrm{A g^{-1}}$ within $0.01 - 2\mathrm{V}$ , as well as a $94.6\%$ capacity retention rate for 500 cycles. To further validate the commercial application value of the prepared anode material, Si@SNG@G-P is used as the anode, and commercial $\mathrm{LiFePO_4}$ (LFP) is used as the cathode to assemble the full cell. The full cell exhibited a high specific capacity of $128\mathrm{mAhg^{-1}}$ at $4\mathrm{C}$ , $93.1\%$ capacity retention for 200 cycles coupled with a high maximal energy density of $257.3\mathrm{Whkg^{-1}}$ , showing an excellent electrochemical property in LIBs. Thus, this work provides a simple and easily scaled-up preparation strategy for Si-based material with high performance in the SIBs system.

# 2. EXPERIMENTAL SECTION

2.1. Preparation of Si@SNG Composite. First, the S-doped Si/ C composite (Si@SG) was prepared in a vertical fluidized-bed reactor under an Ar atmosphere using a facile CVD method. In which, the lightweight WTPO (S content of $>10^{3}\mathrm{ppm}$ ) was utilized as a carbon source and the commercial Si nanoparticles $(9\mathrm{g})$ as raw material. Then, the reactor containing $100~\mathrm{mL}$ of lightweight WTPO was

heated and vaporized in an oil bath at $120^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ . Subsequently, both the vaporized oil gas and inert Ar gas at a rate of $0.6\mathrm{L}\mathrm{min}^{-1}$ were introduced into the heated CVD reactor at $800^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ , and the mixture was allowed to react for 10 min. After that, the black Si@SG powder was obtained. Moreover, the S,N-codoped Si/C composite (Si@ SNG) was prepared by a similar process to Si@SG. Briefly, a reactor containing $100~\mathrm{mL}$ of lightweight WTPO and $30~\mathrm{mL}$ of pyridine was heated in an oil bath at $120^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ , and then the vaporized oil gas and Ar with $0.6\mathrm{L}\mathrm{min}^{-1}$ were introduced into the heated vertical furnace reactor containing $9\mathrm{g}$ of commercial Si nanoparticles and reacted for 10 min at $800^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ . After reaction, the final Si@SNG composite was obtained.

2.2. Preparation of Si@SNG@Graphite Composite. The as-prepared Si@SG $(9\mathrm{g})$ or Si@SNG composite $(9\mathrm{g})$ was homogeneously mixed with commercial graphite $(1\mathrm{g})$ by using a ball-mill device at a speed of $600~\mathrm{rpm}$ for $2\mathrm{h}$ , which was noted as Si@ SG@G and Si@SNG@G, respectively. Subsequently, the pitch powder $(1\mathrm{g})$ with a high soft temperature $(280^{\circ}\mathrm{C})$ was respectively dispersed into two beakers filled with $300~\mathrm{mL}$ tetrahydrofuran (THF) solution in a fuming cupboard with the assistance of high-speed disperser at a stirring rate of $1000~\mathrm{rpm}$ , which was named as $\mathrm{A}_1$ and $\mathrm{A}_2$ solution, respectively. After being stirred for $20\mathrm{min}$ , the obtained Si@ SG@G and Si@SNG@G composites were, respectively, added into $\mathrm{A}_1$ and $\mathrm{A}_2$ solution and stirred for $4\mathrm{h}$ to obtain the mixture of Si@C and commercial graphite. Finally, the as-prepared mixtures were calcinated at $900^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ for $2\mathrm{h}$ with a heating rate of $10^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\mathrm{min}^{-1}$ in an Ar atmosphere, and the final products were denoted as Si@SG@G-P and Si@SNG@G-P, respectively.

2.3. Material Characterizations. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (Gemini, Model SEM-300) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (FEI, Model F20) were employed to characterize the detailed morphology features and microstructures of these as-prepared Si-based composites. Their lattice structure was examined by X-ray diffraction (XRD) (Empyrean). The related carbon content of these Si/C composites was also tested by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) (Shimadzu) in an $\mathrm{O}_2$ atmosphere. The detailed defect information on Si/C composites was performed on Raman analyzer (LabRAM, Model HR800). The composition of individual elements for these Si/C samples was analyzed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (PHI, Model Quantera). Additionally, the corresponding water-contact angle testing was performed on an optical contact angle and an interfacial tension meter (Model SL200 KS).

2.4. Electrochemical Measurements. Active material, lithiated poly(acrylic acid) (Li-PAA) binder, and conductive carbon black were mixed with each other in the ratio of 90:5:5, along with a moderate amount of deionized water and anhydrous ethanol and stirred for $4\mathrm{h}$ to form a homogeneous viscous slurry. After that, the as-prepared slurry was evenly coated on the current collector of copper foil with a spatula and dried in an oven at $80^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ for $10\mathrm{h}$ . Finally, the as-dried copper foil coated with different active materials was cut into electrode wafers with a diameter of $13\mathrm{mm}$ by a manual slicer, and the mass loading was controlled at $2.5\mathrm{mgcm}^{-2}$ . Subsequently, the CR2032 type half-cell was assembled in an Ar-filled glovebox $(\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O} < 0.1\mathrm{ppm},\mathrm{O}_2 < 0.1\mathrm{ppm})$ where the metal Li foil was utilized as the counter electrode, the as-prepared disk was utilized as the working electrode, and Celgard 2400 polypropylene was utilized as the membrane. The electrolyte used was $1\mathrm{M}$ lithium hexafluorophosphate $\left(\mathrm{LiPF}_6\right)$ dissolved in ethylene carbonate, diethyl carbonate, and methylene carbonate in a 1:1:1 volume ratio coupled with $5\mathrm{wt}\%$ fluorosubstituted ethylene carbonate. Whereafter, the detailed electrochemical properties including specific capacity, rate performance, and cycling stability for these as-assembled half-cells were performed on the NEWARE battery testing system. Additionally, both the electrochemical impedance within $0.01 - 100\mathrm{kHz}$ and cyclic voltammetry (CV) profiles ranging from 0.1 to $0.5\mathrm{mVs}^{-1}$ were tested on the electrochemical workstation (Model CS2350H). Moreover, the button full cells were fabricated by using the prelithiated Si@ SNG@graphite as an anode and the commercial LFP as a cathode, and the detailed electrochemical performances were tested on the

electrochemical workstation within $2.0 - 4.2\mathrm{V}$ . The specific capacity of the electrode is based on the total weight of the active material (Si/ C) in the electrode. The electrode is composed of active material (90 wt %), binder (5 wt %), and conductive carbon black (5 wt %).

# 3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

WTPO, derived from the pyrolysis of waste tires, contains a complex mixture of organic compounds, primarily composed of aromatic hydrocarbons, such as styrene and naphthalene. The choice of WTPO not only supports the recycling of waste materials but also leverages the high S content intrinsic to tire composition. The composition information on the WTPO is shown in Table S1. Styrene and naphthalene are the main components of WTPO, accounting for 38.70 and 31.37 wt %, respectively. Due to their unique molecular structure (monocyclic and bicyclic aromatic hydrocarbons), the dehydrogenated product is the basic structural unit of graphene. Therefore, the above two components are ideal components for the growth of graphene. Because WTPO is generally derived from an industrial thermal cracking device, some metal impurities may be introduced into WTPO. The presence of metal impurities (e.g., Fe, Ni, Cu, etc.) in WTPO can affect the electrical conductivity and structural integrity of the graphene layer, leading to a degradation of the overall electrochemical performance of the cell. The inductively coupled plasma (ICP) detection was performed to distinguish whether the metal content can meet the standard (GB/T 37386-2019). To detect the metal content in the carbon coating layers, Si@SNG was immersed in a NaOH aqueous solution for $24\mathrm{h}$ to dissolve the Si cores. Then, the final graphene was obtained, followed by inductively coupled plasma (ICP) analysis for detecting the trace-metal contents in the grown graphene layer. As shown in Table S2, the metal elements including Cu, Fe, Na, Ni, and Pb in the graphene layer are at ultralow levels. Most of the metal content is $<1$ ppm, and the maximum content of elemental iron is only 1.199 ppm. These results demonstrate that various metal contents have met the demand of the standard, and thus, the influence of residual metal impurities on the electrode property can be ignored. The whole preparation process of Si@SNG sample is displayed in Figure 1a, in which the lightweight WTPO utilized as carbon source was gradually vaporized into oil gas at 120 °C, and adsorbed on the surface of commercial Si nanoparticles in the vertical fluidized-bed reactor with the assistance of Ar gas. Subsequently, the aromatic compounds in WTPO were further dehydrogenated and rearranged into graphene structures under the catalytic effect of the inherent $\mathrm{SiO}_x$ layer on the surface of commercial Si particles. Meanwhile, the introduced pyridine gas was also spilled into moderate $\mathrm{NH}_3$ gas and then reacted with the as-formed graphene on the Si particles to generate a N-doped graphene coating layer. It can be clearly seen that both the Si@SG (Figure 1b) and Si@SNG samples (Figure 1e) maintained a similar nanospherical morphology ( $\sim50\mathrm{nm}$ ) compared to that of pure Si (Figure S1) particles after the CVD reaction, indicating that the graphene formation and N doping process did not change the intrinsic morphological structure of the commercial Si nanoparticles. From the high-resolution TEM images of Si@ SG (Figure 1c), it can be obviously observed that the fluffy graphene layer homogeneously covered the surface of pure Si nanoparticles. The close-up view distinctly reveals the layered structure of graphene, which has a thickness of $\sim2.5\mathrm{nm}$ (consisting of $\sim4$ layers). Moreover, in our previous work,[23]

In other words, the Si crystal structures in the Si@SG and Si@SNG composites have not been damaged during the CVD process. This can be mainly ascribed to the fact that the Si nanoparticles have been well embedded into the flexible graphene coating layer with a high mechanical strength. It is well recognized that the graphene coating layer is greatly conducive to maintaining the structural integrity and stability of the commercial Si nanoparticles, thereby effectively buffering serious volume expansion of Si electrodes during the continuous lithiation and delithiation process in LIBs.[28] To determine the C content in the as-prepared Si/C composites prepared by the CVD technique, the corresponding TGA testing in an $\mathrm{O}_2$ environment within a heating temperature range of $0 - 900^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ was carried out. The N 1s spectra of Si@SNG can be divided into four peaks (Figure 2i) concluding the pyridine N (398.8 eV), pyrrole N (400.5 eV), graphite N (401.8 eV), and oxide N (403.8 eV).<sup>38</sup> All of the original peak spectra of S 2p and N 1s mentioned above are provided in Figure S3a-S3c. Both the fitted C 1s and N 1s spectra of Si@SNG indicate that the N atoms have been successfully doped into the graphene framework during the CVD process. Additionally, the peak intensity of pyridinic N is higher than that of the other three components, showing that the main existing form of N atoms in the Si@SNG sample was dominated by pyridine N, which is more favorable for enhancing the electronic conductivity and, thus, accelerating the charge transfer speed in LIBs compared to the pyrrolic N and graphitic N.<sup>39</sup> In addition, the pyridinic N is also

electrochemically active as a lone pair of electrons, which can be easily conjugated to the carbon $\pi$ -bonds. As for the pyrrolic N, it can effectively improve the electronic transfer speed of the electrode materials. Therefore, the doped N and S atoms can effectively provide substantial $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ storage room and active sites, meanwhile accelerating the ionic/electronic transport speed, thereby enhancing the electrochemical performance in LIBs.[40]

The CV curves of the Si@SG anode in the first three cycles at a scan rate of $0.1\mathrm{mV}\mathrm{s}^{-1}$ (Figure 3a) show redox reaction peak positions similar to those of Si@SNG (Figure 3b), indicating that it possesses a similar and comparable Li-ion storage mechanism in LIBs. Apparently, a broad reduction peak appears at $\sim 0.7\mathrm{V}$ in the first cycle, which is attributed to the formation of SEI film. As the cycling progresses, this broad reduction peak is completely diminished, indicating the formation of a stable SEI film in the subsequent cycles.[41] Besides, a new reduction peak at $\sim 0.22\mathrm{V}$ can be observed in the discharge process, which is ascribed to the alloying reaction of the Si crystals.[42] Obviously, two peaks that emerged at $\sim 0.4$ and $0.6\mathrm{V}$ can easily be found in the charge process, which can be attributed to the delithiation reaction of $\mathrm{Li}_x\mathrm{Si}$ alloy. It is noteworthy that the redox peak intensity is gradually reinforced as the cycle increases due to the step-by-step activation process of the Si electrode.[43,44] Both the charge and discharge curves of Si@SG and Si@SNG electrodes at $0.3\mathrm{A}\mathrm{g}^{-1}$ in the first cycle (Figure 3c) deliver two obvious voltage plateaus at $\sim 0.1$ and $0.4\mathrm{V}$ , which corresponds to the typical lithiation and delithiation reaction process of Si-based anode, respectively.[22] Meanwhile, the corresponding charge and discharge specific capacity of Si@SNG anode in the first cycle is as high as 2620 and $3014.5\mathrm{mAh}\mathrm{g}^{-1}$ within $0.01 - 1\mathrm{V}$ , respectively, corresponding to a high initial Coulombic efficiency (ICE) of $86.91\%$ , which is significantly higher than that of pure Si ( $73\%$ , see Figures S4a and S4b). Besides, the charge and discharge specific capacity of the Si@SG anode is 2432 and $2835.5\mathrm{mAh}\mathrm{g}^{-1}$ , respectively, with a high ICE value of $85.77\%$ , which is slightly lower than that of the Si@SNG anode but much higher than that of the pure Si anode. The above results indicate that the introduced S and N doped atoms into the graphene coating layer are greatly conducive to reducing the defect structures of Si nanoparticles and generating a stable SEI film, thereby lowering the irreversible Li-ion consumption derived from SEI film formation.[45,46] The rate performances of Si@SG and Si@SNG electrodes at various current densities ranging from 0.3 to $2\mathrm{A}\mathrm{g}^{-1}$ also have been tested at a voltage range of $0.01 - 1\mathrm{V}$ (Figure 3d). Obviously, the average specific capacities of Si@SNG at various current densities are higher than that of Si@SG, indicating that the S,N-codoped synergistic effect can greatly enhance the Li-ion storage capability. The corresponding average specific capacities of Si@SNG are 2446, 1859, 1479, 1344, and $1068\mathrm{mAh}\mathrm{g}^{-1}$ at 0.3, 0.5, 0.8, 1, and $2\mathrm{A}\mathrm{g}^{-1}$ , respectively, with $43.7\%$ capacity retention rate, showing an excellent rate performance. When the current density was recovered to $1\mathrm{A}\mathrm{g}^{-1}$ , the average specific capacity of Si@SNG was still maintained at $1475\mathrm{mAh}\mathrm{g}^{-1}$ , which is higher than that of Si@SG ( $1248\mathrm{mAh}\mathrm{g}^{-1}$ ), showing an outstanding capacity reversibility. The enhanced performance of the Si@SNG anode compared to the Si@SG anode can be attributed to several synergistic effects that contribute to the better buffering of volume expansion and improved capacity in the following ways:

(1) Structural stability and defect engineering: Both S and N doping introduce controlled defects into the graphene lattice. N doping typically creates pyridinic, pyrrolic, and graphitic nitrogen configurations, each contributing differently to the electronic structure and chemical reactivity of the graphene. S doping modifies the electronic properties and local chemical reactivity. The copresence of S and N in graphene not only modifies the electronic structure more significantly than either dopant alone but also creates a more disordered structure that can accommodate the volume changes of Si during lithiation and delithiation processes.

(2) Mechanical buffering: The intrinsic mechanical properties of the graphene coating are altered by the codoping of S and N. This modified graphene layer can provide better mechanical support for the Si, cushioning its drastic volume changes during cycling and reducing the risk of electrode delamination or particle pulverization.

The phenomenon that the Si@SNG anode has a higher capacity is ascribed to the following reasons:

(1) Improved $\mathsf{Li}^{+}$ accessibility: The defects and dopants in the graphene structure can act as active sites for $\mathsf{Li}^{+}$ storage, effectively increasing the specific capacity of the electrode. The presence of both S and N increases the density of such active sites compared with doping with S alone. These heteroatoms alter the electronic environment of adjacent C atoms, potentially enhancing $\mathsf{Li}^{+}$ binding and diffusion at the electrode surface.

(2) Electrochemical kinetics: The combined effects of S and N doping may also improve the kinetics of $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ insertion and extraction. N doping can enhance the electron transfer rate, while S doping might contribute to a more favorable interface for $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ transport, thus, collectively improving the overall capacity of the anode.

The observed behavior in the rate performance of both Si@ SG and Si@SNG anodes, where the capacity initially declines more rapidly at lower current densities (0.3, 0.5, and $0.8\mathrm{Ag}^{-1}$ ) and then stabilizes or declines more slowly at higher current densities (1 and $2\mathrm{Ag}^{-1}$ ), can be attributed to several interrelated factors:

(1) Kinetics of $Li^{+}$ diffusion: At lower current densities, the $Li^{+}$ has more time to diffuse into the anode material. However, the observed rapid decline suggests that even at these slower rates, the diffusion might not be fully keeping up with the demand, especially during the structural changes and the development of SEI layer. The SEI layer can initially hinder ionic transport but may become more stable and ionically conductive over time.   
(2) Structural adjustments and electrode conditioning: The initial cycles at lower current densities might involve some conditioning of the electrode, where the anode materials adjust structurally to the insertion and extraction of $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ . This structural adjustment can initially lead to higher capacity losses. Once the electrode materials are conditioned and partially stabilized, they might exhibit more stable performance, even when subjected to higher rates.   
(3) SEI formation dynamics: The initial decline at lower rates could also be linked to the dynamics of SEI formation. The SEI layer, which forms during the initial cycles primarily at lower rates, can initially act as a barrier to

$\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ transport. As the current density increases, the effect of the SEI layer might become less pronounced relative to the total capacity, and the material might exhibit a kind of "activation" behavior where the accessible capacity stabilizes or even improves due to more complete utilization of the anode material.

(4) Electrode material utilization: At higher rates, the faster charge and discharge cycles may limit the depth of the $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ penetration into the anode material. This limitation can paradoxically lead to a more stable capacity if the material suffers from issues like fracturing or delamination at deeper discharge levels, which are more pronounced at lower rates where the $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ has the opportunity to interact more deeply and expansively with the electrode material.

(5) Rate capability of the doping elements: The presence of S and N dopants in the Si@SNG anode could enhance its electrical conductivity and structural resilience compared to that of Si@SG, potentially allowing it to better accommodate higher rates of $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ insertion and extraction.

Although we are very eager to supply the experiment of graphene layers without S atoms to demonstrate the role of S in graphene layers, it is very difficult to remove S from WTPO to obtain the WTPO without elemental sulfur. The hydrodesulfurization is the good strategy to remove elemental sulfur. However, due to the existence of unsaturated hydrocarbon molecules in WTPO, these unsaturated hydrocarbons will be saturated by the hydrodesulfurization process. Then the component of the hydrotreated WTPO presents the distinct contrast with the initial WTPO, meaning that the difference between the treated WTPO and the initial WTPO is not only in the absence of sulfur, but also the raw material composition also has changed. Thus, the experiment with the single-factor variation cannot be guaranteed. Therefore, due to the intrinsic S containing property of WTPO, it is difficult to obtain WTPO without elemental sulfur under the condition of not causing any changes in other components. Although the pure sulfur doping is not realized, the similar enhancements of physical and electrochemical properties contributed by S doping were revealed in previous efforts. The role of pure S doping in graphene layers and the synergistic effect of S and N codoping have been reported in some literatures, including the enhancement of electrical conductivity and the introduction of more structural defects, as well as the enhancement of electrochemical properties.[47-51] Based on the relevant conclusions of the previous studies, the role of S doping possesses a positive contribution to the improvement of electrode performance. Therefore, an inexpensive and available sulfur-containing WTPO serving as both carbon source and in situ S dopant to synthesize Si@C electrode material is one of the characteristics of this work. The difference in cycling performance, as observed in Figure 3e between pure Si and the Si@SG and Si@SNG electrodes, can be attributed to several factors related to the structural and chemical modifications imparted by the doping process:

(1) SEI layer formation and stability: One of the most significant challenges with Si anodes is the formation of the SEI layer during the initial cycles. For the pure Si electrode, the SEI layer can be unstable and continuously reform, because of the substantial volume changes (up to $300\%$ ) during lithiation and delithiation processes. This continuous breaking and reforming of the SEI layer consumes the electrolyte and active lithium, leading to a steady capacity fade. In contrast, the S and N codoping atoms in the Si@SG and Si@SNG composites are conductive to stabilizing the SEI film layer. The defects introduced by dopants can activate decomposition of the electrolyte and promote formation of the SEI layer. In addition, the functional groups introduced by the dopants can firmly bind to the SEI components, thereby contributing to the formation of a more stable and less permeable SEI layer. This stabilization can reduce the rate of electrolyte decomposition and $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ consumption, resulting in improved cycling stability.   
(2) Buffering of volume expansion: The graphene matrix in the Si@SG and Si@SNG composites serves to buffer the volume changes experienced by the Si particles. This buffering reduces the mechanical stress on the electrode, helping to maintain electrical connectivity and structural integrity over many cycles. The initial increase in capacity observed in these composites may be due to the activation of additional active material that was not fully utilized in the initial cycles or gradual improvements in the electrode's ionic conductivity as the structure settles.   
(3) Enhanced electrical conductivity: The incorporation of conductive graphene and doping elements, such as S and N, also improves the overall electrical conductivity of the electrode. This enhancement allows for more-efficient electron transport throughout the electrode material, enabling better utilization of the Si even in deeper charge/discharge states. This improvement in electrical conductivity can manifest as a slight increase in capacity over the initial cycles as the electrode materials become better conditioned for the cycling process.   
(4) Electrochemical kinetics: The doped and composite structures likely alter the electrochemical kinetics of the electrode, enhancing $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ diffusion rates and the accessibility of active sites. These changes are beneficial for a more complete and efficient intercalation and deintercalation process, which might explain the slight increase in capacity during the initial 200 cycles.

The cycling performances (Figure 6e) show that the initial and final specific capacity of Si@SNG@G-P are 408 and $386\mathrm{mAhg}^{-1}$ at $0.2\mathrm{A}\mathrm{g}^{-1}$ , corresponding to a $94.6\%$ capacity retention rate for 500 cycles, which is slightly lower than that of commercial graphite $(95.7\%)$ , showing an excellent cycling stability. As for the Si@ SG@G-P anode, the corresponding initial specific capacity is $384\mathrm{mAhg}^{-1}$ and finally reaches $355\mathrm{mAhg}^{-1}$ , with a capacity retention rate of $92.4\%$ , also delivering an outstanding cycling performance. The as-obtained excellent cycling performance for the Si@SG@G-P and Si@SNG@G-P electrodes can be ascribed to the fact that the graphite matrix and S,N-codoped

graphene coating layer can greatly enhance the electrical conductivity and buffer the volume expansion, as well as accelerate the ionic/electronic transfer rates, thereby improving the long-term cycling stability for the electrode in LIBs.

The LFP cathode performance is shown in Figure S8, which exhibits excellent rate capability and cycling stability. To evaluate the commercial application prospects for these as-prepared Si-based composites well, the full cells were rationally assembled by using the commercial LFP as cathode and the Si@SG@G-P or Si@SNG@G-P as anode, and the corresponding full cell was labeled as LFP//Si@SG@G-P and LFP//Si@ SNG@G-P, respectively. As exhibited in Figure 7a, the average specific capacities of LFP//Si@SNG@G-P full cell at 0.2, 0.5, 1, 2, 3, and 4 C are 157.7, 150.5, 144.3, 137, 131.3, and 128 mAh g $^{-1}$ , respectively. Even at a higher current density of 5 C, it still exhibits a large average specific capacity of $123.7 \mathrm{mAh} \mathrm{g}^{-1}$ , corresponding to a capacity retention rate of $78.4\%$ , compared to the initial specific capacity at $0.2 \mathrm{~A} \mathrm{~g}^{-1}$ , showing excellent rate performance. When the current density was recovered to 1 C, the average specific capacity is as high as $142.3 \mathrm{mAh} \mathrm{g}^{-1}$ , delivering outstanding capacity reversibility. As for the LFP//Si@SG@G-P full cell, the corresponding average specific capacities within $0.2-5 \mathrm{C}$ are 152.6, 147.8, 142.6, 135.4, 129.1, 124.3, and $119.7 \mathrm{mAh} \mathrm{g}^{-1}$ , respectively, which are lower than that of the LFP//Si@SNG@G-P full cell. The charge and discharge curves of LFP//Si@SG@G-P and LFP// Si@SNG@G-P full cells at different current densities are also recorded. Apparently, two long and flat voltage platforms within $3.0-3.5 \mathrm{~V}$ can be clearly observed in the LFP//Si@ SNG@G-P (Figure 7b) and LFP//Si@SG@G-P full cells (Figure 7c), showing that they own the stable voltage plateaus and low electrochemical polarization at various current densities ranging from 0.2 to $5 \mathrm{C}$ . Moreover, the detailed energy densities and power densities for these as-assembled full cells can be calculated by the following equations:

$$
P = \frac {\Delta V \times I}{m} \tag {3}
$$

$$
\Delta V = \frac {V _ {\max} + V _ {\min}}{2} \tag {4}
$$

$$
E = \frac {P \times t}{3 6 0 0} \tag {5}
$$

where $I$ is the discharge current, $\Delta V$ is the average value between the maximal charge voltage ( $V_{\mathrm{max}}$ ) and minimal discharge voltage ( $V_{\mathrm{min}}$ ), and $t$ is the discharge time. After calculation, the maximal energy density of LFP//Si@SNG@G-P (Figure 7d) is as high as 257.3 Wh kg $^{-1}$ , which is higher than that of LFP//Si@SG@G-P (215 Wh kg $^{-1}$ ) at 0.2 C. Even at a higher current density of 5 C, the LFP//Si@SNG@G-P full cell still gains a large energy density of 201.8 Wh kg $^{-1}$ , which is much higher than that of LFP//Si@SG@G-P (168.6 Wh kg $^{-1}$ ), showing promising application potential in LIBs. This also proves that the LFP//Si@SNG@G-P full cell has a superior range and usage time. Additionally, the cycling performance of LFP//Si@SNG@G-P full cells at 1 C for 200 cycles (Figure 7e) shows that it has large initial and final specific capacities of 143.4 and 133.5 mAh g $^{-1}$ , respectively, corresponding to a 93.1% capacity retention rate, delivering an excellent long-term cycling stability. Meanwhile, the LFP//Si@ SG@G-P full cell exhibits a high specific capacity of 140.8 mAh g $^{-1}$ , and finally reaches up to 127.8 mAh g $^{-1}$ , corresponding to

capacity retention rates of $90.7\%$ , which is slightly lower than that of LFP//Si@SG@G-P, but also shows an outstanding cycling performance. The large average specific capacity, good rate performance, and excellent cycling stability for the LFP// Si@SG@G-P and LFP//Si@sNG@G-P full cell further prove that the graphene coating layer doped with heteroatoms and graphite matrix can effectively improve the electrical conductivity and maintain the structural stability, as well as suppress the huge volume expansion change of the pure Si electrode.

# 4. CONCLUSIONS

In summary, the Si@SNG@G-P composite was prepared by a facile, high-efficiency, and easily scaled-up strategy that combined the FB-CVD and ball milling, in which lightweight WTPO and pyridine were used as the carbon source and dopant and graphite were used as the matrix. As the anode, the Si@SNG anode exhibits a high ICE value of $86.91\%$ and a capacity retention rate of $90.3\%$ for 600 cycles. Additionally, the Si@SNG@G-P composite shows a high ICE value of $82.3\%$ , large specific capacity of $695\mathrm{mAhg^{-1}}$ at $0.05\mathrm{A g^{-1}}$ within $0.01 - 2\mathrm{V}$ , along with a capacity retention rate of $94.6\%$ for 500 cycles. Furthermore, the assembled full cell of LFP// Si@SNG@G-P displays a high specific capacity of $128\mathrm{mAhg^{-1}}$ at $4\mathrm{C}$ , $93.1\%$ capacity retention for 200 cycles, and a high maximal energy density of $257.3\mathrm{Whkg^{-1}}$ , showing promising application prospects in the LIBs system. The resultant outstanding electrochemical properties can be attributed to the fact that S,N-codoped graphene coating layer on Si nanoparticles and graphite matrix can greatly improve electrical conductivity and structural stability of the Si electrode. Therefore, this work will pave the way for preparing high-performance Si-based anode materials.
