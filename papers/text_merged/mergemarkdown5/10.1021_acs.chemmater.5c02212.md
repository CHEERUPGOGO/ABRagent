ABSTRACT: The tortuosity factor of composite cathodes significantly affects the rate performance of all-solid-state batteries (ASSBs) and has significant differences from systems with liquid electrolytes. In this work, we report a simulation-experiment combined approach that quantifies the effective Li-ion transport in an ASSB composite cathode, which links tortuosity factor on $\sim \mu \mathrm{m}$ scale to terminal voltage during cycling at the cell level (on $\sim$ cm scale). Two independent approaches of tortuosity factor quantification are considered: fitting electrochemical cycling data and verifying at different cycling rates and calculating from segmented tomography images, with the tortuosity factor quantified from both methods reaching self-consistency. The simulated terminal voltage using the quantified tortuosity factor

has a small relative error of $< 3\%$ compared to the experimental measurements. We find a significantly reduced value of the Bruggeman exponent of the catholyte phase (1.75), and using shape analysis, we show that rod-shaped catholyte particles play an important role in lowering the tortuosity factor.

All-solid-state batteries (ASSBs) have received considerable attention in recent years due to their potential for high energy density when paired with a Li metal anode and the elimination of flammable liquid electrolyte, which poses safety concerns. Despite numerous advancements, the rate capability remains a key limiting factor for ASSBs, hindering their application in scenarios demanding high power density such as electrified aviation or fast charging for electric vehicles to compete with internal combustion engine vehicles. One of the key factors affecting rate capability is the sluggish Li-ion transport in the composite cathode of ASSBs, and improvements in effective Li-ion transport within the composite cathode can be achieved with improvements in the Li ionic conductivity of the catholyte or lowering the tortuosity factor of the composite cathode such that a higher effective ionic conductivity of cathode is achieved. Over the past decade, incredible progress has been made on the discovery of solid-state Li conductors which significantly improves the ionic conductivity of the catholyte. However, the microstructure of the composite cathode also plays an important role. The Li ion transport path in a composite cathode with the solid catholyte is often longer than that in liquid electrolytes, resulting in higher tortuosity factor and, therefore, a reduced effective ionic conductivity in the composite cathode.

Previously, the effective transport of Li ions in Li-ion batteries with a liquid electrolyte was studied intensively, $^{11-14}$ and it was shown that the morphology of porous electrodes in

Li-ion batteries affects the effective ionic conductivity of the electrodes and thus the rate capability of the batteries. $^{11,12}$ Specifically, Ebner et al. illustrated a method that estimates the tortuosity factor of a porous electrode using tomography images of active material particles based on effective medium theory. $^{13,14}$ Usseglio-Viretta et al. demonstrated different approaches for tortuosity factor quantification. $^{15}$ Reducing the tortuosity factor has been proven to be effective in improving the rate capability of Li-ion batteries. $^{16-19}$ By engineering the morphology of the pores to minimize Li-ion transport distance, studies have reported that lower tortuosity factor results in improved rate capability of Li-ion batteries. $^{16-19}$ For ASSBs, the effect of composite cathode morphology on the effective ionic conductivity in ASSBs has been studied with synthetic cathode morphologies. $^{20,21}$ Specifically, by studying synthetic cathode morphologies consisting of sphere-shaped active material and solid electrolyte (SE) particles, Naik et al. identified the key limiting factor to ASSB cathode performance at different compositions. $^{21}$

Two independent methods are used: Method 1 is to fit the tortuosity factor and the Bruggeman exponent from electrochemical cycling data with a pseudo-2D model of ASSB and then validate cycling data at different current densities. Method 2 is to solve the tortuosity factor and the Bruggeman exponent by solving the steady-state diffusion equation within the catholyte region, segmented from X-ray tomography of the ASSB.

Minnmann et al. demonstrated that decreasing the volume fraction of the catholyte significantly reduces the effective ionic conductivity of the cathode compared to the bulk conductivity of the catholyte.[22] Methods similar to how tortuosity factor is reduced in Li-ion batteries were also reported for ASSB composite cathodes.[23,24]

Modeling tortuosity factor, or equivalently the effective transport properties of the composite material, with effective medium theory has a long history that dates back to 1892 with the Rayleigh formula that describes the effect of inclusions on the effective conductivity of a medium.[25] Later, in 1935, Bruggeman[26] derived differential effective medium approximations,[27,28] which was then generalized as $\tau = e^{-\alpha}$ that relates tortuosity factor $\tau$ with the volume fraction $\epsilon$ of a conductive material (assuming the inclusions are nonconductive) and is referred to as the generalized Bruggeman's relation[13] or Archie's relation.[28] Specifically, when applied to Li-ion batteries, the Bruggeman exponent $1 + \alpha = 1.5$ is often adopted in various Li-ion battery simulations and is a very good approximation for porous electrodes with NMC due to the near-spherical shape of NMC particles.[13] It should be noted that effective medium theories assume the background medium to be conductive,[28] which is a valid assumption for liquid electrolytes that fill the porous electrode and serve as the Li-ion conductive medium. In ASSBs, however, catholyte is added as particles or "inclusions"[28] in the context of effective medium theory, and the basic assumption in the effective medium theory does not hold. While the generalized Bruggeman's relation might still be applied for solid-state systems,[29,30] the value of tortuosity and the generalized Bruggeman exponent are quantified with methods other than that described in Ebner and Wood,[14] thus posing difficulties to tortuosity factor modeling in the ASSB cathode. In summary, despite the significant impact of effective Li-ion transport on the rate capability of ASSBs, there lacks a systematic study that illustrates the effect of tortuosity factor of the cathode on the $\sim \mu \mathrm{m}$ scale on the terminal voltage of ASSBs during cycling on the cell-level scale on the $\sim \mathrm{cm}$ scale.

In this work, we demonstrate a simulation-experiment combined approach for effective Li-ion transport quantification

in the cathode of ASSBs, as summarized in Figure 1. Lying at the heart is the catholyte tortuosity factor, which is quantified with two independent methods, one being fitted from electrochemical cycling data and verified on different cycling rates and the other being calculated from segmented tomography images. We applied the tortuosity factor quantification methods to a Li alloy—LLZTO—NMC811+LTLC $(\mathrm{Li}_{0.388}\mathrm{Ta}_{0.238}\mathrm{La}_{0.475}\mathrm{Cl}_3^8)$ solid-state cell, and the quantified tortuosity factor from the two independent methods reaches self-consistency. It is also found that the tortuosity factor of the composite cathode is reduced due to the presence of rod-shaped catholyte particles. We suggest that controlling the shape of catholyte particles can improve the rate performance of solid-state batteries. By analyzing the Bruggeman exponent of the LTLC phase and its anisotropy obtained from different sources of data, we showed self-consistency of the key conclusions stated above. Also, more accurate determination of kinetics between NMC and LTLC can be achieved with higher-resolution X-ray tomography data if it resolves the contact area between the cathode active materials.

Electrochemical Cycling Cell Assembly and Testing. A lithium (99%, Sigma-Aldrich) alloy anode with 3 vol % of mechanically mixed sodium (99.9%, Fisher Scientific) was made using a method adapted from Mann et al. The anode was attached to a Ta-doped polycrystalline cubic $\mathrm{Li}_{6.6}\mathrm{La}_3\mathrm{Zr}_{1.6}\mathrm{Ta}_{0.4}\mathrm{O}_{12}$ (LLZTO, 99.9%, Toshima Manufacturing Co., Ltd.) SE which was first sanded with 800 grit Si-C (Allied High Tech Products Inc.) and heat-treated for $3\mathrm{h}$ at $500^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ to decrease interfacial impedance. After the anode

was attached, LLZTO was heated to $170^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ under $2.8\mathrm{MPa}$ pressure to improve interfacial contact with the anode and the other (cathode) surface exposed. Free-standing film of the solid-state composite cathode incorporating the synthesized LTLC SE was made using a method adapted from Yao et al. $^{32}$ 30 vol % of the LTLC SE powder was mixed with 0.5 wt % PTFE particles ( $>40~\mu \mathrm{m}$ , Sigma-Aldrich), 3 wt % carbon fibers (vapor grown, high aspect ratio), and NMC811 active material ( $9 - 15~\mu \mathrm{m}$ , MSE Supplies LLC). The mixture was homogenized into a free-standing film within a heated ( $100^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ ) mortar and pestle. Rolling the film with a steel rod reduced film thicknesses to around $140~\mu \mathrm{m}$ , which corresponded to high cathode loadings of approximately $6 - 7\mathrm{mAhcm}^{-2}$ . A punch of the composite

cathode film was attached to an aluminum current collector coated with carbon and then pressed onto LLZTO. The same composite film was used to assemble a micro-CT cell, which will be described in the following sections. The exposed surface of LLZTO after anode attachment was sanded off in the glovebox with 800 grit Si-C sandpaper before the cathode film was attached. 4.3 MPa stack pressure was applied during galvanostatic cycling using a homemade pressure fixture (Figure S4 in Supporting Information) and was carried out within an argon glovebox. A $0.05\mathrm{mA}\mathrm{cm}^{-2}$ current density $(C / 120)$ was applied for the first charge/discharge cycle of the full cell, with a charge capacity of $0.17\mathrm{mAhcm}^{-2}$ and a discharge capacity of $0.09\mathrm{mAhcm}^{-2}$ . However, when the rates moved to lower values at $0.01\mathrm{mA}\mathrm{cm}^{-2}$ $(C / 600)$ and $0.02\mathrm{mA}\mathrm{cm}^{-2}$ $(C / 300)$ , larger discharge capacities of 1.25 and $0.89\mathrm{mAhcm}^{-2}$ were achieved, respectively. Low cathode utilization during charge and discharge may occur because of low SE ionic conductivity.

Micro-CT Cell Fabrication. For the micro-CT measurements, cylindrically shaped cells were constructed with the same anode, solid-state composite cathode, and LLZTO SE materials as the larger-scale full cells described earlier in the Experimental Section. The Li alloy anode $(20\mu \mathrm{m}\times 1\mathrm{mm},H\times D)$ was attached to one face of a LLZTO pellet $(500\mu \mathrm{m}\times 2.5\mathrm{mm},H\times D)$ . The opposite side was affixed with the cathode composite $(140\mu \mathrm{m}\times 1\mathrm{mm},H\times D)$ , which was formulated by mixing NMC, LTLC, and VCGF together with a PTFE binder and was cut from the same film that produced the cathode for the larger-scale electrochemical testing cell. Stainless-steel disks (5 mm × 3 mm, $H\times D$ ) served as current collectors on both ends of the assembled cell. To ensure structural integrity and prevent movement, a plastic Swagelok-type fixture with springs was used to securely hold the cell components in place under 4.12 MPa stack pressure. No electrochemical tests were performed prior to collecting tomography images of the micro-CT cell.

Tomography Measurements. X-ray microtomography experiments were conducted at the BMIT-ID/BM beamline of the Canadian Light Source (CLS) $^{33}$ and the 7-BM beamline at the Advanced Photon Source (APS). A monochromatic X-ray beam with $80\mathrm{keV}$ was utilized and optimized according to the absorption characteristics of the cell materials. At the beginning and end of each scan, 50 flat-field images and 10 dark images were collected, respectively. During $180^{\circ}$ rotation of the sample, 2000 projections were captured with an exposure time of $500~\mathrm{ms}$ per projection. The optical setup featured an indirect detection imaging system, employing a $50~\mu \mathrm{m}$ -thick LuAG:Ce single-crystal scintillator (Crytur) by means of an optical system (Optique Peter) with $4.5\times$ magnification. This visible emission was then magnified using a $5\times$ long-distance optical microscope and recorded with a machine vision camera. Maintaining a consistent sample-to-detector distance of 20 cm across all experiments facilitated an effective pixel size of $1.44\mu \mathrm{m}^2$ obtained by a PCO Edge 4.2bi camera, effectively encompassing the entire sample within a $2.9\times 3.8\mathrm{mm}^2$ field of view. Each scan under these conditions was completed within 30 min. For data reconstruction, TomocuPy software utilizing the gridrec algorithm was employed. Subsequent material segmentation and analysis were performed using Dragonfly software.

# RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Tortuosity Factor Quantification via Fitting to Electrochemical Cycling Data. Two independent approaches are applied to quantify the tortuosity factor of the cathode of the ASSB. In this section, we describe how tortuosity factor is quantified via fitting to electrochemical cycling data and then verified on cycling data with different rates. To model the terminal voltage during cycling, a pseudo2D ASSB model is implemented in open-source software PyBaMM $^{34}$ in order to perform the simulation. Compared with the Li-ion battery P2D model described in Doyle, Fuller and Newman, $^{35}$ the ASSB P2D model reported in this work does not have an electrolyte concentration equation since we assume that migration due to potential is the dominant Li-ion transfer mechanism, and the transference number of solid-state electrolyte is 1. The main control equations of the model are charge conservation equations within the SE and catholyte regions, kinetic equations at the anode separator and the catholyte-cathode active materials interfaces, and mass conservation equations in cathode active materials. The mathematical model details of the model can be found in the Supporting Information. The value of the parameters used in the model are listed in Table S8 in the Supporting Information.

Using the measured terminal voltage of the cell in two cycles, the tortuosity factor $\tau = 3.39$ (equivalent to Bruggeman exponent $1 + \alpha = 1.89$ ) is fitted. Figure 2 shows a comparison between the measured and simulated voltage profile of the same full cell at different current densities. It can be seen that the simulated profile matches the experimental measured voltage profile, indicating that the effective Li-ion transport in the cathode is captured well through the calculated Bruggeman exponent. It is worth mentioning that the current density applied in the cycling shown in Figure 2 is small, such that the contact area between the anode and LLZTO is unchanged, as assumed in the pseudo-2D ASSB model. Note that for the exchange current density prefactor between NMC and the electrolyte $(j_{00})$ , Chen et al. $^{36}$ reported $j_{00} = 3.42 \times 10^{-6} \mathrm{~A~m}^{-2} \cdot (\mathrm{m}^{3} / \mathrm{mol})^{1.5}$ , an $\sim 1000$ -times larger $j_{00}$ between NMC and the liquid electrolyte in a Li-ion battery compared with the $j_{00}$ fitted in this work $(j_{00} = 4.11 \times 10^{-9} \mathrm{~A~m}^{-2} \cdot (\mathrm{m}^{3} / \mathrm{mol})^{1.5}$ ; see Table S8 in the Supporting Information). Aside from the difference of the electrolyte that gives different kinetics at solid-solid and solid-liquid interfaces, the order of magnitude difference might also be caused by the fact that in Li-ion batteries, the full surface area of NMC particles is in contact with the liquid electrolyte, while in solid-state batteries, only a small portion of the NMC surface area is in contact with LTLC, causing a small "effective" exchange current density. As the contact area $a$ and current density $j$ are multiplied with each other in the control equation $\nabla \cdot i_{s,c} = -aj$ that describes the current distribution in the full-cell model (see the Supporting Information for details on the full-cell model), it is only possible to determine the value of the product, i.e., $aj$ . Therefore, the fitted $j_{00}$ contains the effect of the contact area between NMC and the electrolyte. It is worth mentioning that the Bruggeman exponent $1 + \alpha$ is quantified to be 1.89, which is smaller than the value in previous literature $^{30,37,38}$ and comparable to those in Li-ion batteries with liquid electrolyte $1 + \alpha = 1.5$ . $^{13}$ In the next section, we will investigate the origin of

the small Bruggeman exponent $1 + \alpha$ with the X-ray tomography approach, an independent approach of tortuosity factor quantification.

Tortuosity Factor Quantification via X-ray Tomography. As another independent way of quantifying the tortuosity factor, as well as to investigate the origin of the small Bruggeman exponent $1 + \alpha$ fitted from electrochemical cycling data, we applied synchrotron X-ray tomography to investigate the morphology of the composite cathode in ASSBs. Tortuosity factor of the catholyte and thus the Bruggeman exponent can be then calculated from the segmented tomography image. Previously, X-ray tomography has been applied to probe the microstructure of many battery components, e.g., polymer separator,[39] cathode[40] and anode[41] in Li-ion batteries, as well as the $\mathrm{SE}^{42-45}$ and the Li metal anode[46] in ASSBs. In this work, X-ray tomography images of the composite cathode region were obtained from an assembled ASSB described in the Supporting Information. Different components in the composite cathode were distinguished by image segmentation using Dragonfly software.[47] The gray scale contrast in X-ray tomography is approximately proportional to the inverse of the X-ray attenuation length of each material.[48] The different sensitivities can be illustrated by calculating the transmission of an 80 keV X-ray beam passing through 3 mm of each material, as shown in Figure 3a. LTLC exhibits an X-ray transmission of 44%, NMC shows a transmission of 87%, and PTFE and C show 95%. The volume fractions of different components in the cathode are calculated based on the weight fraction of components as well as the measured porosity, as summarized in Table 1. Additionally, isolated LTLC particles can be segmented in the tomography images. As illustrated in Figure S10a in the Supporting Information, we define isolated LTLC as those LTLC particles that are not in contact with the percolating catholyte network corresponding to active LTLC particles. As the ionic conductivity of LTLC is much higher than other components in the composite cathode, only LTLC conducts Li ions effectively in the cathode but only if they are connected by Li-ion conducting phase (i.e., other LTLC particles) to those LTLC particles at the LLZO-cathode interfaces, making them inside the percolating network. By identification of the percolation network in the segmented tomography image, isolated LTLC particles can be identified. The isolated LTLC accounts for $16.48\%$ of the total LTLC, representing $4.12\mathrm{vol}$ $\%$ of the entire cathode. Details on the detection of isolated LTLC particles can be found in the Supporting Information. Our tomography analysis cannot precisely capture all relevant microstructures due to limited resolution, but it follows statistical trends. Further details on this are provided in the Supporting Information. It is also worth mentioning that due to the resolution limitations of X-ray tomography used in this work, determination of the contact area between NMC and LTLC is out of the scope of this paper. Accurate determination of the contact area and, therefore, the kinetics between NMC and LTLC might be obtained by higher-resolution X-ray tomography that can resolve the interface between individual NMC and LTLC particles.

Using the segmented tomography image, the value of tortuosity factor $\tau$ and the Bruggeman exponent $1 + \alpha$ are quantified as follows. Laplace equation $\nabla \cdot (\sigma \nabla \phi) = 0$ is solved within the catholyte region with open-source software FeniCS,49 where $\phi$ is the electric potential and $\sigma$ is the conductivity $(\sigma = 1$ is assumed here for simplicity). Boundary conditions are $\phi_{\mathrm{u}} = 1$ at the top of the catholyte region and $\phi_{\mathrm{b}} = 0$ at the bottom, and no-flux boundary condition elsewhere. Solving the Laplace equation results in determination of $\sigma$ and ionic current $J = \sigma \nabla \phi$ at the lower boundary of the catholyte region. The above-solved current is then compared with the ideal scenario current where the volume fraction of catholyte $\epsilon$ is the same, except that catholyte particles are densely packed, and therefore $\tau = 1$ . In this ideal scenario, $J_{\mathrm{ideal}} = \epsilon \sigma \frac{\phi_{\mathrm{a}} - \phi_{\mathrm{b}}}{L}$ . For the real scenario, the ionic current is $J = \sigma \nabla \phi = \sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}\frac{\phi_{\mathrm{a}} - \phi_{\mathrm{b}}}{L} = \sigma \frac{\epsilon}{\tau}\frac{\phi_{\mathrm{a}} - \phi_{\mathrm{b}}}{L}$ . Since the value of $J$ is computed from solving the Laplace equation, the values of $\sigma_{\mathrm{w}}$ , $\sigma_{\mathrm{b}}$ , $L$ and $\epsilon$ are known, and therefore, the value of tortuosity

factor $\tau$ can be determined. The method described above is similar to Lin et al.,[44] in which the tortuosity factor of the SE separator was determined. Note that there exists isolated LTLC within the cathode that does not contribute to Li-ion conduction; the volume fraction of catholyte $\epsilon$ is given by the total volume fraction of the catholyte subtracted by the volume fraction of the isolated catholyte. Applying the above-described method to the segmented tomography image, we get tortuosity factor of LTLC phase along the $z$ direction (i.e., along thickness direction of cathode) $\tau = 3.19$ which agrees well with the tortuosity factor $\tau$ quantified with electrochemical cycling data, and the corresponding Bruggeman exponent $1 + \alpha = 1.75$ if excluding the isolated LTLC particles from the volume percent of the catholyte. The Bruggeman exponents of the LTLC phase along the $x$ and $y$ directions are also calculated to be $(1 + \alpha)_x = 2.25$ , and $(1 + \alpha)_y = 2.26$ . Similarly, the Bruggeman exponent for the NMC phase along the $z$ direction of the cathode is calculated to be $1 + \alpha = 1.38$ , and the tortuosity factor for the NMC phase is calculated to be $\tau = 1.22$ . Inspired by Krygier et al.,[50] we investigated the impact of segmentation threshold on the calculated Bruggeman exponent by varying the threshold of segmentation, resulting in varying morphology of the segmented LTLC phase. Detailed description can be found in the Supporting Information. In short, varying the segmentation threshold has little impact on the calculated Bruggeman exponent.

To explain the lower than expected Bruggeman exponent of the LTLC phase determined by both electrochemical data and tomography data, we investigated the morphology of LTLC. According to the SEM image of LTLC in Yin et al.,[8] LTLC particles show a rodlike morphology. Inspired by the shape analysis in Ebner et al.,[13] the isolated LTLC particles segmented from the 3D tomography image are fitted to ellipsoids, and the shape of the ellipsoids is examined and shown in Figure 4. This shows that $66.06\%$ of the isolated LTLC particles fall into either the "bladed" or "prolate" category, indicating that LTLC particles have a rodlike shape,

As a result, Li ions can move in straight lines within the LTLC particles, reducing the transport distance and thus reducing the tortuosity factor. The conclusion also agrees with the above-calculated Bruggeman exponent of the LTLC phase in $x$ and $y$ directions to be larger than that in $z$ direction as the tortuosity factor is minimized when Li-ion transport direction aligns with the longest axis of LTLC particles and is larger along the smaller two axes. It is also worth mentioning that applying large stack pressure can lead to better contact between the cathode active material and the catholyte, as well as deformation of the catholyte which also reduces the tortuosity factor and the Bruggeman exponent. However, applying large stack pressure to ASSBs requires a bulky fixture, which reduces the specific energy of the cell and is thus undesirable.

# SUMMARY

In summary, we report a tortuosity factor quantification approach for ASSBs that integrates experiments and modeling. Tortuosity factor $\tau$ and Bruggeman exponent $1 + \alpha$ are determined via two independent ways, i.e., fitted from electrochemical cycling data, as well as calculated from tomography data. Tortuosity factors quantified from both methods agree with each other, indicating self-consistency and reliability of the quantified $\tau$ and $1 + \alpha$ . The quantified tortuosity factor and thus the corresponding effective ionic conductivity of the catholyte in the composite cathode were used in subsequent pseudo-2D full-cell simulations and validated at different current densities against experimentally measured voltage profiles. We applied the tortuosity factor quantification to a Li Alloy anode—LLZTO—NMC811+LTLC solid-state cell and found that rod-shaped catholyte particles help lower the tortuosity factor of the composite cathode. We suggested that controlling the shape of catholyte particles can boost the rate performance of solid-state batteries.
