# Stable Catholyte Interface Enables Practical Operation of Sulfide-Based All-Solid-State Li Metal Batteries

Hyeongseok Lee, Sumin Ko, Soojin Park,* and Sang-Min Lee*

Achieving chemical and electrochemical stability of sulfide-based solid electrolytes is crucial for enabling practical slurry fabrication and reliable operation of all-solid-state batteries (ASSBs). Herein, a fluorocarbon-terminated self-assembled monolayer (SAM) strategy is reported that forms a conformal and chemically inert surface on $\mathrm{Li}_6\mathrm{PS}_5\mathrm{Cl}$ (LPSCl), yielding a stabilized catholyte ( $-\mathrm{CF}_3@$ LPSCl) compatible with polar solvent-based processing. The SAM layer effectively suppresses nucleophilic degradation induced by ester solvents and moisture while maintaining the crystalline bulk structure and high ionic conductivity of LPSCl. The surface fluorination simultaneously enhances both chemical and electrochemical stability, characterized by X-ray absorption near-edge structure measurements, enabling high-rate capability and stable cycling under $1.0\mathrm{C}$ conditions. Under low stack pressure ( $\approx 0.3\mathrm{MPa}$ ), the $-\mathrm{CF}_3@$ LPSCl catholyte suppresses not only the catholyte degradation but also alleviates mechanical contact loss within the cathode, achieving superior cycling stability without reliance on binder reinforcement. Notably, full cells assembled with thin Li metal and a low N/P ratio exhibit $90.5\%$ capacity retention over 300 cycles. This work demonstrates that a simple but straightforward fabrication of surface-stable catholyte—beyond binder and electrode engineering—can play a decisive role in achieving scalable and pressure-tolerant ASSBs platforms.

# 1. Introduction

All-solid-state batteries (ASSBs) have emerged as a promising next-generation energy storage technology owing to their superior safety and higher energy density compared to conventional liquid electrolyte-based lithium-ion batteries (LIBs).[1-3] Among various solid electrolytes, sulfide-based materials are considered the most viable candidates for commercialization, owing to their high ionic conductivity, low-temperature processability, and excellent mechanical deformability that ensures intimate solid-solid interfacial contact.[4-6] To enable scalable manufacturing of ASSBs, slurry-based wet processing has been increasingly adopted for fabricating large-area composite cathodes and solid electrolyte membranes.[7,8] Accordingly, the selection of appropriate solvents and binders for slurry processing is of critical importance to make a uniform electrode and/or electrolyte membrane.

Ethyl acetate (EA), in particular, is highly suitable for large-scale processing since it shows high polarity due to a short alkyl chain and low toxicity $(\mathrm{LD}_{50,\mathrm{Oral,rat}} = 10,200\mathrm{mgkg}^{-1})$ with classification as a green solvent based on life cycle assessment (Table S1, Supporting Information).[12,13] Despite these advantages, EA's high dielectric constant can trigger nucleophilic attack on the soft thiophosphate framework of sulfide-based solid electrolytes.[14,15] This results in degradation initiating at the particle surface, disrupting lithium-ion conduction pathways or partially dissolving the electrolyte, ultimately accelerating failure. Herein, we employed a polar poly(vinylidene fluoride-co-hexafluoropropylene) (PVDF-co-HFP) binder in combination with EA to fabricate slurry-cast composite cathodes based on $\mathrm{Li}_6\mathrm{PS}_5\mathrm{Cl}$ (LPSCl). To enhance interfacial stability, self-assembled monolayer (SAM) coatings composed of hydrocarbon or fluorocarbon precursors were introduced. These SAMs mitigated undesirable solvent-electrolyte reactions and increased tolerance to moisture, thereby improving compatibility with dryroom slurry processes. Notably, the fluorocarbon-based precursor not only facilitated the formation of a SAM but also generated a fluorine-rich surface layer on LPSCl in-situ during synthesis, which significantly enhanced electrochemical stability under high-voltage operation. Moreover, by suppressing the degradation of the solid electrolyte, the modified surface directly mitigated unfavorable interfacial reactions at the cathode active material.

Importantly, the stability of the catholyte interface emerges as a key determinant of full-cell performance, underscoring its central role in enabling long-term and reliable operation. A chemically and electrochemically stabilized catholyte enabled rapid and uniform charge transfer even under high current densities and low stack pressures, ensuring stable operation in practical conditions (Figure 1). Previous studies have largely focused on addressing mechanical contact loss by employing mechanically robust binders.[20,21] Although some coating-based approaches were made, they have typically targeted a single functionality—such as moisture tolerance or electrochemical stability—without considering their interplay.[22-24] However, we show that once the catholyte is degraded by polar solvents and binders, electrochemical bias can accelerate oxidative decomposition and exacerbate mechanical detachment. Our findings demonstrate that surface-stable catholytes simultaneously suppress mechanical contact loss and reduce charge transfer resistance arising from interfacial degradation, enabling low-pressure operation even under commercial coin cell configurations. As a result, the use of SAM-coated catholytes in slurry-processed cathodes enabled the fabrication of ASSBs that operate under low stack pressure and low negative-to-positive electrode $(\mathrm{N / P})$ ratios with a thin Li metal anode, delivering $90.5\%$ capacity retention over 300 cycles at 0.5C.

# 2. Results and Discussion

# 2.1. Characterization of SAM-Coated Catholytes

A SAM is an ordered molecular layer composed of a head group that forms a chemical bond with a substrate, an alkyl or fluoroalkyl chain of uniform length, and a functional tail group. Upon chemisorption of the head group, surface binding sites become saturated, resulting in the formation of a monolayer with a typical thickness of $1 - 3\mathrm{nm}$ . Subsequent van der Waals interactions among adjacent chains promote lateral alignment, enabling the formation of a uniform and coherent protective layer across the surface.[25] As shown in Figure 2a, the sulfide electrolytes, LPSCl, were dispersed in toluene and mixed with SAM-forming monomers. Given the reactive nature of the thiophosphate framework, monomers bearing thiol head groups—known to form strong and stable interactions with sulfide surfaces—were selected.[26-28] Tail groups included hydrocarbon $\left(-\mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{SAM}\right)$ and fluorocarbon $\left(-\mathrm{CF}_3\mathrm{SAM}\right)$ functionalities, both of which are associated with high chemical resistance and hydrophobicity.[29-30]

Following prolonged agitation, the monomers gradually adsorbed onto the polar surfaces of LPSCl particles and underwent self-assembly into ordered monolayers. Cryogenic transmission electron microscopy (cryo-TEM) images showed that both $-\mathrm{CH}_3@$ LPSCl and $-\mathrm{CF}_3@$ LPSCl exhibited thin, uniform, and amorphous surface layers indicative of successful SAM formation (Figure 2b,c; Figure S1, Supporting Information). Fast Fourier transform (FFT) patterns confirmed that the bulk crystalline structure of LPSCl remained intact after SAM coating. Evidence for the conformal, single-molecule-layer nature of the SAM coating was further obtained through a combination of microscopic and compositional analyses. TEM-EELS directly revealed a continuous coating layer enveloping individual LPSCl particles (Figure S2, Supporting Information), while complementary low-magnification TEM-EDX mapping confirmed that this surface layer extended uniformly across multiple particles on a larger scale (Figure S3, Supporting Information). # 2.2. Stability of Different Catholytes

To assess whether the bulk structure of LPSCl was affected by SAM coating, Raman spectroscopy and X-ray diffraction (XRD) analyses were performed. All samples, including pristine, $-\mathrm{CH}_3@$ LPSCl, and $-\mathrm{CF}_3@$ LPSCl, exhibited a characteristic Raman peak at $425~\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ corresponding to the symmetric stretching of the $\mathrm{PS}_4^{3-}$ tetrahedron (Figure 3a), while their XRD patterns confirmed that the original crystalline phase was retained (Figure S9, Supporting Information). These results indicate that the ultrathin SAM layer in the nanometer scale was uniformly formed without inducing any detectable bulk structural distortion or triggering parasitic reactions.

The influence of SAM coatings on lithium-ion transport properties was investigated via electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) using a lithium-ion-blocked symmetric cell (Figure S10, Supporting Information). Pristine LPSCl, $-\mathrm{CH}_3@$ LPSCl, and $-\mathrm{CF}_3@$ LPSCl exhibited room-temperature ionic conductivities of 1.84, 1.32, and $1.57~\mathrm{mS~cm^{-1}}$ , respectively, with all values remaining above $1.0~\mathrm{mS~cm^{-1}}$ . The reduction in

conductivity was marginal, reflecting the minimal thickness of the surface coating. Notably, this trend persisted at sub-ambient temperatures, suggesting that the SAM layer does not critically impede Li-ion conduction even under thermally suppressed kinetic conditions. The effect of external pressure on ion transport was also evaluated (Figure S11, Supporting Information). All SAM-coated electrolytes exhibited a nearly linear increase in ionic conductivity with increasing pressure, without abrupt changes in slope. If the SAM layer were compromised at high pressure, a pronounced increase in ionic conductivity would be expected due to enhanced interparticle contact by cold sintering. The absence of such non-linear behavior indicates that the SAM layer remains mechanically robust under compression. This interpretation is further corroborated by TEM-EELS analysis, which revealed that the surface coating remained intact even after pressing at $500\mathrm{MPa}$ (Figure S12, Supporting Information).

The electronic conductivities were comparable across all samples in the range of $10^{-8}\mathrm{Scm}^{-1}$ (Figure S13, Supporting Information), indicating that the SAM layer does not compromise electronic insulation.

The chemical stability of the SAM-coated electrolytes was evaluated by exposing the samples to EA, a polar solvent with known reactivity toward sulfide electrolytes. After EA exposure, pristine LPSCl exhibited a noticeable increase in bulk resistance, whereas both $-\mathrm{CH}_3@$ LPSCl and $-\mathrm{CF}_3@$ LPSCl largely retained their original conductivity profiles (Figures S14 and S15, Supporting Information). As summarized in Figure 3b, $-\mathrm{CF}_3@$ LPSCl maintained the highest ionic conductivity $(1.44~\mathrm{mS~cm}^{-1})$ among the tested samples, suggesting that the SAM layer effectively shields the surface from nucleophilic attack.

# 2.3. Degradation Mechanism of Slurry-Fabricated Cathodes

The electrochemical oxidation stability of the coated catholytes was evaluated using linear sweep voltammetry (LSV). As shown in Figures 4a and S18 (Supporting Information), bare LPSCl exhibited a pronounced anodic current above $2.5\mathrm{V}$ (vs. $\mathrm{Li} / \mathrm{Li}^{+}$ ), consistent with known oxidative decomposition of unstable an

ions such as $\mathrm{PS}_4^{3-}$ , leading to LiCl precipitation and the formation of $\mathrm{P}_2\mathrm{S}_x$ species through sulfur-bridging reactions.[32] The $-\mathrm{CH}_3@$ LPSCl sample displayed reduced but still substantial oxidative current, indicating that surface passivation was only partially effective. In contrast, $-\mathrm{CF}_3@$ LPSCl exhibited significantly lower oxidation current, suggesting that parasitic reactions were effectively suppressed. This is attributed to the inherent electrochemical stability of the fluorocarbon SAM and the formation of a fluorine-enriched LPSCl surface, which together enhance resistance to high-voltage decomposition.[33,34] These results suggest that $-\mathrm{CF}_3$ SAM coating enables stable catholyte performance under slurry processing with polar binders and solvents (Figure 4b).

To further validate this stability, catholyte degradation before and after extended cycling was evaluated using sulfur K-edge tender X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) spectroscopy (Figure 4c-e; Figure S19, Supporting Information). Even prior to cycling, slight spectral differences were present among the samples, likely originating from early-stage chemical interactions with the solvent. These differences became more pronounced after cycling, with $-\mathrm{CF}_3@$ LPSCI showing the most stable spectral profile (Figure S20a, Supporting Information). Longer cycling at $1.0\mathrm{C}$ further highlighted differences in catholyte stability. As shown in Figure 6b, cells using LPSCl and $-\mathrm{CH}_3@$ LPSCl underwent abrupt degradation at 20th and 25th cycles, respectively. In stark contrast, the $-\mathrm{CF}_3@$ LPSCl-based cell exhibited remarkable stability, retaining $96.9\%$ of its initial capacity and maintaining a coulombic efficiency of $99.8\%$ over 100 cycles. These results indicate that even when polar solvents and binders are used in electrode fabrication, employing $-\mathrm{CF}_3@$ LPSCl as the catholyte can secure sufficient chemical and electrochemical stability for robust cell operation. Furthermore, its performance was comparable to that of electrodes fabricated using nitrile butadiene rubber (NBR) binder and the weakly polar butyl butyrate solvent—components commonly adopted for sulfide electrolyte compatibility. For instance, a cell employing bare LPSCl and NBR/butyl butyrate (BB) showed a capacity retention of $89.7\%$ and a Coulombic efficiency of $99.8\%$ over 100 cycles (Figure S23, Supporting Information), which was still inferior to the cell using $-\mathrm{CF}_3@$ LPSCl with EA.

These findings emphasize that improving the chemical stability of the solid electrolyte plays a more decisive role in ensuring battery performance than merely selecting lower polarity binders and solvents. Although butyl butyrate contains a bulky side chain that can suppress side reactions, its ester functional group still imparts mild polarity, leading to residual reactivity with the electrolyte. Thus, applying $-\mathrm{CF}_3@$ LPSCl as the catholyte still outperformed bare LPSCl under low-polarity conditions in both capacity and long-term retention, reaching superior performance over 200 cycles (Figure S23, Supporting Information). The internal pressure of the cell remained consistently stable throughout cycling (Figure S24, Supporting Information), reflecting reversible electrochemical reactions when $-\mathrm{CF}_3@$ LPSCl was employed. In contrast, the cell with bare LPSCl showed a gradual reduction in pressure fluctuations, indicative of increasing charge-transfer resistance and irreversible interfacial degradation. The origin of these observations will be discussed in greater detail in the subsequent section.

Even under high-loading conditions $\approx 6$ mAh cm $^{-2}$ , $-\mathrm{CF}_3@$ LPSCl exhibited significantly enhanced rate capability compared to the bare LPSCl catholyte, and delivered stable cycling at $0.5\mathrm{C}$ (Figure S25a,b, Supporting Information), further validating the robust electrochemical interface enabled by the SAM coating. The TEM-EELS analysis also confirmed that the SAM-coated interface remained unharmed after cycling (Figure S25c, Supporting Information). These results underscore the critical importance of surface stability in solid electrolytes for enhancing cell durability.

# 2.5. Effect of Catholyte Degradation on Low-Pressure Operation

The use of a surface-stable catholyte resulted in markedly improved battery performance under low stack pressure conditions. In general, the performance deterioration of ASSBs under low

pressure is primarily attributed to mechanical contact loss at the electrolyte-electrode interface, which arises from insufficient interfacial adhesion. To mitigate this, previous studies have introduced various strategies such as modifying binder compositions and/or process conditions.[18,44] However, even without any mechanical reinforcement, simply replacing the catholyte with a stable variant significantly enhanced cell performance under identical low-pressure assembly conditions.

As shown in Figures 7a and S26 (Supporting Information), in CR2032 coin cells equipped with a wave spring applying $\approx 0.3\mathrm{MPa}$ pressure (see Note S1, Supporting Information),[45,46] the cell with $-\mathrm{CF}_3@$ LPSCl catholyte exhibited an initial discharge capacity of $156.6\mathrm{mAhg^{-1}}$ and a Coulombic efficiency of $95.4\%$ at $0.2\mathrm{C}$ . In contrast, the cell using bare LPSCl delivered only $133.8\mathrm{mAhg^{-1}}$ with a lower Coulombic efficiency of $92.6\%$ . This performance gap persisted throughout extended cycling, with average Coulombic efficiencies of $99.8\%$ for $-\mathrm{CF}_3@$ LPSCl and $99.1\%$ for bare LPSCl over 50 cycles, respectively, indicating irreversible lithium loss in the latter. After 100 cycles, the capacity retention of the $-\mathrm{CF}_3@$ LPSCl cell reached $66.0\%$ , nearly double

that of the bare LPSCl cell $(35.5\%)$ , confirming significantly improved long-term reversibility.

Voltage profiles further revealed the underlying causes of performance divergence (Figure 7b). Compared to the stable discharge profile of the $-\mathrm{CF}_3@$ LPSCl cell, the bare LPSCl cell exhibited voltage fluctuations, implying unstable charge-transfer processes and capacity fading. Although such behavior is often attributed to increasing charge-transfer resistance $(R_{\mathrm{ct}})$ caused by contact loss under low pressure,[45,47] both cells in this study were fabricated under identical conditions using single-crystalline cathode materials, which effectively minimize intraparticle contact loss. In fact, due to the higher realized specific discharge capacity of the $-\mathrm{CF}_3@$ LPSCl cell, one might expect greater internal stress and volume change, potentially leading to more severe contact loss.[48] However, the observed improvement in performance suggests a dominant role of catholyte stability.

EIS and distribution of relaxation time (DRT) analysis were conducted to elucidate this phenomenon (Figure 7c,d; Figure S27, Supporting Information). Peaks $\approx 10^{4}$ , $10^{2}$ , and $1\mathrm{Hz}$ were attributed to the cathode-electrolyte interphase (CEI) resistance, cathode charge-transfer resistance, and Li-In anode charge-transfer resistance, respectively.[47,49-51] These were denoted as L1-L3 for the bare LPSCl cell and F1-F3 for the $-\mathrm{CF}_3@$ LPSCl cell. The CEI resistance in the L1 component increased substantially after cycling, whereas F1 remained relatively stable, consistent with surface stabilization by the fluorinated SAM, as also observed in Figure 5. More crucially, cathode $\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{ct}}$ (L2 vs. F2) showed a markedly milder increase in the $-\mathrm{CF}_3@$ LPSCl cell. This resistance originates from both mechanical and chemical degradation at the cathode interface.[45,47] Cross-polished scanning electron microscopy-energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (CP SEM-EDX) revealed cracks at the cathode-electrolyte interface in both cases (Figure S28, Supporting Information), but the bare LPSCl electrolyte appeared highly porous due to irreversible molar volume shrinkage upon degradation.[52,53] This pore-forming degradation not only reduced ionic conductivity but also exacerbated interfacial contact loss, highlighting the critical role of catholyte stability in maintaining fast charge-transfer pathways and suppressing resistance growth under low pressure. Meanwhile, anode $\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{ct}}$ (L3 vs. F3) remained relatively low for the bare catholyte cell, likely due to irreversible lithium plating and excessive lithiation from poor Coulombic efficiency.[52]

The benefits of SAM-coated catholytes were further validated in full-cell configurations using thin lithium metal anodes. As shown in Figure 7e, the $-\mathrm{CF}_3@$ LPSCl cell exhibited superior initial capacity and Coulombic efficiency relative to the bare catholyte counterpart. Under a stack pressure of $10\mathrm{MPa}$ , this cell achieved a capacity retention of $90.5\%$ over 300 cycles (Figure 7f), demonstrating exceptional cycling stability. In contrast, capacity fading in the bare LPSCl cell was not due to dendrite-induced short circuits but instead stemmed from progressive interfacial degradation and increasing $\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{ct}}$ at the cathode-electrolyte interface (Figures S29 and S30, Supporting Information). This trend remained consistent even under elevated temperature $(70^{\circ}\mathrm{C})$ , where improved lithium-ion kinetics failed to compensate for catholyte instability (Figure S31, Supporting Information). These results clearly demonstrate that, beyond electrode architecture and binder design, the chemical and electro

chemical stability of the catholyte itself is a decisive factor in enabling reliable ASSB operation under practical, low-pressure conditions.

# 3. Conclusion

In summary, we developed surface-stable sulfide electrolytes by introducing a fluorocarbon-terminated self-assembled monolayer (SAM) onto $\mathrm{Li}_6\mathrm{PS}_5\mathrm{Cl}$ (LPSCl). The resulting $-\mathrm{CF}_3@$ LPSCl catholyte exhibited markedly enhanced chemical resistance to polar solvents and moisture, thereby enabling reliable slurry-based fabrication in dry-room environments using conventional polar binders and solvents. Furthermore, the in situ formation of a fluorine-enriched surface layer conferred improved electrochemical stability under high-voltage conditions, as confirmed by S K-edge XANES and Ni TXM-XANES, and enabled superior rate capability and stable cycling even at $1.0~\mathrm{C}$ . Stabilized catholyte also significantly mitigated performance degradation under low stack pressure, where mechanical contact loss typically dominates. Unlike conventional strategies that focus on binder optimization, our approach emphasizes the mitigation of catholyte degradation as a means to suppress charge transfer resistance and improve interfacial integrity. As a result, full cells employing thin lithium metal and a low N/P ratio achieved excellent cycling stability, retaining $90.5\%$ of their initial capacity over 300 cycles. These findings underscore the critical importance of catholyte surface design in realizing practical and scalable all-solid-state battery systems.
