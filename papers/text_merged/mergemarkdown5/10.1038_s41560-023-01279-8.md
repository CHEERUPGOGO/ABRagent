All-solid-state batteries (ASSBs) with Li metal anodes or Si anodes are promising candidates to achieve high energy density and improved safety, but they sufer from undesirable lithium dendrite growth or huge volume expansion, respectively. Here we synthesize a hard-carbon-stabilized Li–Si alloy anode in which sintering of Si leads to the transformation of micro-metre particles into dense continuum. A 3D ionic-electronicconductive network composed of plastically deformable Li-rich phases $( \mathrm { L i } _ { 1 5 } \mathrm { S i } _ { 4 }$ and LiC6) that enlarges active area and relieves stress concentration is created in the anode, leading to improved electrode kinetics and mechanical stability. With the hard-carbon-stabilized Li-Si anode, full cells using L $\mathbf { i } { \mathsf { C o O } } _ { 2 }$ or $\mathsf { L i N i } _ { 0 . 8 } \mathsf { C o } _ { 0 . 1 } \mathsf { M n } _ { 0 . 1 } \mathsf { O } _ { 2 }$ cathodes and $\mathsf { L i } _ { 6 } \mathsf { P S } _ { 5 } \mathsf { C l }$ electrolyte achieve favourable rate capability and cycle stability. In particular, the ASSB with $\mathbf { L i N i } _ { 0 . 8 } \mathbf { C o } _ { 0 . 1 } \mathbf { M n } _ { 0 . 1 } \mathbf { O } _ { 2 }$ at high loading of $5 . 8 6 \mathsf { m A h c m } ^ { - 2 }$ delivers 5,000 cycles at 1 C $( 5 . 8 6 \mathsf { m A c m ^ { - 2 } } .$ ), demonstrating the potential of using hard-carbon-stabilized Li–Si alloy anodes for practical applications of ASSBs.

All-solid-state batteries (ASSBs) have triggered a surging interest from both academics and industry, thanks to their potentially high safety and power/energy density. Among all candidates, ASSBs with sulfide solid electrolytes (SEs) and lithium metal anode have been recognized as one of the most promising systems. However, the practical application of lithium metal anode encounters two critical issues: dendrite growth and interfacial reactions, which leads to rapid capacity decay and failure1 . Numerous strategies including artificial solid–electrolyte interphase (SEI)2 , protection layers (for example, indium metal3 , graphite4 ) and lithium-free anodes such as Ag–C anode5 and so on have been proposed to solve the above issues. However, these methods are either hard to achieve both high loading and high current density

or still suffer from failure of lithium dendrite growth. For example, researchers have observed the dendrite growth of the Li–In anode after a long-term cycle leading to the death of ASSB3 . Although ASSBs with graphite protection layer and Li anode can cycle at a high current rate of 20 C $( 8 . 6 \mathsf { m A } \mathsf { c m } ^ { - 2 } )$ ), their areal mass loading was notably sacrificed $( - 2 \mathsf { m g c m } ^ { - 2 } )$ ) (ref. 4). The ASSBs with lithium-free Ag–C anodes may have the risk of lithium dendrite failure, as the Coulombic efficiency becomes unstable after long cycles5 . Therefore, the expected high areal capacity, high current density and long lifetime of ASSBs with lithium metal anode may be impractical.

Si anode is a promising alternative candidate due to its abundant reserves, low cost, high room-temperature specific capacity

Notably, ASSBs paired with Si anodes have little risks of lithium dendrite growth and undesirable interfacial side reactions, such as continuous fracture and regeneration of SEI on Si particle surfaces in conventional lithium-ion batteries and the decomposition of sulfide SEs at low potential. Nevertheless, research on the construction of practical sulfide ASSBs with Si-based anodes is still limited8 .

Most studies have focused on composite anodes composed of Si-based active materials and electrolytes. Recently, Zhu et al. reported a full cell with a Si composite anode, a thin SE membrane and a $\mathsf { L i N i } _ { 0 . 8 } \mathsf { M n } _ { 0 . 1 } \mathsf { C o } _ { 0 . 1 } \mathsf { O } _ { 2 }$ cathode7 , which has a cell-level energy density of 285 Wh ${ \sf k g } ^ { - 1 }$ . Meanwhile, some researchers have demonstrated that SE-free Si anodes can be used in ASSBs for higher energy density9 . Takada et al. verified intrinsic high rate capacity of amorphous non-porous Si film (200 C with reversible capacity of $2 , 4 0 0 \mathrm { { m A h g ^ { - 1 } ) } }$ (ref. 10) and high capacity upon cycling (2.19 mAh cm−2 and 2,962 mAh $\mathbf { g } ^ { - 1 }$ after 100 cycles) with amorphous porous Si films11. They also observed coalescence of Si nanoparticles during lithiation, which may facilitate application of SE-free Si anodes in ASSBs12. Keskel et al. demonstrated that columnar Si anode could cycle stably at a high areal capacity of 3.5 mAh $\mathsf { c m } ^ { - 2 }$ (ref. 13). Meng et al. reported a carbon-free micro silicon $( 9 9 . 9 \mathrm { w t \% } )$ ) anode, which delivered long-cycle performance (500 cycles) at a high areal capacity (2.5 mAh cm−2) and a high areal current density $( 5 \mathsf { m A } \mathsf { c m } ^ { - 2 } )$ ) (ref. 9).

However, most ASSBs with Si anodes suffer from severe degradation, probably due to structural instability and sluggish electrode kinetics. Li–Si alloy anodes may be more suitable for ASSBs than Si anodes because Li doping can sinter Si particles by the formation of a Li–Si–Li–Si bond14. Li–Si alloys are also a good ${ \mathsf { L } } { \mathsf { i } } ^ { + }$ conductor with high electronic conductivities $( > \mathsf { S i } ) ^ { 1 5 }$ . As the hardness of Li–Si alloys (1.3–1.5 GPa for $\mathsf { L i } _ { 3 . 7 5 } \mathsf { S i } ^ { 1 6 } )$ ) is typically approximately one order of magnitude lower than that of Si (10.0–11.6 GPa (ref. 9)), the swelling of Li–Si alloy and stress concentration are obviously milder than those of silicon. Furthermore, lithium contained in Li–Si alloy can supplement the lithium loss during cycling to prolong cycle life.

In this work, a hard-carbon-stabilized Li–Si alloy anode is introduced into ASSBs. Electrochemical sintering of Si anode during cycling leads to a continuous amorphous film rather than pulverization in conventional lithium-ion batteries. Sintering of Si and Li trapping after the first cycle can activate the anode for improved electronic conductivity and lithium diffusivity. Therefore, SE-free Si anode can be used in ASSBs with improved energy density. A 3D fast Li+ -electronic conducting network is created inside the anode, containing Li-rich phases $( \mathrm { L i } _ { 1 5 } \mathrm { S i } _ { 4 } + \mathrm { L i C } _ { 6 } )$ . The kinetics and stability of the anode are hence improved. As a result, coupling with hard-carbon-stabilized LiSi alloy anodes, the ASSBs with areal capacities of 0.4 and 3.6 mAh cm−2 cycle stably for 30,000 and 5,000 cycles at current densities of 14.64 (20 C) and $5 . 8 6 \mathsf { m A } \mathsf { c m } ^ { - 2 } ( 1 \mathsf { C } )$ , respectively. High current densities, mass loadings and cell-level energy density are also achieved for different ASSBs.

# Sintering of Si

To evaluate the electrochemical performances of Si-contained anode, ASSBs with $\mathsf { L i N i } _ { 0 . 8 } \mathsf { C o } _ { 0 . 1 } \mathsf { M n } _ { 0 . 1 } \mathsf { O } _ { 2 }$ (NMC811; Supplementary Fig. 1a,b) or $\mathsf { L i C o O } _ { 2 }$ (LCO; Supplementary Fig. 1c,d) cathode, Si-contained anode and $\mathrm { L i } _ { 6 } \mathrm { P S } _ { 5 } \mathrm { C l }$ (SE) were assembled by the process shown in Supplementary Fig. 2. The capacity of Si-contained films was controlled at a constant that is obtained from reversible capacity of the half cell (Supplementary Fig. 3) and detailed in Supplementary Table 1.

The ASSBs with Si anodes show low reversible capacity and rapid capacity decay at 1 C (Fig. 1a–c). Cross-section scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of the Si anodes (Fig. 1d–f) are provided to clarify the lithiation and degradation mechanism of Si anodes. After cycling, μm Si anode (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. 5) transforms into a dense and continuous Si film (Fig. 1e–f) with disappeared particle boundaries, leading to reduced tortuosity and improved electrode

kinetics. This phenomenon seems to contradict with the widely accepted Si fragmentation and pulverization, but this can be explained by the electrochemical sintering of $S \mathbf { i } ^ { 1 7 }$ , that is, boundary fusion of Si. Volume expansion during Si lithiation fills the vacancy for full contact of Si particles and Li–Si–Li–Si bond formation, followed by particle boundary sealing by Si–Si bonds after delithiation. This SE-free Si anode (no SEI re-generates at the surface of inner Si particles) turns to be in amorphous continuum after the first cycle and cycles with a single-phase reaction subsequently18.

After cycling, the anode shows penetrating vertical cracks (caused by the lithium diffusion-induced stress and brittle fracture of the Si–Si bond14,19) and no horizontal cracks (healed by the vertical compressive pressure) such that lithium diffusion and electron conduction along the vertical direction do not appear to be affected. More and broader vertical cracks appear after ten cycles at 1 C indicating degraded electrode structure with the increasing cycle numbers.

The 2D interface contact between the Si and SEs can reduce side reactions or active Li loss but shows vertical/horizontal cracks (Fig. 1e,f). This may result from mechanical mismatch of Si and SE. Large volume change of Si during cycling leads to interfacial stress concentration and delamination. Moreover, the vertical cracks may aggravate the debonding of the anode19. We can rationally confer that the interfacial cracks are also present at the interface of the current collector and anode. These interfacial cracks impede lithium and electron transport, leading to large polarization and capacity decay. In Fig. 1a,b, capacity decay is more pronounced in rate tests than that in cycle tests. It may be caused by more lithium intercalation and deintercalation of the anode at low current density and the consequently more notable volume change. This may further trigger more fracture in the vertical direction or at the interface, leading to degradation of electrode performance. Moreover, the lithium coefficient $( 1 0 ^ { - 1 5 } \mathsf { m } ^ { 2 } \mathsf { s } ^ { - 1 }$ ; Supplementary Fig. 6a) and electronic conductivity $( 1 0 ^ { - 6 } \mathsf { S } \mathsf { m } ^ { - 1 }$ ; Supplementary Fig. 9) indicate sluggish kinetics, contributing to poor electrochemical performance of Si anodes in ASSBs. In general, electrochemical sintering allows SE-free μm-Si to be used in ASSBs without pulverization. The weak performance of the $\mu \mathrm { m } S \dot { \mathbf { l } }$ anode can be ascribed to sluggish kinetics and structural instability.

Li Si alloy with plastic deformation capability20 and enhanced lithium/electron diffusivity/conductivity15 can improve mechanical stability and electrode kinetics. For example, ${ \mathbf { L i } } _ { 1 5 } { \mathbf { S i } } _ { 4 }$ , the phase with highest lithiation degree for bulk Si at ambient temperature, is prone to plastic deformation20. Compared with the hardness of Si (10–11.6 GPa (ref. 9)), that of $\mathrm { L i } _ { 1 5 } \mathrm { S i } _ { 4 }$ is notably smaller (1.3–1.5 GPa (ref. 16)) to reduce the stress concentration in the anode (leading to fewer cracks) and boost the cycle stability of ASSBs, especially coupling with the supplemental lithium. In addition, the reported diffusion coefficients of ${ \mathsf { L i } } _ { 1 5 } { \mathsf { S i } } _ { 4 }$ range from $1 0 ^ { - 1 0 } – 1 0 ^ { - 1 2 } \boldsymbol { \mathrm { m } } ^ { 2 } \boldsymbol { \mathrm { s } } ^ { - 1 }$ (ref. 15), surpassing that of $\mathrm { L i } _ { 6 } \mathrm { P S } _ { 5 } \mathrm { C l }$ $( { \sigma _ { \mathrm { L i } } } ^ { + } = 3 . 9 \mathsf { m S c m } ^ { - 1 }$ , Li+ diffusion coefficien $= 2 . 5 \times 1 0 ^ { - 1 2 } \mathsf { m } ^ { 2 } \mathsf { s } ^ { - 1 }$ (ref. 21)). The electronic conductivity of $\mathrm { \bf { L i } } _ { 1 5 } \mathrm { \bf { S i } } _ { 4 }$ (the bandgap of $\mathrm { L i } _ { 1 5 } \mathrm { S i } _ { 4 }$ diminishes, showing more metallic character) is also notably higher than that of Si to facilitate electrode kinetics22.

Herein, we construct Li–Si alloy anode mainly containing ${ \mathsf { L i } } _ { 1 5 } { \mathsf { S i } } _ { 4 }$ (Supplementary Fig. 10) via in situ reaction of contacted Li foil and Si film under external pressure during ASSB assembly (Supplementary Fig. 2), which is industry viable and safe (compare with previous synthesis in Supplementary Note 1). Supplementary Fig. 11 shows that increased Li/Si ratio contributes to higher capacity but short circuiting of ASSBs at 1 C rate $( 6 \mathsf { m A } \mathsf { c m } ^ { - 2 }$ ). The LiSi anode with 0.8 ratio enables the ASSB with highly stable reversible capacity of 115 mAh gNMC −1 $( \small { \sim } 3 . 8 \operatorname { m A h } \mathsf { c m } ^ { - 2 } )$ ), high Coulombic efficiency above $9 9 \%$ after five cycles and no capacity decay but short circuits at the 27th cycle (Fig. 1g). In comparison, ASSBs exploiting the LiSi anode with a Li/Si capacity ratio of 0.6 and 0.4 exhibit reduced reversible capacity and accelerated degradation, potentially stemming from X-ray diffraction (XRD)-documented unreacted Si particles (Supplementary Fig. 10).

A rate test was also performed for ASSBs with the LiSi anode (Fig. 1h,i). The Coulombic efficiency of the ASSB was above $9 7 \%$ at 0.1 C and 0.2 C but decreases to $62 \%$ when the rate comes to 0.5 C. The discharge curves in Fig. 1i show two plateaus, where the first plateau (~4 V) and second plateau (~3.3 V) correspond to lithium stripping and delithiation of the LixSi alloy, respectively. As the current density increases, the cell polarization increases. The charge of ASSB at 1 C hits the cut-off voltage earlier such that the capacity is mainly contributed by the lithiation of Si. Although prelithiation enhances electrode kinetics and structural stability of the anode, excessive lithium accumulation leads to continuous dendrite growth and subsequent short circuit failure upon cycling.

To effectively utilize a LiSi anode with a stable cycle and high reversible capacity, the short circuit issue must be addressed. Limited by sluggish lithium diffusion and few voids (Fig. 1j and Supplementary Fig. 12), high current densities and loadings may result in lithium

deposition at the anode/SE interface instead of diffusion into the anode. Moreover, the potential difference of 50 mV (vs Li+ /Li) for ${ \mathsf { L i } } _ { 1 5 } { \mathsf { S i } } _ { 4 }$ and overpotential for Li nucleation on Si (13 eV) (ref. 23) also facilitates large lithium dendrite growth at a high deposition rate. Therefore, a stabilizer (that enables fast lithium diffusion, more lithium deposition sites and low potential vs Li+ /Li at full lithiation state) should be incorporated with the LiSi alloy anode to alleviate lithium dendrite growth.

# Hard-carbon-stabilized Li–Si alloy anode

Carbon materials have been used in lithium metal anodes as a protection layer to suppress lithium dendrite growth and work well4 . Li et al. used Li graphite composite to enable a high external pressure for lithium dendrite suppression between Li metal and $\mathsf { L i } _ { 1 0 } \mathsf { G e P } _ { 2 } \mathsf { S } _ { 1 2 }$ (LGPS). This method was also used to couple with multilayer electrolytes to achieve ASSBs with 10,000 cycles at $2 0 \mathsf { C } ( 8 . 8 \mathsf { m A c m ^ { - 2 } } ,$ ) (ref. 4). Hard carbon (HC) with high disorder, large specific surface area, sufficient defects and low plateau at ~0.001 V may be a more preferred candidate compared with soft carbons/graphite, due to its high capacity, good rate capability and low expansion rate24.

The discharge curve of an ASSB with LiHC anode shows smooth slope, whereas those with LiSH anodes and LiSi anode exhibit two plateaus, between which the slope capacity increases with increasing HC content. That may be resulting from the special slope plateau of the HC (Supplementary Fig. 3). The slope and plateau (~0.001 V) may buffer the potential difference between lithium and the LixSi alloy and induce uniform lithium deposition from the surface of HC. It can thus be inferred that the lithiation process in LiSH anodes involves full lithiation of Si, complete lithiation of HC and finally lithium plating. Ex situ XRD on LiSH46 anode (Supplementary

Fig. 15 and Supplementary Note 2) also corroborates this process. More crystalline ${ \mathsf { L i } } _ { 1 5 } { \mathsf { S i } } _ { 4 }$ ${ \bf c } { \bf - } \lfloor \mathbf { i } _ { 1 5 } { \bf S } \mathbf { i } _ { 4 } )$ generates as charging capacity increases, and lithium peak shows after 8 h.

HC facilitates Li diffusion (Supplementary Fig. 6) and increases thickness (Supplementary Fig. 13), leading to different Li atom distributions and Li dendrite growth, as demonstrated by phase field simulation (Fig. 2d,e and Supplementary Note 3). Dendrite formation is more likely to occur at Si/HC weight ratio ${ \geq } 6 { : } 4 .$ . Meanwhile, dendrites will promote the continuous adsorption of Li+ on the dendrite surface due to the ‘tip effect’25, leading to active Li+ loss and continuous dendrite growth. During lithiation, Li is either embedded in the anode or deposited at the anode/SE interface (Supplementary Fig. 16). The length of Li dendrite increases with Si content. During the delithiation process, some lithium will be trapped in LiHC and LiSH28 with larger thickness, resulting in lower capacity and rapid degradation26,27.

In general, LiSH46 anode with improved lithium diffusion, moderate thickness/porosity and no lithium dendrite is favourable to achieve optimal performance of LiSi alloy anode. ASSBs with excessive Si are prone to soft short circuit under high current density (Fig. 2a). ASSBs with LiSH82 cycle with low/unstable Coulombic efficiency and reversible capacity due to continuous Li dendrite growth and active Li loss lead to increased polarization and rapid capacity decay. ASSBs with LiSH64 suffer dramatic degradation after several cycles, possibly due to the lithium consumption caused by reactions of plated lithium with SE. The ASSBs with excessive HC suffer from rapid capacity decay and relatively low reversible capacity. ASSBs with LiSH28 anode have similar reversible capacity as other LiSH anodes at 0.1 C but deliver much smaller reversible capacity at 1 C, possibly due to the large polarization caused by thickened electrode. Additionally, LixSi in LiSH28 anode is discrete, leading to mechanical mismatch, structural degradation and great difference in volume change between HC and Si during cycling. Moreover, lithium accumulation in Si during cycling along with the volume expansion of Si exerts pressure on the surrounding HC, leading to lithium storage capacity reduction of $\mathsf { H C } ^ { 2 6 }$ . Therefore, ASSB with LiSH28 anode manifests continuous capacity decay to a stable capacity even lower than that of ASSB with HC anode.

To understand how HC works in LiSH46 anode, LiSS46 and LiSG46 anodes were prepared by replacing HC in LiSH46 anode with soft carbon (SC) and graphite (Gr). Figure 3a–e shows that ASSBs with LiSH46 anode have the best cycle stability and lithium dendrite suppression at

high current density, while LiSS46 and LiSG46 anodes cannot prevent soft short circuit or maintain stable cycling or high reversible capacity.

XRD patterns in Fig. 3f show that the crystallinity of Gr is the highest while HC is the lowest. The HC has broadest interlayer spacing due to the smallest θ, while interlayer spacing of Gr is smallest. Furthermore, $\mathsf { L i C } _ { 6 }$ formed in all three carbons by in situ reaction between carbon film and Li foil under pressure, with interlayer spacing increasing from LiGr to LiSC to LiHC, as the $\mathsf { L i C } _ { 6 }$ peak shifted to smaller angles (Fig. 3g). Therefore, HC exhibits better diffusion in the LiSi alloy anode than that of Gr and SC due to its largest interlayer spacing and its highest structural disorder.

SEM images of the initial Si, HC, SC and Gr (Supplementary Fig. 21) and particle size distribution of the three types of carbon (given by laser particle size analyser; Supplementary Fig. 22) show that the mean particle size of HC is smallest among all three carbons. The surface area of HC $( 4 . 1 6 2 \mathsf { m } ^ { 2 } \mathsf { g } ^ { - 1 } )$ is larger than SC $( 2 . 3 7 8 \mathsf { m } ^ { 2 } \mathsf { g } ^ { - 1 } )$ and Gr $( 1 . 1 8 2 \mathsf { m } ^ { 2 } \mathsf { g } ^ { - 1 } )$ as illustrated in Fig. 3h, indicating that HC can provide more sites for lithium deposition. After ball milling, the particle size of SG46 is larger than that of SH46 and SS46 (Supplementary Fig. 24). Additionally, HC has a small expansion rate of $1 \%$ during the ${ \mathsf { L } } { \mathsf { i } } ^ { + }$ (de)intercalation process, while those of Gr and SC are $10 \%$ and $1 - 1 0 \%$ (ref. 24), respectively. HC can effectively buffer the volume change and maintain stability of the LiSH46 anode.

Therefore, ASSBs using LiSG46 and LiSS46 encounter soft short circuit at high current densities, and ASSBs with LiSG46 also exhibit large polarization. In contrast, ASSBs with LiSH46 maintain a stable cycle under large current densities as it possesses large specific surface area, interlayer spacing and disordered structure to mitigate volume change, enhance lithium diffusion and provide numerous sites for uniform Li plating.

To further examine the electrochemical performance of LiSH46 anode in full cells, ASSBs with NMC811/LCO composite cathode and LiSH46 anode were fabricated. # Conclusions

A hard-carbon-stabilized Li–Si alloy anode is fabricated to realize long-term cycling at high loadings and high current densities with suppressed lithium dendrite growth. The LiSH anode was prepared by a simple press-induced reaction between Si-contained film and Li foil. The validity of the LiSH anode and the optimal weight ratio of Si and HC (4:6) were demonstrated according to phase field simulation on Li atom distribution and Li-ion distribution. The LiSH46 anode can prevent the rapid degradation of the Si anode and alleviate the soft short circuit of the LiSi anode. As verified by XRD, XPS, cross-section SEM and AES of the anode, an ionic-electronic Li-rich network permeates the anode, which enlarges the active area of the anode and consequently, the enhanced electrode kinetics or stability. ASSBs with a LiSH46 anode and LCO cathode demonstrate 30,000 cycles at a current density of $2 0 \mathsf { C } ( 1 4 . 6 4 \mathsf { m A c m ^ { - 2 } } )$ and an areal capacity 0.7 mAh cm−2. Moreover, the LiSH46 anode enables NMC811 ASSBs with 5,000 cycles at a loading

of 5.86 mAh cm−2 and a current density of $5 . 8 6 \mathsf { m A c m } ^ { - 2 }$ . An ultra-high discharge current density $( 1 5 5 . 3 \mathsf { m A c m ^ { - 2 } }$ ) is also achieved, with NMC811 loading of 6 mAh cm−2. Finally, ASSBs with a cell-level energy density of 263 Wh ${ \sf k g } ^ { - 1 }$ and an areal capacity of 16.92 mAh cm−2 are obtained from the 20 mAh cm−2 loading NMC811|LiSH46 full cell. The HC-stabilized Li–Si alloy anode demonstrates favourable electrode kinetics and stability and superior electrochemical performance, providing a promising method for commercialization of the ASSBs.

# Methods

# Materials preparation

$\mathsf { L i } _ { 6 } \mathsf { P S } _ { 5 } \mathsf { C l }$ powder was obtained by mechanically ball milling a stoichiometric mixture of Li2S (Alfa, $9 9 . 9 \%$ ), ${ \bf P } _ { 2 } \mathsf { S } _ { 5 }$ (Macklin, $9 9 \%$ ), and LiCl $( 9 9 . 9 \%$ , Aladdin) for 20 h at 600 r.p.m. under an argon atmosphere, followed by sintering at $5 5 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for 5 h in a sealed glass ampul. The silicon particles were obtained from Tianmu Energy Anode Materials Co. and used after ball milling. The hard carbon (HC) was obtained from HiNa Battery Technology Co., used as received for characterization and after ball milling for fabrication of the LiHC anode. Soft carbon (SC) was obtained from HiNa Battery Technology Co. and used as received. The graphite (Gr) was obtained from Tianmu Lake Institute of Advanced Energy Storage Technologies and used as received. The SH powders (SH82, SH64, SH46, SH28) were prepared by mechanically ball milling of Si and HC with weight ratio of 8:2, 6:4, 4:6, 2:8, respectively. The SS46 and SG 46 were prepared by ball milling Si with SC and Gr in a weight ratio of 4:6, respectively. The polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) binder used for the cathode and anode was obtained from Daikin Industries and used as received. Vapour grow carbon fibre (VGCF) used for the cathode composite was obtained from Showa Denko K.K. and used as received.

$\operatorname { L i } _ { 1 . 5 } \mathsf { A l } _ { 0 . 5 } \mathsf { T i } _ { 1 . 5 } ( \mathsf { P O } _ { 4 } ) _ { 3 } ( \mathsf { L } i$ ATP)-coated Lithium nickel cobalt manganese oxide (NMC811) was obtained from Beijing Welion New Energy Technology Co. via fluid-bed technology. $\mathsf { L i } _ { 2 } \mathsf { Z r O } _ { 3 } .$ -protected $\mathsf { L i C o O } _ { 2 }$ was prepared by sol-gel method based on ${ \bf { Z r O } } _ { 2 }$ coating, detailed in previous works34.

# Anode preparation

The Si anode was prepared by repeated rolling of the mixture of Si and PTFE with a weight ratio of 95:5 to a uniform film with loading of $- 2 . 1 \mathsf { m g c m } ^ { - 2 }$ ; then the film was punched to discs with a diameter of $1 0 \mathsf { m m }$ .

To develop the Li–Si alloy-based anode and LiHC anode, Si-contained films and HC film were obtained first by repeated rolling the mixture of ball-milled powders and PTFE with a weight ratio of 95:5. The films were punched to discs with diameters of $1 0 \mathrm { m m }$ for cell assembly. The films used against the NMC811 cathode controlled the weight in a constant capacity 6.5 mAh cm−2. The weight and capacity are shown in Supplementary Table 1. When using the LCO cathode, the loading of SH46 was $- 2 . 2 \mathsf { m g c m } ^ { - 2 }$ . Li foil was pouched to discs with diameters of $8 \mathsf { m m }$ , and the weight of Li foil was dependent on the Li/ Si and Li/SH capacity ratio; in a typical cell, the Li/Si or Li/SH capacity ratio is above 0.8. The thickness of the Li foil is around $3 5 \mu \mathrm { m }$ in ASSBs with NMC811. The Li-contained anodes were in situ formed by short contact between Si-contained films and Li foils during cell assembly. During cycles, the reduction of PTFE may lead to anode structure degradation and Li loss due to PTFE decomposition. However, LiSH anodes were used in ASSBs where sufficient stress and sintering of Si can maintain the structure of the anodes. Additionally, pre-doping lithium can supplement Li loss. Moreover, PTFE is a commonly used binder with low cost in dry electrode preparation, which is easier to control the thickness of Si-contained films.

# Cathode preparation

To prepared the NMC811 electrodes, 79.4 wt% NMC811, 16.9 wt% Li P-${ \sf S } _ { 5 } { \sf C } { \sf I } _ { i }$ , 3 wt% VGCF and $0 . 7 \mathrm { w t \% }$ PTFE were first mixed to form a single flake. Subsequently, the flake was hot rolled repeatedly to a desired thickness and punched to $8 \mathsf { m m }$ diameter discs for cell assembly.

To prepared LCO electrodes, 59.7 wt% LCO, 39.8 wt% Li PS Cl and 0.5 wt% PTFE were first mixed to form a single flake. Subsequently, the flake was hot rolled repeatedly to a desired thickness and punched to 9 mm diameter discs for cell assembly.

# Electrochemical Characterization

Electronic conductivity of the anode was measured via a voltammetry test using a Zennium Pro Electrochemical Workstation by pressing the anode into Swagelok model cell. The Li diffusion coefficient was

calculated by potentiostatic intermittent titration technique (PITT); the potential step is $5 \mathsf { m V }$ , and each step lasted 30 min. Half cells paired with LiIn anode were used in PITT, and the test voltage range is $- 0 . 6 2 \ – 0 . 8 8 \lor .$ To fabricate the half cells to determine the capacity of the Si-contained film, a composite anode was first prepared with a mixture of $5 0 \mathrm { w t \% }$ active material (Si, HC or SH), $4 5 \mathsf { w t } \% \mathsf { L i } _ { 6 } \mathsf { P S } _ { 5 } \mathsf { C l }$ and $5 \mathrm { w t \% }$ VGCF. Finally, In foil and Li foil was attached on another side of SE, in turn, under pressure of 120 MPa (1 t). Thus, the four-layer pellet cell was obtained. Galvanostatic charge–discharge was conducted on the LANHE (CT2001A, LAND Electronic Co.) battery test system with voltage window set as −0.615–0.88 V (vs Li–In) and constant current density of $4 0 0 \mathsf { m } \mathsf { A } \mathsf { g } ^ { - 1 }$ .

# Electrochemical performance

Galvanostatic charge–discharge was conducted in a voltage range of 2.5–4.2 V via battery test system (LAND, CT2001A) at $5 5 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ . The 6 mAh $\mathsf { c m } ^ { - 2 }$ NMC811 full cells with various anodes (Si, LiHC, LiSH28, LiSH46, LiSH64, LiSH82, LiSi, LiSS46, LiSG46, SH46) were activated at 0.1 C for two cycles, and then the cycle test was performed at 1 C and the rate test was conducted at 0.2 C to 2 C. For the temperature range test, NMC811|LiSH46 full cells were cycled at 75, 55, 30 and $5 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ respectively at the same rate 0.1 C.

# Phase field simulation

A phase field with a nucleation model is introduced to simulate the transition of Li+ /Li. In the charging process, Li ion is either embedded in the electrode or deposited as dendrites into the electrolyte, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 16. The three main pathways are considered in the model to describe the relative reactions:

$$
1 5 \mathrm {L i} ^ {+} + 4 \mathrm {S i} + 1 5 \mathrm {e} ^ {-} \rightarrow \mathrm {L i} _ {1 5} \mathrm {S i} _ {4} E _ {1} ^ {\ominus} = 0. 0 5 \mathrm {V} \tag {1}
$$

$$
\mathrm {L i} ^ {+} + 6 \mathrm {C} + 6 \mathrm {e} ^ {-} \rightarrow \mathrm {L i C} _ {6} E _ {1} ^ {\ominus} = 0. 0 0 1 \mathrm {V} \tag {2}
$$

$$
\mathrm {L i} ^ {+} + \mathrm {e} ^ {-} \rightarrow \mathrm {L i} E _ {2} ^ {\ominus} = 0. 0 0 \mathrm {V} \tag {3}
$$

The second reaction (equation (2)) is the diffusion-driven reaction where the Li atom inserts into the anode. The lithium dendrite presents

in the third transition path. Three independent non-conserved order parameters $\scriptstyle { \vec { \xi } } = ( \xi _ { 0 } , \xi _ { 1 } , \xi _ { 2 } )$ denote the normalized concentration of species considered in the model. The value of ⃗ξ (0, 0, 0), (1, 0, 0), (0, 1, 0) and (0, 0, 1) represents the states of electrolyte, lithium metal, $\mathrm { L i C } _ { 6 }$ and Si electrode, respectively. In the model, the Li will transport from the near interface region towards the deeper electrode region with a diffusion-like pattern. The Li atom is in the form of LiC6 and ${ \mathsf { L i } } _ { 1 5 } { \mathsf { S i } } _ { 4 }$ inside the electrode. On the contrary, the Li atom at the surface of the anode tends to form Li metal dendrite, damaging ASSBs’ health and leading to short circuiting.

Assuming that the first reaction (equation (1)) occurs only at the interface between the electrode and the electrolyte, the lithium atoms are transported inside the carbon electrode by coupling and decoupling with the hard carbon. In the initial state, there are no lithium dendrites, while nucleation leads to the formation of small dendrites, and the dendrites grow during the charging process.

The evolution of the process obeys two key equations derived from the nonlinear phase field model:

$$

$$

$$
h \left(\xi_ {2}\right) \left[ \exp \frac {\left(1 - \alpha_ {1}\right) F \left(\eta - E _ {1} ^ {\ominus}\right)}{R T} - \frac {c _ {\mathrm {L i}} ^ {+}}{c _ {0}} \exp \frac {- \alpha_ {1} F \left(\eta - E _ {1} ^ {\ominus}\right)}{R T} \right]
$$

$$
\frac {\partial \xi_ {0}}{\partial t} = - L _ {\sigma 0} \left(\frac {\partial f _ {0}}{\partial \xi_ {0}} - \kappa_ {0} \nabla^ {2} \xi_ {0}\right) - L _ {\eta 0} h ^ {\prime} (\xi_ {0}) \left[ \exp \frac {(1 - \alpha_ {0}) F \eta}{R T} - \frac {c _ {\mathrm {L i} ^ {+}}}{c _ {0}} \exp \frac {- \alpha_ {0} F \eta}{R T} \right] \tag {5}

The nucleation process is restricted to occurring only near the interface. The nucleation process is described by a series of equations:

$$
\delta_ {\mathrm {N}} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l} 1, P _ {\mathrm {N}} = 1 - \exp (- J ^ {*} \Delta t) > \text {r a n d o m} [ 0, 1 ] \\ 1, \text {o t h e r w i s e} \end{array} \right. \tag {6}

$$

$$
\Delta G ^ {*} = \frac {1 6 \pi \gamma^ {3} V _ {m} ^ {2}}{3 \Delta \mu^ {2}} \tag {8}
$$

$$
\Delta \tilde {\mu} = - R T \ln \frac {\tilde {c} _ {L i +} \tilde {c} _ {e}}{a _ {\mathrm {L i}}} - F (E ^ {\Theta} - \Delta \varphi) \tag {9}
$$

where $P _ { \mathrm { { N } } }$ is a Poisson distribution whose expected value is the Li nucleation rate J * . Where $a _ { \mathrm { { L i } } }$ is the activity of Li metal atom, $E ^ { \Theta }$ is the standard half-cell potential, and Δφ is the interfacial potential difference between the electrode and the electrolyte.
