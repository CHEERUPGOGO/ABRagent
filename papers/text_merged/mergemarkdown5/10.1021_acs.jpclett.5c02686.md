ABSTRACT: Introducing functional electrolyte additives which could significantly improve solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) composition and lithium deposition morphology is the most economical and effective way to mitigate active lithium loss and improve the cycling performance of anode free lithium metal batteries (AFLMBs). However, due to the lack of real-time detection methods, the precise impact of these additives on lithium deposition/stripping processes and dead lithium evolution still remains unclear. In this work, the effects of fluoroethylene carbonate (FEC) additive in ester-based electrolytes along with $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ additive in ether-based electrolytes on lithium deposition and dead Li formation in $\mathrm{CuLLiFePO_4}$ batteries are investigated by in situ nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) in combinations with post-mortem scanning

electron microscopy (SEM). Although the FEC additive shows a negligible impact on initial lithium deposition, it effectively mitigates dead lithium accumulation by altering its growth kinetics from exponential to linear with the cycle repeated. Comparative studies in ether-based electrolytes reveal that $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ additive significantly affects deposition morphology by promoting dense lithium deposition. However, it fails to suppress dead lithium accumulation as the growth kinetics still follow an exponential trend with the cycle repeated. The failure mode of $\mathrm{CuIIFePO_4}$ batteries shifts from dead lithium dominated to SEI dominated after introducing FEC additive, while SEI dominated failure mode persists after introducing $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ additive. Our findings provide new insights into the role of FEC and $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ additives on the lithium metal deposition and dead lithium evolution, which could assist in improving the cycling performance of AFLMBs.

Lithium metal battery is regarded as a competitive selection for next-generation high-energy-density battery due to its low reduction potential $(-3.04\mathrm{V}$ vs standard hydrogen electrode) and exceptionally high theoretical specific capacity $(3860\mathrm{mAh}$ $\mathbf{g}^{-1})$ . However, capacity decay and safety risks caused by lithium dendrite growth and dead Li accumulation persistently hinder their practical applications. Electrolytes are pivotal components of anode-free lithium metal batteries (AFLMBs), as they determine lithium deposition morphology and the composition of the solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) layer formed on the lithium surface. By precisely tailoring electrolytes to induce uniform and smooth lithium deposition, the growth of high-surface-area "mossy" lithium and undesirable side reactions between the electrolyte and metallic lithium could be suppressed.

Ester- and ether-based electrolytes are the two dominant organic electrolyte categories for AFLMBs.[11,12] Traditional carbonate ester-based electrolytes exhibit high conductivity, strong wettability, and robust oxidation stability ( $>4.2\mathrm{V}$ vs $\mathrm{Li^{+} / Li}$ ), making them ideal candidates for high-voltage AFLMBs.[13,14] However, challenges persist in carbonate ester-based electrolytes, including their high reactivity with lithium metal, chemical instability, and transition metal dissolution at high voltages.[15-19] Compared to carbonate-based electrolytes, ether-based electrolytes demonstrate better compatibility with

lithium metal but face the problem of oxidative decomposition above $4.0\mathrm{V}$ , rendering them incompatible with high-voltage intercalation-type cathodes.[20-22] On the other side, uncontrolled dendritic or mossy lithium deposition on copper foils and continuous active lithium consumption during repeated stripping/deposition cycles severely hinder the practical application of AFLMBs in both ester-based and ether-based electrolytes.[23,24] The consumption of lithium primarily stems from two sources: chemical instability between lithium metal and the electrolyte, leading to lithium incorporation as lithium ions $(\mathrm{Li}^{+})$ in the SEI; physical/mechanical instability during lithium stripping, which detaches dendritic lithium from the bulk metal or current collector, forming electrochemically inactive lithium $(\mathrm{Li}_{0})$ .[25]

influenced by operating conditions. Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) technology, as a nondestructive and quantitative detection method, is highly effective for in situ monitoring of the lithium deposition process, including the study of its reaction kinetics and the formation of microstructural lithium. $^{31-33}$ Due to the characteristic Knight shift of lithium NMR, it is easy to distinguish between "mossy" and "dendritic" lithium metals; their Knight shifts are located at 260 and $270~\mathrm{ppm}$ or even higher, while the bulk lithium metal is situated between 240 and $250~\mathrm{ppm}$ .

In this work, the effects of FEC additive in ester-based electrolytes along with $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ additive in ether-based electrolytes on lithium deposition and dead Li formation in Cull

$\mathrm{LiFePO_4}$ batteries are investigated by a self-developed in situ NMR in combinations with post-mortem scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Results show that while FEC has no significant effect on initial lithium deposition, the LiF-rich SEI effectively slows the growth of dead Li by transforming its growth mode from exponential to linear. In contrast, $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ additive notably improves lithium deposition morphology but fails to suppress dead Li formation during early cycles, where growth of dead Li still follows exponential trend. However, dead Li saturation is observed with the cycle repeated. These findings provide new insights into the mechanisms of different additives on lithium deposition and dead Li evolution in AFLMBs.

The basic ester electrolyte (EC/DEC) consisted of $1\mathrm{M}$ $\mathrm{LiPF_6}$ dissolved in ethylene carbonate (EC) and diethyl carbonate (DEC) $(1:1\mathrm{v / v})$ . Advanced ester electrolyte (EC/DEC+FEC) incorporated 5 wt % FEC additive. For ether-based systems, the basic electrolyte (DOL/DME) contained 1 M lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) in 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) and 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) $(1:1\mathrm{v / v})$ while the modified version $(\mathrm{DOL} / \mathrm{DME} + \mathrm{LiNO}_3)$ added 2 wt $\%$ $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ . All electrolytes were purchased from Duoduo Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. The lithium iron phosphate $(\mathrm{LiFePO_4})$ cathode (areal capacity: $8.67~\mathrm{mg~cm^{-2}}$ ) was sourced from Guangdong Kelude New Energy Technology Co., Ltd. Cathodes, copper foils $(25\mu \mathrm{m}$ thickness), were cut into specified dimensions of $5\mathrm{mm}\times 10\mathrm{mm}$ , while PP separators (Cegard 3500, $25\mu \mathrm{m}$ ) and glass fiber separators (Whatman GF/D, $6.75~\mathrm{mm}$ ) were cut into specified dimensions of $6\mathrm{mm}\times 12\mathrm{mm}$ . Then all the materials were dried under vacuum at $100^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ for $12\mathrm{h}$ and transferred to an argon-filled glovebox for storage.

The in situ NMR test cells were assembled in a cylindrical polyetheretherketone (PEEK) mold as shown in Figure S1. Each cell was filled with $50\mu \mathrm{L}$ of electrolyte. To ensure airtight sealing and prevent air exposure during testing, ultraviolet (UV)-curable adhesive was applied to the mold seams within an argon-filled glovebox. All electrochemical tests were conducted at $25^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ under a voltage window of $2.8 - 3.8\mathrm{V}$ , using a constant current density of $1\mathrm{mAcm}^{-2}$ as detected by LAND CT-20001A tester.

In situ $^{7}\mathrm{Li}$ NMR NMR spectra were acquired on a Bruker Ascend 400WB spectrometer (9.4 T) equipped with a custom-built probe containing a $12\mathrm{mm}$ solenoid coil as shown in Figure S2. The sample chamber of probe incorporated a $12\mathrm{mm}$ solenoid coil, and all experiments were performed with the NMR circuit tuned to $155.6\mathrm{MHz}$ . $^{7}\mathrm{Li}$ NMR spectra were acquired via a single-pulse excitation of $5.0~\mu \mathrm{s}$ , corresponding to a flip angle of $30^{\circ}$ and a relaxation delay of $0.25\mathrm{s}$ . Each spectrum requires 512 scans to achieve sufficient signal-to-noise ratio, resulting in a total acquisition time of $152\mathrm{s}$ . The calibration of Knight shift of $^{7}\mathrm{Li}$ was referenced to a $1\mathrm{M}$ LiCl aqueous solution $(0\mathrm{ppm})$ . Probe retuning was performed during $5\mathrm{min}$ equilibrium intervals after each charge/discharge step to compensate for circuit drift.

The ratio of dead Li and SEI is calculated from the data acquired by NMR and electrochemistry measurements. According to previous study,[37] the detailed calculation method is shown below. First, the total irreversible capacity $C_{\text{irreversible}}$ is obtained from the subtraction between charging capacity $C_{\text{charge}}$ and discharging capacity $C_{\text{discharge}}$ which is

$$
C _ {\text {i r r e v e r s i b l e}} = C _ {\text {c h a r g e}} - C _ {\text {d i s c h a r g e}} \tag {1}
$$

Here, $C_{\text{irreversible}}$ is assumed to reflect the total amount of dead Li and SEI. The capacity loss due to the formation of SEI $C_{\text{SEI}}$ and the capacity related to the formation of dead Li $C_{\text{dead Li}}$ can then be calculated as below:

$$
C _ {\mathrm {S E I}} = C _ {\text {i r r e v e r s i b l e}} - C _ {\text {d e a d L i}} \tag {2}
$$

The $C_{\mathrm{irreversible}}$ , $C_{\mathrm{charge}}$ and $C_{\mathrm{discharge}}$ can be obtained according to the calculation method reported before.37 Thus, the capacity of SEI and dead Li can be calculated through the above equations. The proportion of SEI $\phi_{\mathrm{SEI}}$ and dead Li $\phi_{\mathrm{dead~Li}}$ can be calculated as below:

$$
\phi_ {\mathrm {S E I}} = \frac {C _ {\mathrm {S E I}}}{C _ {\text {c h a r g e}}} \tag {3}
$$

$$
\phi_ {\text {d e a d L i}} = \frac {C _ {\text {d e a d L i}}}{C _ {\text {c h a r g e}}} \tag {4}
$$

The relative proportion of dead Li over SEI can be acquired from the following equation:

$$
\frac {\phi_ {\text {d e a d L i}}}{\phi_ {\text {S E I}}} = \frac {C _ {\text {d e a d L i}}}{C _ {\text {S E I}}} \tag {5}
$$

Figure 1 panels a and b present the contour plots of in situ $^7\mathrm{Li}$ NMR spectra during the first charge-discharge cycle of $\mathrm{CuIIFePO_4}$ full cells employing ester-based electrolytes without (Figure 1a) and with (Figure 1b) FEC additive. The temporal change of the peak position during deposition/ stripping is tracked by green dashed lines. The white dashed line at $252~\mathrm{ppm}$ represents the Knight shift of metallic lithium. The resonance peak near $252~\mathrm{ppm}$ corresponds to densely deposited bulk lithium (b-Li), while the resonance peak deviating significantly from the left side of $252~\mathrm{ppm}$ corresponds to lithium microstructures (LMs) with noncompact morphologies. Notably, both systems exhibit an incomplete resonance shift to the b-Li position upon full charging, suggesting that the lithium deposits heterogeneously in ester electrolytes regardless of the presence of FEC additive. As shown in Figure 1a,b, the resonance peaks show rightward shifts during lithium plating and leftward shifts during stripping. Regardless of the presence of FEC additive, the Knight shift of the resonance peak is always larger than $252~\mathrm{ppm}$ , which corresponds to the formation of LMs. Figure 1 panels c and d present the contour plots of in situ $^7\mathrm{Li}$ NMR spectra during the first charge-discharge cycle of $\mathrm{CuIIFePO_4}$ full cells employing ether-based electrolytes without (Figure 1c) and with (Figure 1d) $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ additive. As shown in Figure 1c, the lithium deposition peaks fail to reach the b-Li position, reflecting the noncompact morphologies of lithium. On the contrary, the addition of $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ can greatly improve the cycling performance of $\mathrm{CuIIFePO_4}$ cells compared with FEC additive, as shown in Figure S2. As shown in Figure 1d, the peak position moves rapidly to the right during the initial $30~\mathrm{min}$ , indicative of accelerated lateral growth and densified lithium nucleation. This enables final deposition peaks to reach the b-Li position (252 ppm), which is in contrast with the noncompact morphologies observed in Figure 1c. However, the stripping processes remain largely unaffected by $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ addition, suggesting $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ additive-specific modulation of the deposition process rather than the stripping process.

For ester-based electrolytes, as shown in Figure 1e, the NMR resonance peak of deposited lithium without FEC additive is centered at 261.37 ppm. Notably, this peak position deviates significantly from the Knight shift of bulk lithium (i.e., 252 ppm), indicating noncompact lithium deposition morphology in the absence of FEC. By introducing FEC as shown in Figure 1f, the peak shifts slightly to 260.11 ppm, suggesting minimal alteration in the first-cycle deposition behavior. Despite this minor displacement, the overall peak position remains largely unchanged, implying that FEC exerts limited influence on the initial deposition morphology of lithium. Notably, when the sample is situated in a magnetic field as studied by NMR, lithium metal tends to grow along the direction of the external

magnetic field and its Knight shift exhibits a leftward deviation.36 Therefore, the peak of dendritic lithium has a lower Knight shift value (272 ppm) due to anisotropic electronic shielding, while the peak of m-Li also has a lower Knight shift value (263 ppm) as lithium metal growth is oriented at an angle relative to the magnetic field and accompanied by a higher aggregation density.39-41 Considering the anisotropic dipole-dipole interaction between the lithium atom in the solid state, the NMR spectra can be deconvoluted into three overlapping peaks corresponding to distinct lithium morphologies: the peak near 254 ppm closely aligned with the bulk lithium (b-Li), represents densely deposited lithium; the peak at 263 ppm arises from mossy lithium (m-Li); the peak at 272 ppm originates from dendritic lithium (d-Li).34-36 Figure 1g exhibits the relative ratio of three deconvoluted peak areas in $\mathrm{CuLLiFePO_4}$ full batteries

employing ester-based electrolytes with or without an FEC additive. Without FEC additive, d-Li dominates (33%), while b-Li constitutes only 25%. With FEC additive, the d-Li decreases to 18%, and b-Li increases to 41%, whereas m-Li remains around 40%. Additionally, the Knight shift of lithium deposition peak changes little from 261.37 to 260.11 ppm by introducing FEC, which suggests the total morphologies of both samples are quite different from that of bulk lithium. These results suggest that introducing FEC additive could suppress the growth of dendritic lithium and enhanced dense lithium deposition fail to fundamentally restructure the deposition morphology of lithium. Figure 1 panels h-j exhibit the relative proportions of three deconvoluted peak areas in $\mathrm{CuIIFePO_4}$ full batteries employing ether-based electrolytes with or without $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ additive. As shown in Figure 1h, the lithium deposition peak without $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ additive appears at 259.80 ppm, still distinct from that of bulk lithium, indicating a loosely packed lithium morphology. The ratio of d-Li is 10%, and the ratio of b-Li is 32%, whereas the ratio of m-Li is 58% as shown in Figure 1j. The low d-Li content is attributed to the inherent affinity of ether-based electrolytes for lithium, which promotes uniform nucleation.[42] Remarkably, introducing $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ shifts the lithium deposition peak to 253.58 ppm near the bulk lithium peak as shown in Figure 1i, with d-Li reduced to 4% and b-Li increased to 63%. This suggests that the $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ additive can optimize the lithium deposition morphology by suppressing the growth of dendritic lithium and promoting the dense lithium deposition of the first cycle.

Furthermore, the effects of additives on the deposition of lithium and morphology are investigated during the first five charge/discharge cycles. Figure 2 panels a-d show the ${}^{7}\mathrm{Li}$ NMR spectra acquired after plating at first five cycles in Cull $\mathrm{LiFePO_4}$ batteries under different electrolyte conditions. The relative proportions of three deconvoluted peak areas corresponding to the deposition peak are shown in Figure $2\mathrm{e}-\mathrm{h}$ . As shown in Figure 2a, the lithium deposition peak appears at $261.37~\mathrm{ppm}$ in the first cycle and shifts slightly to $259.62~\mathrm{ppm}$ in the second cycle, indicating denser deposition. With cycle number increased, it shifts to a higher Knight shift value and reaches $263.70~\mathrm{ppm}$ at the fifth cycle, suggesting a looser deposition morphology. Such a phenomenon is also proved by the deconvoluted analysis as shown in Figure 2e. At the first two cycles, the b-Li content increases slightly and the d-Li decreases slightly. After the second cycle, the b-Li content decreases sharply, and d-Li content increases; the LMs dominates the morphology. Thus, it can be inferred that the solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) formed by the reactions between electrolyte and lithium metal exhibits limited mechanical strength and can only provide transient protection to the deposited lithium in the cell employing ester-based electrolytes without FEC additive. By introducing FEC additive, the deposition peak during the first five cycles is shown in Figure 2b. The deposition peak shifts rightward to $255.86~\mathrm{ppm}$ at the first three cycle, approaching bulk lithium NMR signals, suggesting improved SEI stability and denser deposition. From the fourth cycle, the deposition peak shifts leftward to $260.18~\mathrm{ppm}$ at the fifth cycle, suggesting FEC depletion and SEI failure under repeated cycles. As shown in Figure 2f, deconvolution results reveal that the b-Li content decreases sharply after the third cycle, while the d-Li content gradually decreases to a much smaller value in the first three cycles but subsequently increases rapidly from the fourth cycle. This indicates that introducing FEC could suppress the growth

of d-Li in the early cycles but the FEC-derived SEI gradually loses its ability to adapt to the volume fluctuations during lithium metal deposition and stripping, thus triggering growth of d-Li in subsequent cycles. Moreover, the m-Li content decreases abruptly in the first two cycles and then gradually increases after the second cycle, which indicates that the FEC-derived SEI effectively suppresses m-Li formation in early cycles. These results demonstrate that FEC additive could suppress the formation of d-Li and m-Li, but the FEC-derived SEI is not stable enough and will lose its ability to protect lithium after five cycles. Figure 2c shows the lithium deposition peak acquired in the $\mathrm{CullLiFePO_4}$ cell employing an ether-based electrolyte without $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ additive. The deposition lithium peak continuously shifts leftward, indicating increasingly loose lithium deposition morphology with cycle number increased. As shown in Figure 2g, the b-Li content decreases rapidly to below $10\%$ while the d-Li content increases to above $40\%$ with cycle number increased. This suggests the SEI formed in ether-based electrolyte is not stable enough to suppress the formation of d-Li. After introducing the $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ additive, the deposition lithium peak stabilizes at around $253.58~\mathrm{ppm}$ from the third cycle as shown in Figure 2d, reflecting denser lithium deposition due to SEI stabilization. Notably, a left-shifted shoulder peak emerges and gradually intensifies from the third cycle, which is attributed to the increased content of m-Li and d-Li. As shown in Figure 2h, deconvolution results reveal that the b-Li content decreases rapidly in the first three cycles and then becomes stable afterward while the d-Li content and m-Li content increase in the first three cycles and then become stable afterward. Thus, the introduction of the $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ additive could change the dynamic equilibrium between b-Li and LMs with the cycle number increased. Notably, the d-Li content with $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ additive is smaller than that without $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ additive but higher than that with FEC additive, which suggests that the $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ -derived SEI could also suppress the growth of d-Li but the ability is not as effective as the FEC-derived SEI.

To investigate the impact of the lithium deposition morphology on dead lithium formation in subsequent discharge processes, we show the $^7\mathrm{Li}$ NMR spectra acquired after stripping in Figure 2i-1. The resonance peaks reflect the formation of dead lithium. As shown in Figure 2i, the peak area slightly decreases in the first two cycles, reflecting a decrease of dead lithium ratio from $12.2\%$ and then increases after the second cycle, reflecting an increase of dead lithium content in the cell without FEC additive. By introducing FEC additive, the peak area remains small reflecting a tiny amount of dead lithium which is around $2.0\%$ as shown in Figure 2j. Thus, it can be inferred that the introduction of FEC additive could suppress dead lithium growth despite the looser morphology in the $\mathrm{CuIIFePO_4}$ batteries employing ester-based electrolytes. Figure 2 panels k and l reveal the rapid growth of dead lithium in the $\mathrm{CuIIFePO_4}$ batteries employing ether-based electrolytes regardless of the usage of $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ additive. The growth modes of dead Li in both situations show little difference and the ratio of dead Li is nearly the same ( $4.7\%$ vs $4.0\%$ ). This suggests that the growth of dead Li is just slightly influenced by the deposition morphology and the usage of the $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ additive in the $\mathrm{CuIIFePO_4}$ batteries employing ether-based electrolytes.

The above analysis indicates that both FEC and $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ additives could effectively reduce the dead Li content, but their mechanisms are quite different. The initial nucleation behavior of lithium acts as a "template" for subsequent deposition and also governs the chemical composition and spatial distribution of the nascent solid electrolyte interphase. Studies demonstrate that advanced electrolyte formulations facilitate the formation of an SEI with highly consistent chemistry on both lithium metal and copper current collector surfaces.[53] Such compositional homogeneity ensures uniform interfacial lithium-ion transport during plating and stripping, which promotes spatially homogeneous lithium dissolution and helps avoid localized isolation, often referred to as "pinch-off" that leads to dead lithium formation.[54] Conversely, nonuniform SEI formation and heterogeneous nucleation result in loose, porous, or dendritic lithium morphologies from the initial cycle. This inhomogeneity is further amplified in subsequent cycling: dendritic tips are prone to fracture, generating dead lithium, while the high-surface-area porous structures continuously consume electrolyte and foster a thicker, less stable SEI, collectively accelerating active lithium loss. As shown in Figure 1a,b, FEC additives show minor influences on lithium metal deposition/stripping behavior in the first cycle, different from prior studies which suggests that FEC promote denser lithium deposition.[44] In addition, the formation of SEI consumes $16.1\%$ of capacity in the first cycle with FEC compared with that consumes $4.4\%$ of capacity without FEC as shown in Figure 4b, which suggests the introduction of FEC additive promotes the growth of SEI during the first cycle along with modifying SEI composition. With the cycle repeated to the third cycle, the deposition lithium peak shifts rightward to $255.86~\mathrm{ppm}$ , approaching bulk lithium NMR signals, suggesting denser deposition as shown in Figure 2b. Despite the minor influence on the deposition and stripping during first cycle, FEC additive promotes a denser lithium deposition during the second and third cycle by inducing the formation of LiF-rich SEI,[45,46] since the LiF-rich SEI has a higher mechanical strength which could mitigate volume fluctuations and suppress dendritic growth.[47,48] In contrast, the SEI formed without FEC additive weakens rapidly, leading to leftward peak shifts as shown in Figure 2a and dendritic lithium proliferation as shown in Figure 3b,c. After the fourth cycle, the growth modes of SEI aligns regardless of the usage of FEC additive as shown in Figure 4b, reflecting FEC depletion after the first five cycles. The variations in lithium metal deposition behavior also lead to different formation of dead Li during cycling. While initial deposition/stripping remains unaffected, the dead Li peak becomes smaller in the first cycle with FEC as shown in Figure 2j, which suggests FEC reduces dead lithium accumulation. Previous study has shown the effect of FEC additive on suppressing dead Li growth in the first five cycle.[41] In this study, the knowledge of FEC additive is renewed as the investigated cycle number is extended to 25. In summary, the effects of FEC and $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ additives on lithium metal deposition and inactive lithium evolution of Cull $\mathrm{LiFePO_4}$ cells employing ether-based and ester-based electro

lyte are studied through in situ NMR. The FEC additive shows no impact on the first-cycle lithium deposition/stripping behavior. However, in the second and third cycles, the LiF-rich SEI layer effectively induces dense lithium deposition. After the third cycle, FEC fails to suppress dendrite growth. The $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ additive significantly improves lithium deposition morphology during the first charging cycle and partially enables reversible lithium deposition in subsequent cycles, though localized dendrite growth persists. Both FEC and $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ reduce the dead Li content throughout the lifespan of $\mathrm{CuIIFePO_4}$ batteries. FEC shifts dead Li growth from exponential to linear, achieving a final dead lithium content of $29.5\%$ after 25 cycles. $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ delays dead lithium mitigation until midcycle, stabilizing at $31.3\%$ after 35 cycles. With FEC, the failure mode of batteries shifts from dead Li-dominated to SEI-dominated. The failure mode of $\mathrm{CuIIFePO_4}$ cells employing ester-based electrolyte consistently maintains SEI-dominated regardless of usage of the $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ additive. Our findings provide new insights into the role of FEC and $\mathrm{LiNO}_3$ additives on lithium metal deposition and inactive lithium formation, which assists to improve the cycling performance of AFLMBs.
