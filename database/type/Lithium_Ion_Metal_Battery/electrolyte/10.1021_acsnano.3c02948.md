Note: The Li-OPFPN and Li-NPFPN pairs in Figure S2a indicate the stable coordination between PFPN and $\mathrm { L i ^ { + } }$ . Owing to the steric hindrance of PFPN compresses the effective space within the solvation, the local concentration of anions and solvents are all increased, leading to the increase in RDF. However, since PFPN occupies a lot of coordination space originally belonging to DME or FEC and significantly reduces the binding energy, the coordination numbers of DME and FEC are reduced inevitably. For anions in solvation, because of the electrostatic interaction between anion and $\mathrm { L i ^ { + } }$ is stronger than that of weakened solvent, the coordination of anions will not be squeezed by steric hindrance, and the coordination number will increase due to the increased local concentration.

# 1. INTRODUCTION

Lithium metal anode, with its high theoretical specific capacity $(3860\mathrm{mAhg}^{-1})$ and low electrochemical potential $(-3.04\mathrm{V}$ vs. standard hydrogen electrode), is expected to replace the graphite-based anodes to overcome the energy density limit of commercialized lithium-ion batteries (LIBs). $^{1-3}$ When paired with high-voltage cathodes $(>4.0\mathrm{V}$ vs. $\mathrm{Li^{+} / Li}$ ), the energy density of lithium-metal batteries (LMBs) potentially becomes twice that of LIBs. $^{4}$ Unfortunately, the progress of high-voltage LMBs is obstructed by the poor reversibility of Li metal, aggressive dendrite growth, and poor stability of high-voltage cathode. These problems are mainly derived from the low thermodynamic stability of electrolyte and the inadequate interphases. $^{5-9}$ Therefore, various strategies have been explored, among which electrolyte engineering has attracted extensive attention due to its practicability in manufacturing. $^{10,11}$

Plentiful works on electrolyte engineering emphasized the pivotal role of $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ solvation structure and its significant influence on interfacial chemistry. $^{12-14}$ In the traditional electrolyte, $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ ions are mostly coordinated with solvent

molecules to form a solvent-separated ion pairs (SSIP) structure, which induces an organics-rich interphase derived from solvents and leads to poor performances for LMBs. $^{15-18}$ Increasing the salt concentrations can force the anions enter into solvation to create contact ion pairs (CIP) and aggregated ions (AGG) structures in the electrolyte, which can generate an anion-derived interphase to improve Li metal stability. Nevertheless, the high viscosity and high cost of the high concentration electrolyte (HCE) hinder its practical application. $^{19-22}$ By adding inert diluents, a localized high concentration electrolyte (LHCE) is proposed to reduce the viscosity while retaining the solvation structure of HCE. $^{23-27}$ Using weak solvents to weaken the $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ -solvent coordination is another efficient tuning strategy, as this weakly solvating

Such an inorganic-rich SEI has a superior ability to isolate electrons and lower resistance to $\mathrm{Li}^{+}$ diffusion, resulting in faster $\mathrm{Li}^{+}$ transfer kinetics, suppressed corrosion reactions, inhibited lithium dendrites growth, and improved Li metal reversibility.

2.3. Oxidation Stability and Full-Cell Performance. After investigating the Li metal compatibility, we further explored the stability on the high-voltage cathode. Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) was conducted on Li/Al cells to unveil the oxidation stability of investigated electrolytes (Figure 5a). The poor oxidation stability of ether solvents leads to sudden anodic current increase below $4.0\mathrm{~V}$ in $1\mathrm{M}$ LiTFSI in DME. Forming a protective CEi can effectively isolate electrons and reduce the oxidation of solvents.[49] Thereby, relying on the CEi-forming ability of additives, E-DFOB/FEC broadens its electrochemical stability window to $4.5\mathrm{~V}$ , but it is still insufficient to enable the ultrahigh-voltage LMBs $(>4.5\mathrm{~V}$ vs. $\mathrm{Li}^{+}/\mathrm{Li})$ . Surprisingly, with E-PFPN, there are no obvious oxidation current increases until $5.1\mathrm{~V}$ . Floating tests were employed by continuously exposing the cathode at $4.5\mathrm{~V}$ and recording steady leakage current to quantify the rate of the parasitic reaction. The cell using E-PFPN reaches a minimum current value of $2.0\mu\mathrm{A}$ , which is significantly lower than that of $\mathrm{LiPF_6/EC/DEC}$ and E-DFOB/FEC (Figure 5b).[50] A self-discharge test was conducted by recording the open-circuit voltage of a fully charged cells to indicate the reactivity of electrolytes with cathodes.[50] As shown in Figure S13, the cell with E-PFPN exhibits less voltage decrease than $\mathrm{LiPF_6/EC/DEC}$ and E-DFOB/FEC. These observations from floating tests and self-discharge tests indicate E-PFPN has favorable oxidation stability and can effectively suppress the interfacial parasitic reaction. Therefore, LMBs with E-PFPN is qualified for the ultrastable and ultrahigh voltage cycling (Figure S14). With a cutoff voltage of $4.5\mathrm{~V}$ , the Li/NMC811 half-cells with E-PFPN achieved a discharge capacity of 218.1 mAh g $^{-1}$ at $0.5\mathrm{~C}$ and retained $90.8\%$ capacity even after 300 cycles, while that of E-DFOB/FEC is only $72.6\%$ . Even at an ultrahigh voltage of $4.6\mathrm{~V}$ , E-PFPN still enabled stable cycling over 200 cycles with a capacity retention of $81.5\%$ .

Li metal full-cells were further assembled under aggressive cycling conditions. Li/NMC811 full-cells using E-PFPN achieved superior cycling performance at $4.5\mathrm{V}$ with a high charge and discharge currents of 0.8 and $1.2\mathrm{mA}\mathrm{cm}^{-2}$ , which delivered a capacity retention of $89.2\%$ after 180 cycles. In contrast, cells with E-DFOB/FEC could only operate stably for 60 cycles and degraded after 140 cycles (Figure 5c). At an ultrahigh voltage of $4.6\mathrm{V}$ , the full-cells with E-PFPN provide $84.1\%$ initial capacity after 150 cycles with a high average CE of $>99.56\%$ (Figure 5d), while in E-DFOB/FEC, cells show a massive capacity fading after 100 cycles and a low average CE of $97.2\%$ . Despite the harsher testing conditions, our designed

electrolyte shows higher capacity retention, higher energy efficiency and more stable voltage profiles than control electrolyte (Figure S15). The ultrahigh voltage cycling performance of the Li/NMC811 full-cells using E-PFPN is observed among the top levels to the best of our knowledge.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) test was further conducted. The impedance of cells in $\mathrm{LiPF_6 / EC / DEC}$ or E-DFOB/FEC increased obviously during cycling, probably resulting from the gradual broken of unstable interphase (Figures 5e and S16).46 By comparison, the impedance of cells using E-PFPN was lower and decreased with the increase of cycling number. This suggests the interphases generated from E-PFPN have superior ion conductivity and becomes more and more compact during the cycling (Figure 5f). To study the impedance of the CEI layer without the interference of SEI, EIS tests were further used in cathode/cathode symmetric cells (Figure 5g).51 The cycled cathodes were reassembled to form NMC811/NMC811 symmetric cells using the same E-DFOB/ FEC baseline electrolyte (Figure S17). At the earlier 50 cycles, the impedance of symmetric cells was all relatively small, meaning both electrolytes can form an effectively CEI. Nonetheless, with the cycles increases (200 cycles), the resistance increased rapidly in the cells originally using E-DFOB/FEC while that of E-PFPN showed relatively stable resistance. This illustrates the CEI generated in E-DFOB/FEC will be damaged in a long-term cycle, which probably due to the consumption of additives, whereas E-PFPN is able to form CEI with persistent protection to preserve the stability of cathodes. Combined with the impedance results of full-cells, we found the impedance of symmetric cells is almost twice of that of full-cells in E-DFOB/FEC, which proves the impedance increase on the cathode side is the key reason for the decay of cells using E-DFOB/FEC. However, compared with the rapid impedance increase of full-cells using $\mathrm{LiPF_6 / EC / DEC}$ , the impedance of symmetrical cells increases slightly. This indicates the impedance increase in $\mathrm{LiPF_6 / EC / DEC}$ is largely attributed to the poor Li metal reversibility (Figures S16 and S18). Only the cells with E-PFPN can maintain lower impedance, which contributes to better $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ transport and charge transfer properties.52,53 These advantages of E-PFPN also bring about a favorable rate capacity.54,55 The Li/NMC cell with E-PFPN delivers a high discharge capacity of 156.5 mAh $\mathbf{g}^{-1}$ at $5.0\mathrm{C}$ rate, whereas that of the $\mathrm{LiPF_6 / EC / DEC}$ system was limited to only $80.9\mathrm{mAhg^{-1}}$ (Figure S19). The rate capacity of E-PFPN also superior to analogous LHCE, WSE, or FWSE (Table S4),12,22,23,29 which highlights the importance of the "bulky coordinating" solvation in $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ transfer kinetics.

Summarily, we concluded that E-PFPN has a better oxidation stability and can form a protective CEI with low resistance on the NMC cathode. Thus, it realizes the outstanding cycling performance of ultrahigh voltage LMBs.

In the aim to make certain the influence of the "bulky coordinating" solvation in the CEI forming process, XPS analysis was applied to investigate the detailed compositions of the CEI on NMC811 after 50 cycles at $4.6\mathrm{V}$ . The spectra of NMC811 cycled in E-DFOB/FEC were shown in Figure 6i. On account of the decomposition of solvents, apparent signals of $\mathrm{C - O}$ and $\mathrm{C = O}$ (288.6 eV) species can be observed in the C 1s spectrum, which agrees well with the O 1s spectrum (C-O at 533.2 eV and $\mathrm{C = O}$ at 531.6 eV). The signal of the metal oxide bond (M-O at 529.4 eV) is not detected at the first sputtering (0 s) due to the excessively thick CEI. The feeble signals of LiF and $\mathrm{Li}_3\mathrm{N}$ are derived from the slight decomposition of anions.[23] These XPS results demonstrate

that an organic-rich CEI was generated in E-DFOB/PEC, which cannot suppress aggressive attacks from solvent decomposition and become uneven and thicken during its continuously destruction. However, for the CEI formed in E-PFPN (Figure 6j), although C-O and C=O species were also found in the C 1s spectrum, their content is obviously less than that in E-DFOB/PEC, indicating the solvent decomposition is efficiently suppressed in E-PFPN. This should be attributed to the "bulky coordinating" solvation weakening the $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ -solvent coordination and decreasing the polarization within the DME and FEC.[61] In F 1s and N 1s spectra, higher LiF and $\mathrm{LiN}_x\mathrm{O}_y$ content was detected on the cathode surface in E-PFPN, demonstrating that the anion decomposition is facilitated in E-PFPN. This could be ascribed to the "bulky coordinating" solvation increasing the probability of anions appearing at the interface and promoting the anions to participate in the CEI formation. These changes have great beneficial influence on cathode stability owing to the excellent electronic insulation of LiF and advantageous ion conducting property of $\mathrm{LiN}_x\mathrm{O}_y$ .[37] In addition, PFPN itself also has a film-forming ability.[36] In the O 1s, F 1s, N 1s, and P 2p spectra, peaks corresponding to N-O/ P-O, $\mathrm{Li}_x\mathrm{PF}_y\mathrm{NO}_2^-$ , N=P-N, and $\mathrm{Li}_x\mathrm{PO}_y$ are associated with the decomposition products of PFPN. These inorganic compounds derived from PFPN are generally believed to have superior ion conductivity, leading to fast ionic transport and low interphase impedance.[37,51] Owing to the fact that the CEI generated in E-PFPN is rich in LiF and N,P-containing inorganic compounds, parasitic reactions can be effectively suppressed at ultrahigh voltage. Thus, the signal of M-O can be observed even at the first sputtering (0 s), indicating an ultrathin CEI layer is formed in E-PFPN. Energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) was also performed to show the uniform distribution of F, N, and P elements in cathode surface, which agrees with the XPS analysis results (Figure S27).[51]

Overall, since the "bulky coordinating" solvation in E-PFPN presents as anion-dominated rather than solvent-dominated, the irreversible decomposition of solvents is significantly restrained and more anions participate in the CEI formation process. Combining with the film-forming effect of PFPN, abundant $\mathrm{F}-$ , $\mathrm{N}-$ , and P-rich inorganic compounds construct a thin, robust, and ionic conducting CEI layer, which efficiently preserves the integrity of NMC particles and suppresses the cathode surface reconstruction and TM dissolution.

2.5. Performance of Practical Ultrahigh Voltage Li Metal Batteries. Industrial dry Li/NMC811 pouch cells were cycled in different electrolytes (Table S3 and Figure S28). The cycling performance of Li/NMC811 pouch cells using E-PFPN are shown in Figure 7a,b. Under the voltage range of $3.0 - 4.5\mathrm{V}$ , pouch cells exhibited a capacity retention of $84.5\%$ after 100 cycles with an outstanding average CE of nearly $100\%$ and good repeatability (Figure S29). Even at $4.6\mathrm{V}$ , it still had $80.2\%$ capacity retention over 50 cycles. Furthermore, a multilayer Li/NMC811 pouch cell with a higher capacity of 1.5 Ah was assembled and tested under the same conditions, which also showed outstanding cycling performance (Figure S30). Relying on the ultrahigh voltage capacity, the Li/ NMC811 pouch cell exhibits a high specific energy of 345 Wh $\mathrm{kg}^{-1}$ determined from the total weight of the pouch cell (Figure 7c). Considering the high proportion of the packaging in the total mass of the $500\mathrm{mAh}$ pouch cell, we calculated the specific energy based on the cell weight except packaging to simulate a practical cell with higher rated capacity, which displays a spectacular specific energy up to $495\mathrm{Whkg^{-1}}$ .

To compare the solvation tuning strategy proposed in this work with the conventional $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ solvation tuning strategies (HCE, LHCE, WSE, FWSE),[12,22,23,29] we evaluated the electrolytes from six angles: energy density, cutoff voltage, ion conductivity, full-cell life, safety and rated capability. These parameters of LMBs in this work show obvious advantages over that based on other solvation tuning strategies in previous reports (Figure 7d), which correspond to the metrics listed in Table S4.

Overall, the function of the "bulky coordinating" solvation in the electrolyte of ultrahigh-voltage LMBs are illustrated schematically in Figure 7, parts e and f, and the specific advantages can be summarized as follows: (1) Outstanding compatibility with the lithium metal anode. The anion-dominated solvation in E-PFPN contribute to form anion-derived SEI rich in LiF, which effectively reduces the irreversible decomposition of electrolyte, prevents Li metal corrosion and inhibit lithium dendrites growth. (2) Increased stability of cathode at ultrahigh voltage. E-PFPN itself has improved oxidation stability and forms a robust CEI contains F-, N-, and P-rich inorganic compounds and is able to restrain persistent parasitic reactions of electrolyte, suppress the cracks and reconstruction of the cathode, and reduce the TM dissolution. (3) Favorable $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ transport and charge transfer properties. The steric effect weakens the solvents binding energies, which decrease the barrier of $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ migration. Moreover, the thin and conductive CEI generated from E-PFPN enables a fast ionic transport and low interphase impedance. (4) Nonflammability and improved battery safety. E-PFPN is completely nonflammable, which gives an improved safety in the event of accidents.

# 3. CONCLUSION

To conclude, a solvation tuning strategy based on the molecular steric effect has been developed for ultrahigh-voltage LMBs. Demonstrated by both theoretical and experimental studies, we identified that the steric effect of bulky PFPN in $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ solvation causes weaker solvation ability of solvents and a looser solvation environment, contributing to higher anion content in the $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ solvation sheath. The designed electrolyte not only exhibits advantageous $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ conductivity and improved safety, but also generates dense and untrathin SEI and CEI to promote the stability of electrodes at ultrahigh voltage. Therefore, the designed E-PFPN electrolyte promises a high Li plating/stripping CE and $>5\mathrm{V}$ oxidative stability. Furthermore, the electrolytes allowed long-term cycling of thin Li/high-loading NMC811 $(\mathrm{N} / \mathrm{P} = 2.0)$ cells at ultrahigh voltage $(4.6\mathrm{V})$ with an outstanding capacity retention of $84.1\%$ over 150 cycles and high average CE of $>99.56\%$ . Industrial Li/NMC811 pouch cells with a cutoff voltage of $4.6\mathrm{V}$ and lean electrolyte exhibited an $80\%$ capacity retention for 50 cycles, delivering a specific energy of $495\mathrm{Whkg^{-1}}$ . The "bulky coordinating" solvation tuning strategy reported in this work provides a significant direction for the electrolyte engineering of high-energy LMBs.

4.1. Materials. Lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI, $99\%$ , battery grade, DoDoChem), lithium difluoro(oxalato)-borate (LiDFOB, $99\%$ , battery grade, DoDoChem), lithium hexafluorophosphate $(\mathrm{LiPF}_6, 99\%)$ , battery grade, DoDoChem), 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME, $>99.9\%$ , DoDoChem), sodium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (NaTFSI, $99\%$ , battery grade,

DoDoChem), fluoroethylene carbonate (FEC, $>99.9\%$ , DoDoChem), ethylene carbonate (EC, $>99\%$ , DoDoChem), diethyl carbonate (DEC, $>99\%$ , DoDoChem), and ethoxy(pentafluoro)-cyclotriphosphazene (PFPN, $>99\%$ , Innochem Co., Ltd., China) were all used as received without further purification. We store all our materials in Argon-filled glovebox. Metallic Li foil was purchased by China Energy Lithium Co., Ltd. Cathode NMC811 laminates were prepared by laying a mixture of 90 wt % NMC811 material, 5 wt % super P and 5 wt % PVDF (8.0 wt % NMP) on an aluminum foil current collector. Industrial dry Li/NMC811 pouch cells were purchased from Hunan Cubic New Energy Technology Co., Ltd.

4.2. Electrolyte Preparation. The commercial electrolyte was composed of $1\mathrm{M}$ $\mathrm{LiPF_6}$ dissolved in EC:DEC (1:1 by volume). The additive optimized electrolyte (E-DFOB/FEC) was used as control electrolyte, which was composed of $1.5\mathrm{M}$ LiTFSI and $0.5\mathrm{M}$ LiDFOB dissolved in DME:FEC (7:3 by volume). The electrolyte with solvent PFPN (E-PFPN) was prepared by $1.5\mathrm{M}$ LiTFSI and $0.5\mathrm{M}$ LiDFOB dissolved in DME:FEC:PFPN (3:3:4 by volume). Two simulation electrolytes used in NMR test were $1\mathrm{M}$ LiTFSI dissolved in DME:PFPN (1:1 by volume) and $1\mathrm{M}$ NaTFSI dissolved in DME:PFPN (1:1 by volume). All electrolytes were made and stored in an argon-filled glovebox.

4.3. Characterizations. The SET was tested by igniting the electrolytes for $3\mathrm{s}$ and then evacuating the fire source and recording the combustion time. ${}^{1}\mathrm{H}-,{}^{7}\mathrm{Li}-$ and ${}^{19}\mathrm{F}$ -NMR spectra were recorded on a JEOL 400 MHz NMR spectrometer at room temperature. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM, JEOL, JSM-7401F, Japan) was conducted to characterize the surface morphology of lithium deposition and cathode NMC811. The Cu foil or cathode in a half cell after the preparation step was washed with 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) to remove residual lithium salts, dried, and then sealed in the glovebox until transferred for characterization. The whole process of sample preparation is carried out in a glovebox with oxygen and water contents below $0.1~\mathrm{ppm}$ . Transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEOL, JEM-2100Plus, Japan) were used to analyze the microstructure of cathode. The chemical composition information on SEI and CEI was obtained by using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Fisher Scientific Ltd., U.S.A) sputtering at different depths. The C 1s peak at $284.8\mathrm{eV}$ was used as the reference for all binding energies. X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements were detected by Rigaku MiniFlex600-C with a scan speed of 10.0 degree/min and a scan range from 10 to 70 degree to inspect cathode structure. Inductively coupled plasma-Optical emission spectrometer (ICP-OES, Thermo Fisher, IRIS Intrepid II, U.S.A) was used to determine the transition metal concentration in electrolyte. Raman spectrum measurements (HORIBA, Japan) were conducted on a LabRAM HR spectrometer with a $633~\mathrm{nm}$ excitation laser for the liquid electrolytes presealed in capillary tubes.

The $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ conductivity was measured by a conductivity cell with two stainless steel electrodes. The conductivity cell was immersed into electrolyte and the Li ion conductivity was measured by the following formula:50

$$
\sigma = \frac {L}{A \times R} \tag {1}
$$

Here $R$ is the resistance, and $A$ and $L$ are the area and spacing between the two stainless steel electrodes, respectively. The transference number of $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ was evaluated by the DC polarization measurements at $10\mathrm{mV}$ using the Bruce-Vincent method.[62]

4.4. Electrochemical Measurements. The electrochemical behavior of the coin cells was tested using 2032-type cells, and Celgard 2325 was used as the separator. The same amount $(65\mu \mathrm{L})$ of electrolyte was used in each coin cell. All batteries were fabricated in an argon-filled glovebox. The charge-discharge performances of the Li/Cu, Li/Li, and Li/NMC batteries were conducted on a battery test station (LANHE CT3001A). The electrochemical stability windows of different electrolytes were tested in a half cell with aluminum foil as a working electrode, Li foil as the working electrode and reference electrode using CHI 760E electrochemical workstation. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was performed from the

frequency of $0.1\mathrm{Hz}$ to $100\mathrm{kHz}$ using two-electrode coin cells with Li as both the counter electrode and reference electrode. Aurbach CE test method was applied to evaluate the Coulombic efficiency. Specifically, $5\mathrm{mAhcm}^{-2}$ of Li were deposited on Cu under a current density of $0.5\mathrm{mA cm}^{-2}$ and then charged to $1.0\mathrm{V}$ to clean the Cu surface. Then plate quantitative Li reservoir $(Q_{t} = 5\mathrm{mAhcm}^{-2})$ on Cu foil, and repeatedly strip/plate Li with a capacity of $1\mathrm{mAhcm}^{-2}$ $(Q_{c})$ for $n$ cycles $(n = 10)$ at a current density of $0.5\mathrm{mA cm}^{-2}$ , and finally, the remaining Li was stripped to $1.0\mathrm{V}$ . The average Coulombic efficiency could be calculated by eq 2

$$
\mathrm {C E} = \frac {Q _ {R} + n Q _ {c}}{Q _ {t} + n Q _ {c}} \tag {2}
$$

Here $Q_{R}$ indicates the remaining capacity in the final stripping procedure.

4.5. Computational Methods. Quantum chemistry calculations were first performed to optimize molecular geometries of $\mathrm{TFSI}^{-}$ , $\mathrm{DFOB}^{-}$ anion, PFPN, DME, and FEC solvent molecules using the Gaussian 16 package at B3LYP/6-311+G(d) level of theory. The atomic partial charges on these solvent molecules were computed by fitting to the molecular electrostatic potential at atomic centers. The atomistic force field parameters for all ions and molecules are described by the AMBER format and are taken from previous work.[63]

Two modeling systems were constructed to mimic real experiments. Atomistic simulations of this modeling system were performed using GROMACS package with cubic periodic boundary conditions. The equations for the motion of all atoms were integrated using a classic Verlet leapfrog integration algorithm with a time step of 1.0 fs. Atomistic simulations were further performed in a canonical ensemble (NVT) for 50 ns, and simulation trajectories were recorded at an interval of 100 fs for further structural and dynamical analysis. Furthermore, representative solvation structures were extracted from atomistic simulation trajectories, and these solvation structures are then used as starting configurations for subsequent density functional theory (DFT) calculations. DFT calculations were first performed using the Gaussian 16 software at the same level of theory (B3LYP/6-311+G(d)) to optimize solvation structures and, thereafter, to calculate the specific binding energies and molecular electrostatic potential contours of these representative solvation structures.
