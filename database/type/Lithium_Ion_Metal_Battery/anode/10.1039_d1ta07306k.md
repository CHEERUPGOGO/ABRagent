A highly lithiophilic 3D scaff old is realized by decupled heavy silver embedding via strong Ag+-pN interactions between silver cations and the argentophilic pyridinic nitrogen of melamine. The strong lithiophilicity increases lithium storage capacity, improving the reversibility of lithium plating and stripping. # Argentophilic pyridinic nitrogen for embedding lithiophilic silver nanoparticles in a threedimensional carbon scaffold for reversible lithium plating/stripping†

Yuju Jeon, ‡ac Jonghak Kim,‡a Haeseong Jang,b Jeongin Lee,a Min Gyu Kim, + $\textcircled{1}$ b Nian Liu*c and Hyun-Kon Song *a

Reversible lithium metal plating and stripping are required for the durable operation of lithium metal batteries. Three-dimensional architecture has been employed for accommodating volume change of lithium metal during repeated plating and stripping while lithiophilic materials have been utilized for the even plating of lithium metal. One of the best pictures would be a three-dimensional electrically conductive scaffold having a significant amount of lithiophilic sites homogenously distributed on its skeleton. To realize the ideal architecture for lithium metal anodes, herein, we embedded silver nanoparticles as lithiophile in a three-dimensional carbon scaffold. To overcome the limited loading amount of silver in the porous structure, melamine as an argentophile having argentophilic (silver-philic) pyridinic nitrogen was introduced into the carbon scaffold. Melamine as the argentophile increased the silver loading ten times in the three-dimensional scaffold via the strong interaction with silver cation. The heavy lithiophile (silver) loading increased the lithium storage capacity, guaranteeing uniform lithium distribution throughout the scaffold. The silver nanoparticles loaded in the scaffold were alloyed with lithium to be silver–lithium alloy $( \mathsf { A g } _ { x } \mathsf { L i } _ { y } )$ during lithium metal plating. The alloy served as the lithiophilic nucleation sites for the dendrite-free growth of lithium metal. As a result, the strong lithiophilicity derived from the heavy-loading silver improved the reversibility of lithium plating and stripping. The cycling durability of lithium metal batteries with lithium-ion-battery cathode reaction and oxygen reduction reaction was twice improved by employing the lithium-metal-infused 3D scaffold anode having the highest lithiophile loading with internal space enough to accommodate the volume of lithium metal.

# Introduction

Lithium metal without scaffolds must be the best lithium source in terms of energy density.1–3 A single lithium atom supplies one electron via ionization, delivering 3680 mA h from 1 gram of lithium metal $\left( 3 6 8 0 \mathrm { \ m A } \mathrm { h } \mathrm { g } _ { \mathrm { L i } } ^ { - 1 } \right)$ ). This high capacity is achieved by the mass benet from the absence of scaffold. On the other hand, scaffold-supported lithium source such as the popularly used graphite intercalation compound $\mathrm { ( L i C _ { 6 } ) }$ presents much smaller lithium storage capacity at 339 mA h $\operatorname { g } _ { \mathbf { L i C 6 } } - 1$

on the $\mathrm { L i C } _ { 6 }$ basis (equivalent to 372 mA h $\mathrm { g } _ { \mathrm { C 6 } } - 1$ on the graphite basis excluding lithium amount). Even if the scaffold-free lithium source is far superior to the scaffold-supported case in terms of lithium storage capacity, there are too many and serious obstacles to allow lithium metal foil to be employed as the lithium source for energy storage devices.4 Uneven metal plating encourages lithium metal to grow dendritically. The high surface area of the dendrites increases the amount of electrolyte decomposition while the extended growth of lithium dendrites short-circuits battery cells. More importantly, from the scaffold's viewpoint, the serious cell volume change between plating and stripping is inevitable due to the scaffoldfree nature of the lithium metal chunk. Dead mass of lithium metal isolated from an electrically conductive environment is generated during the repeated volume change. Therefore, lithium metal plating/stripping reversibility or capacity retention along repeated plating/stripping cycles is not guaranteed.

Between the two extreme cases (scaffold-free lithium metal and graphite intercalation compound), three-dimensional (3D)

electrically conductive scaffolds for lithium metal plating/ stripping have been employed, such as carbon-based5–7 and metal-based8–10 scaffolds. Their structures were designed to provide a plating surface on which lithium metal was deposited as well as a volumetric space to accommodate the volume of plated lithium metal. The porous structure of the 3D scaffold discouraged current density from being localized and therefore suppressed the extended growth of lithium metal dendrites. Therefore, the 3D-architectured electrodes possibly improved lithium capacity retention along repeated plating/stripping cycles. In addition to the reversibility gain, the 3D architecture should be designed to have lithium storage capacities at least higher than that of graphite intercalation compounds (339 mA h gLiC61 ). The graphite intercalation/de-intercalation is highly reversible so that we do not need to use the 3D scaffold for lithium metal plating/stripping if the lithium storage capacity of the 3D scaffold were smaller than that of $\mathrm { L i C } _ { 6 }$ . More desirably, the 3D architecture would be useful if its capacity were higher than the practical value of high-capacity silicon anode at 1200 mA h per gram of $\operatorname { L i } _ { 1 . 8 8 } \mathrm { S i }$ (equivalent to 1800 mA h per gram of silicon at room temperature).11–13

Despite the benets of the 3D scaffolds, it is advisable to decrease the kinetic barrier of lithium metal nucleation during plating by massaging the internal plating surface of the porous scaffolds.14 Lithium metal plating only on the external top front of the scaffolds should be avoided by facilitating the nucleation process on the internal plating surface.15 Lithiophiles were incorporated into porous scaffolds for encouraging the lithium metal nucleation: metals such as gold and silver16,17 and metal oxide such as ZnO, $\mathrm { C o } _ { 3 } \mathrm { O } _ { 4 }$ and $\mathrm { C u } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O }$ .18–20 The lithiophilic materials were lithiated via alloying and conversion reactions before lithium metal plating. The lithiated lithiophiles facilitated lithium metal nucleation, reducing the relevant overpotential.

Silver was one of the best lithiophiles, showing the smallest kinetic barrier of lithium nucleation aer it was lithiated.16 Overpotential was not required for lithium metal nucleation on silver as well as gold, zinc and magnesium, which was less than $5 \ \mathrm { m V }$ for aluminum, $1 3 \ \mathrm { \ m V }$ for silicon and $1 6 \ \mathrm { \ m V }$ for tin. Nanometric to micrometric silver metal was embedded in 3D scaffolds. Silver was deposited on substrates thermally17,21 and electrochemically,22 while silver nanowires were mixed with carbon to form a 3D scaffold.23 It was recognized that the previously reported silver loading methods were based on nonspecically weak physical interaction between silver mass (or its precursor) and substrates. The loading amount of the lithiophile would be limited when the lithiophile or its precursor was introduced to the internal space of pre-made 3D scaffolds. 6 to $7 \mathrm { a t } \%$ of silver was loaded via thermal17 and electrochemical22 deposition. The entrance of porous structures could be blocked by silver deposits when further loading is tried.

From the lesson of the previous works, we designed the next version of strategy for embedding silver in 3D scaffolds. Silver precursors (e.g., Ag+ ) were integrated with scaffold-building blocks (graphene oxide (GO) and carbon nanotube (CNT)) to construct the 3D architecture (in situ silver embedding). More importantly, argentophiles (silver-philes) also participated in

the precursor solution to provide a strong interaction between the sliver precursor and the internal surface of 3D scaffolds. That is to say, the argentophile played a role of carrying silver precursors from solution phase to solid phase. Involving the argentophilic interaction in the 3D architecture was expected to be helpful for achieving heavier loadings of silver metal with keeping its even distribution throughout the whole body of the 3D scaffold.

In this work, we employed melamine as the argentophile (silver-phile) to attract silver ion in the precursor solution for making heavily silver-loaded 3D scaffold electrode. The electronegative electron lone pair of pyridinic nitrogen of melamine (Fig. S1†) was strongly combined with the electropositive metal cation such as ${ \mathbf { A } } { \mathbf { g } } ^ { + }$ .24,25 The argentophile (melamine) embedded in the 3D carbon scaffold built from graphene oxide (GO) and carbon nanotube (CNT) successfully placed the nanodimensional lithiophile (silver nanoparticles) within the 3D structure aer calcination and decupled the loading amount of silver metal. The heavily and homogeneously loaded lithiophile increased lithium storage capacity and improved its plating/ stripping reversibility by growing lithium metal densely on the lithiophilic surface throughout the whole body of the 3D scaffold. The improved plating/stripping reversibility of the lithiophile/argentophile-containing scaffold extended the capacity retention of lithium metal battery cells even with a limited amount of lithium metal stored in the 3D scaffold.

# Results and discussion

Expected growth pictures of lithium metal deposit were compared between copper foil, low-loading-lithiophile 3D scaffold $\left( \mathbf { A } \mathbf { g } _ { 1 . 3 } @ \mathbf { C } \right)$ and heavy-loading-lithiophile 3D scaffold $\left( \mathsf { A g } _ { 1 4 } @ \mathsf { N C } \right)$ (Fig. 1). As reported in literature, 2D electrodes (e.g., copper foil) failed to discourage current to be localized so that lithium metal nucleation was unevenly distributed, and lithium dendrites were grown extensively (Fig. 1a).8 In the 3D scaffold electrodes, on the other hand, the plating current ux was distributed over their high surface area while the compartmented space in their porous structure limited the dendritic growth of lithium metal. When a lithiophile (e.g., silver) was loaded on the 3D scaffold, the lithium affinity of the lithiophile drove the internal surface of the 3D scaffold to be more efficiently utilized for lithium metal plating $\mathbf { \left( A g _ { 1 . 3 } @ C \right. }$ in Fig. 1b). The silver metal lithiophile was lithiated to form silver–lithium alloy $( \mathtt { A g } _ { x } \mathtt { L i } _ { y }$ as the secondary lithophile) before lithium metal plating. The lithiophilic alloy encouraged the lithium metal to be plated at a smaller nucleation overpotential. Aer all of the lithiophilic sites were devoted to nucleating lithium metal and therefore totally covered by the lithium metal, the internal space of the 3D scaffold was not preferred for metal plating any more in the absence of the fresh surface of silver. Lithium metal began to be deposited on the external topmost surface of the scaffold and then grew dendritically, leaving the silver-absent internal surface partially intact. Heavier loading of the lithiophile was required to utilize the whole area and volume of the internal space for lithium metal plating $\left( \mathbf { A g _ { 1 4 } }  @ \mathbf { N C } \right.$ in Fig. 1c). A 3D scaffold having silver nanoparticles heavily loaded on carbon frame was prepared by the solution–ltration–calcination process in the presence of melamine as an argentophile (Fig. 2a). When melamine was mixed with graphene oxide (GO) and carbon nanotube (CNT) as the scaffold-building blocks, the argentophile (melamine) was bound to the carboxyl group of GO (Mel@GO/CNT in the le of Fig. 2a). The presence of melamine signicantly reduced the infrared intensity of the peak for the carboxyl group of GO at $1 7 2 0 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ (Mel@GO/CNT versus GO/ CNT in Fig. 2b and ${ \bf S } 2 \dagger { \bf \Sigma }$ ).26–30 When the silver precursor was introduced into the Mel@GO/CNT-containing solution, ${ \mathbf { A } } { \mathbf { g } } ^ { + }$ was bound to the pyridinic nitrogen of melamine $\left( \mathbf { M e l - A g } ^ { + } @ \mathbf { G O } / \right.$ CNT in the center of Fig. 2a). The interaction between ${ \mathbf { A } } { \mathbf { g } } ^ { + }$ and pyridinic nitrogen $( \mathsf { A g } ^ { + } \mathrm { - } \mathsf { p N } )$ was clearly conrmed by the infrared peak shi of $\mathbf { N - C = N }$ of melamine from $1 4 3 0 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ to $1 3 5 7 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ (from Mel to $\mathrm { \mathbf { M e l - A g } ^ { + } }$ in Fig. 2b and ${ \bf S } 2 \dagger$ ).31,32 The strong ${ \mathbf { A } } { \mathbf { g } } ^ { + } .$ –pN interaction was also conrmed by identifying the molecular fragment of $\mathbf { A g N } ^ { + }$ in the time-of-ight secondary ion mass spectroscopic (TOF-SIMS) spectra. # Conclusions

In this study, we realized the effective three-dimensional carbon-framed scaffold having heavily loaded lithiophiles (silver) for lithium metal anode. The argentophilic (silver-philic) melamine contributed to the heavy embedment of silver nanoparticles throughout the whole body of the scaffold. The FT-IR and TOF-SIMS studies clearly identied the strong interaction between the electronegative pyridinic nitrogen of

melamine and the electropositive silver cation $( \mathsf { A g } ^ { + } \mathrm { - } \mathsf { p N } )$ . The loading of silver increased by 10 times in the presence of melamine. The strong lithiophilicity derived by the heavyloading silver improved the lithium-storage capacity, guaranteeing uniform lithium distribution throughout the scaffold. The narrowing between the neighboring nucleation sites was induced by the lithium-alloyed silver $\left( \mathbf { A g } _ { x } \mathbf { L i } _ { y } \right)$ , resulting in the homogeneous lithium nucleation during lithium metal plating. The SEM measurement visualized the even and compact growth of lithium metal along the laminae of the layered carbon frame. Therefore, the reversibility of lithium plating/stripping electrochemistry of the $\mathrm { A g _ { 1 4 }  @ N C }$ scaffold having the highest lithiophile loading was signicantly improved in four different cell congurations: $\mathbf { A g _ { 1 4 } }  @ \mathbf { N C } \rVert$ Li, $\mathrm { L i A g } _ { 1 4 } @ \mathrm { N C } \rVert$ Li, $\mathrm { L i A g } _ { 1 4 } @ \mathrm { N C } \| \mathrm { L F P }$ and $\mathrm { L i A g } _ { 1 4 } @ \mathrm { N C } \| \mathbf { O } _ { 2 }$ . Especially, the benet of the $\mathbf { A g _ { 1 4 } } \mathbf { \textcircled { 2 } N C } \mathbf { - }$ based lithium electrode on lithium metal battery was demonstrated by the stable capacity retention in $\mathrm { L i A g } _ { 1 4 } @ \mathrm { N C } \| \mathrm { L F P }$ cell $8 1 \%$ at ${ 8 0 0 } ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ cycle at 3C) and $\mathrm { L i A g } _ { 1 4 } @ \mathrm { N C } \| \mathbf { O } _ { 2 }$ cell (180 cycle operation at $0 . 2 \mathrm { \ m A \ c m ^ { - 2 } }$ ).

# Experimental section

# Ag-containing 3D carbon scaffolds

A carbon precursor solution was prepared by mixing the graphene oxide (GO; GO-V50, STANDARD GRAPHENE) and singlewall carbon nanotube $ { \left( 0 . 2 \ \mathrm { \ w t \% } \right. }$ SWCNT; WAB1300ST, KH CHEMICALS) at the mass ratio of 2 to 1 in $1 8 0 ~ \mathrm { m l }$ deionized water. The precursor solution for the $\mathrm { A g } _ { 1 . 3 } ( \textcircled { \mathrm { 2 } } \mathrm { C }$ scaffold was prepared by adding $7 0 6 ~ \mu \mathrm { l }$ of $0 . 5 \mathrm { ~ M ~ A g N O } _ { 3 }$ solution to $1 2 ~ \mathrm { m l }$ of the GO/CNT solution and mixing them at $8 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $^ { 2 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ . The precursor solution for the $\mathrm { A g _ { 1 4 }  @ N C }$ scaffold was prepared by adding $3 4 \mathrm { ~ m l }$ of $2 . 9 4 ~ \mathrm { m g } ~ \mathrm { m l } ^ { - 1 }$ melamine-dispersed aqueous solution to $1 2 ~ \mathrm { m l }$ of the GO/CNT solution and then $7 0 6 ~ \mu \mathrm { l }$ of 0.5 M AgNO solution into the melamine/GO/CNT solution. Each mixture was thoroughly stirred at $8 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $^ { 2 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ . The precursor solutions were ltered through a ltration membrane (VVLP04700, Merk Millipore) by vacuum at room temperature. Freestanding lms were peeled off from the membrane aer drying in ambient atmosphere for enough time. The lms, placed in a covered crucible, were calcined at $5 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $4 . 5 \ \mathrm { h }$ under argon atmosphere: temperature-ramping rate $=$ $6 . 2 5 \ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } \ \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ ; argon ow rate $= 3 0 0$ sccm. The $\mathrm { A g _ { 0 } ( a U C }$ scaffold was prepared by vacuum ltration of $1 2 \ \mathrm { m l }$ of the GO/CNT solution and drying the freestanding GO/CNT lm at ambient atmosphere for enough time.

# Lithium metal infusion

The $\mathrm { A g _ { 0 } ( a U C }$ , $\mathrm { A g } _ { 1 . 3 } ( \widehat { \mathrm { a } } \mathrm { C }$ and $\mathrm { A g _ { 1 4 }  @ N C }$ scaffolds were infused with lithium metal (to be $\mathbf { L i A g _ { 0 } ( a C }$ , $\mathrm { L i A g } _ { 1 . 3 } @ \mathrm { C }$ and $\mathrm { L i A g } _ { 1 4 } @ \mathrm { N C }$ respectively) by placing the scaffolds on lithium metal molten at $3 8 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ in a nickel container under argon atmosphere of a glove box with less than 0.1 ppm oxygen and water. The resultant amounts of lithium and $\mathbf { A g _ { 1 4 } } \mathbf { \textcircled { 2 } N C }$ were 3.8 and $5 . 5 ~ \mathrm { \ m g } ,$ respectively.

# Cathode preparation

The LFP cathode slurry was prepared by mixing LFP (Pulead), SuperP (Timcal) and polyvinylidene uoride (PVDF; Solef 5130, Solvay) at 8 to 1 to 1 in mass in N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) solvent for $3 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ . The slurry was cast on aluminum foil by a doctor blade and then vacuum-dried at $1 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for 6 h. The LFP loading density was $5 . 6 \mathrm { m g } \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 2 }$ , equivalent to $0 . 9 5 \mathrm { m A h c m } ^ { - 2 }$ NCM811 cathode slurry was prepared by mixing NCM811 (EcoPro BM Co., Ltd), SuperP (Imerys) and PVDF (KF9700, Kureha) at 94 to 3 to 3 in mass in NMP solvent for $^ { 2 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ . The slurry was cast on aluminum foil by roll-to-roll comma coating and then vacuum-dried at $1 1 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $^ { 2 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ . The NCM811 loading density was $1 2 . 4 ~ \mathrm { m g } ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 2 }$ , equivalent to 2.5 mA h $\mathrm { c m } ^ { - 2 }$ .

# Lithium metal battery cell assembly

The 2032 coin-type lithium metal battery cells were assembled in an argon-gas-lled glove box. Lithium-free electrodes (copper foil, $\mathrm { A g } _ { 1 . 3 } ( \widehat { \mathrm { a } } \mathrm { C }$ and $\mathbf { A g _ { 1 4 } } ( \mathbf { \widehat { a } N C } )$ and lithium-containing electrodes (lithium metal foil, $\mathrm { L i A g } _ { 1 . 3 } @ \mathrm { C }$ or $\mathrm { L i A g } _ { 1 4 } @ \mathrm { N C }$ ) were tested as the working electrode (radius $= 5 ~ \mathrm { m m }$ ). Either lithium metal foil,

LFP, or NCM811 cathode was used as the counter electrode (radius $= \mathrm { ~ 5 ~ } \mathrm { ~ m m }$ ). A $1 6 ~  { \mu \mathrm { m } }$ -thick polyethylene membrane (NH716, Asahi; radius $= 1 9 \ \mathrm { m m }$ ) was placed as the separator between the working and counter electrodes. Electrolyte was 1 M lithium hexauorophosphate $\left( \mathrm { L i P F } _ { 6 } \right)$ in ethylene carbonate/ diethylene carbonate/dimethoxyethane (EC/DEC/DME in $\mathbf { v } / \mathbf { v } =$ 2/2/1) in the presence of $1 0 \ \mathrm { w t \% }$ of uoroethylene carbonate (FEC) and $1 \ \mathrm { w t } \%$ of lithium nitrate $\left( \mathrm { L i N O } _ { 3 } \right)$ .

# Lithium–oxygen battery cell assembly

Swagelok-type lithium–oxygen battery cells were assembled in argon-gas-lled glove box. Lithium-containing electrodes (Li, $\mathrm { L i A g } _ { 1 . 3 } @ \mathrm { C }$ or $\mathrm { L i A g } _ { 1 4 } @ \mathrm { N C } )$ were used as the working electrode (radius $= 5 \ \mathrm { m m }$ ). They were pre-treated electrochemically to form the solid-electrolyte interphase (SEI) layer before the cell assembly. They were galvanostatically reduced at $0 . 0 5 ~ \mathrm { m A } ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 2 }$ up to $0 . 0 5 \mathrm { \ m A }$ h $\mathrm { c m } ^ { - 2 }$ in the 2032 coin-type lithium metal battery cells described above to form the stable SEI layer. Electrolyte for the SEI-forming process was 1 M lithium bis(- uorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI) in dioxolane/dimethoxyethane (DOL/DME in $\mathbf { v } / \mathbf { v } = 1 / 1$ ) in the presence of $1 \ \mathrm { w t ^ { \mathrm { 0 } } / \mathrm { 0 } }$ of $\mathrm { L i N O } _ { 3 }$ . A carbon paper (P50, AvCarb; radius $= 1 2 . 5 \ \mathrm { m m }$ ) was used as the counter electrode in the lithium–oxygen cells. A glass ber membrane (GF/d grade, Whatman; radius $= 1 2 . 5 \ \mathrm { m m }$ ) was placed as the separator between the working and counter electrodes. The glass ber membrane and carbon paper were vacuum-dried at $8 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $^ \textrm { \scriptsize 8 h }$ before the cell assembly. Electrolyte for the lithium–oxygen cells was 1 M LiFSI in diethylene glycol dimethyl ether (DEGDME) in the presence of $3 \ \mathrm { w t \% }$ of $\mathrm { L i N O } _ { 3 }$ .

# Electrochemical measurements

All battery tests were performed on a galvanostat (WBCS3000, WonATech). In the half-cell conguration (CukLi, $\mathbf { A g } _ { 1 . 3 } @ \mathbf { C } \| \mathbf { L i }$ and $\mathbf  A g _ { 1 4 } @ N C \| L i \}$ , the nucleation potential and the retention of coulombic efficiency were tested. To measure the nucleation potential, lithium metal was galvanostatically plated on the lithium-absent electrode at different current densities (0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1 and $3 \mathrm { \ m A } \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 2 }$ ) up to $8 \mathrm { m A h c m } ^ { - 2 }$ . For the retention test of coulombic efficiency, lithium metal was galvanostatically plated and stripped on the lithium-absent electrode at $2 \textrm { m A }$ $\mathrm { c m } ^ { - 2 }$ . Lithium metal was plated up to 1 mA h $\mathrm { c m } ^ { - 2 }$ . Lithium symmetric cells $\mathbf { \ ( L i \| L i , L i A g _ { 1 . 3 } @ C \| L i }$ and $\mathrm { L i A g } _ { 1 4 } @ \mathrm { N C } \| \mathrm { L i } \ )$ were galvanostatically plated and stripped at 1 or $3 \mathrm { \ m A } \mathrm { \ c m } ^ { - 2 }$ up to 1 mA h $\mathrm { c m } ^ { - 2 }$ . LFP-based full cells (Li LFP, $\mathbf { L i A g _ { 1 . 3 } @ C \| L F P }$ and $\mathrm { L i A g } _ { 1 4 } @ \mathrm { N C } \| \mathrm { L F P } \ )$ were galvanostatically charged up to 4.3 V and then galvanostatically discharged to $2 . 0 \ \mathrm { ~ V ~ } .$ . Constant-voltage step at $4 . 3 \ \mathrm { ~ V ~ }$ was not included in the charging. Lithium– oxygen full cells $\bigl | \mathrm { L i } \bigr | \bigl | \mathbf { O } _ { 2 } , \mathbf { L i A g } _ { 1 . 3 } \boldsymbol { @ } \mathbf { C } \bigr | \bigl | \mathbf { O } _ { 2 }$ and $\mathrm { L i A g } _ { 1 4 } @ \mathrm { N C } \| \mathbf { O } _ { 2 } \ )$ were galvanostatically discharged and charged at $0 . 2 \mathrm { \ m A } \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 2 }$ up to 0.2 mA h cm2 . The voltage limits of discharge and charge was $2 \textrm { V }$ and $4 . 5 \mathrm { ~ V ~ }$ , respectively. The galvanostatic electrochemical impedance spectra (GEIS) of the 2032 coin-type half cells was measured in situ during galvanostatic plating and stripping of lithium metal. Channel 1 (galvanostatic signal) and channel 2 (GEIS measurement) of a potentiostat (VSP-300, BioLogic) were

synchronized. GEIS circuits were obtained between 1 MHz and $0 . 1 \ \mathrm { H z }$ with current amplitude of $1 0 0 ~ \mu \mathrm { A }$ (applied by channel 2) during lithium plating and stripping at $1 \mathrm { \ m A } \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 2 }$ (applied by channel 1). Lithium metal was plated up to 1 mA h $\mathrm { c m } ^ { - 2 }$ .

# Material characterization

Fourier transform-infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR; Alpha, Bruker) was conducted at $4 0 0 { - } 4 0 0 0 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 1 }$ in transmittance mode. The interaction between silver and nitrogen was detected by time-of-ight secondary ion mass spectroscopy (TOF-SIMS; TOF-SIMS 5, ION TOF) measurement. An ion beam using $2 5 ~ \mathrm { k e V ~ B i _ { 1 } } ^ { + }$ in the high current mode was applied for surface and depth proling analysis on the secondary ions with positive polarity. The measured area for the surface and depth proling analysis was $5 0 0 \times 5 0 0 \mu \mathrm { m }$ and $1 0 0 \times 1 0 0 \ : \mu \mathrm { m }$ , respectively. The target area of $3 0 0 \times 3 0 0 ~ \mathrm { \textmu m }$ was sputtered by a $1 \mathrm { k e V O } _ { 2 } ^ { + }$ beam for 2500 s. The surface chemistry of nitrogen and silver was investigated by Xray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS; ThermoFisher K-alpha). The spectra of X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) and extended X-ray absorption ne structure (EXAFS) were obtained using a Pohang light source-II (PLS-II) at BL10C beam line (Wide energy EXAFS) in top-up mode operation with a ring current of $3 0 0 ~ \mathrm { \ m A }$ at 3.0 GeV. The X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of the scaffolds before/aer lithium infusion were obtained at $1 ^ { \circ } \ \operatorname* { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ (XRD; SmartLab, RIGAKU with Cu-Ka1 radiation). The surface and cross-section morphology of electrode was visualized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM; S-4800, Hitachi). The size distribution of silver nanoparticles was investigated by transmission electron microscopy (TEM; JEM-2100F, JEOL). The silver loaded on the scaffold was quantied by optical emission spectroscopy on the electromagnetic radiation emitted from the sample by inductively coupled plasma (ICP-OES; 700-ES, Varian). For accurate quantication of silver, the elemental content of carbon, nitrogen, oxygen and hydrogen in the scaffold was quantied through elemental analysis based on combustion (EA; Flash 2000, Thermo). The zeta potentials of the precursor materials were measured by zeta sizer (Nano ZS, Malvern).
